# Background

The Russia-Ukraine War began with Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine on February 24, 2022. [Vladimir Putin]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/putin.html) launched what is widely understood to be a war of choice, rooted in his personal views about Ukrainian nationhood and Russian imperial identity rather than in any compelling security imperative. The initial phase aimed at regime change in Kyiv failed decisively, and the war settled into an attritional pattern with front lines largely static and [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) advancing only through grinding, high-cost assaults.

[Volodymyr Zelensky]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/zelensky.html) and the Ukrainian leadership have sustained the war effort through a combination of Western military assistance, domestic innovation in [drone warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/drone-warfare.html), and adaptive operational doctrine. The conflict has become the largest sustained land war in [Europe]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/europe.html) since 1945.

# Current Situation

Russia's summer advance has slowed sharply, and Ukrainian strikes are now reaching deep into Russian territory, including St. Petersburg and Moscow [1][2]. In May 2026, Ukraine conducted a major [drone strike on Moscow]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/moscow-drone-strike.html), demonstrating that Putin can no longer protect the capital from war consequences — a moment [Phillips O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) compares to Japan's loss of the Marianas in WWII, when the propaganda narrative collapsed. Russia's cumulative casualties exceed 1 million [8].

The operational picture is defined by several parallel and increasingly synergistic Ukrainian campaigns. Ukraine is successfully isolating [Crimea]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/crimea-isolation-campaign.html), with fuel shortages becoming acute — the Russian-appointed governor has forbidden civilian fuel sales on the peninsula [3]. The isolation campaign has extended into the [Sea of Azov]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/sea-of-azov-campaign.html), where air and sea drones have attacked 28 Russian-controlled vessels and forced Russia to close the Kerch Strait to new traffic [25]. Long-range strikes are targeting not just refineries but Russian war production — the Titan-Barrikady facility in Volgograd has been hit by FP-5 Flamingo missiles [4]. Fuel shortages are appearing across Russia as the refinery campaign bites [4]. Meanwhile, [Lukashenka]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/lukashenka.html) has halted radio guidance equipment that was helping Russian strikes, responding to Ukrainian threats — [Belarus]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/belarus.html) is increasingly exposed, with Minsk closer to Kyiv than Kyiv is to Crimea [5].

Putin has rejected ceasefire proposals, with demands disconnected from battlefield realities [1]. In a stunning acknowledgement of Russia's struggles, Putin dramatically scaled back his annual Victory Day military parade in May 2026 over fears of Ukrainian long-range strikes [44]. [Crimea]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/crimea-isolation-campaign.html) has come under withering attack, forcing Russia to close summer camps and beaches, disrupting fuel supplies, and causing electricity outages [44]. [Seth G. Jones]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/seth-g-jones.html) and [Riley McCabe]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/riley-mccabe.html) (CSIS) document that Putin's primary objectives remain bringing [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html) back into [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html)'s sphere of influence, destroying [NATO]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/organizations/nato.html)'s credibility, preventing further eastward expansion, and weakening the [EU]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/organizations/eu.html) [14][44].

# Analysis

## The Deep-Strike Campaign: Ranged War and Crimea Isolation

[Phillips O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) frames Ukraine's deep-strike operations within a "ranged war" doctrine, distinguishing long-range strikes — strategic bombing of oil refineries, weapons production, and industrial base — from medium-range strikes targeting logistics disruption, supply lines, and rail hubs [8][9]. Both sides remain in the early stages of this ranged war, comparable to mid-1943 in WWII, with significant capacity to increase production. O'Brien notes that contrasting long-range strikes on both sides reveal what each side wants and is capable of attacking — a diagnostic tool for understanding war aims and capabilities [6].

The Crimea isolation campaign exemplifies this doctrine in practice. O'Brien describes a deliberate three-step process: cutting off the peninsula from the sea (achieved), gaining air superiority over the approaches (achieved), and targeting the Kerch Bridge (in progress) [3]. Ukrainian forces have disabled the remaining Russian air defense systems protecting the bridge, bringing the structure within strike range. If Russia cannot counter this process, O'Brien argues, Crimea could be fully isolated with dire strategic results — the loss of Crimea would constitute a political and military blow from which the Putin regime might not recover [3].

The isolation campaign has extended into the [Sea of Azov]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/sea-of-azov-campaign.html), a strategically significant but under-covered theater. The sea was supposed to be a Russian lake — Russia has controlled every inch of its coastline since 2022, with access only through the narrow Strait of Kerch. With road and rail to Crimea degraded and the Kerch Bridge under pressure, sea transport became the primary resupply alternative. Ukraine used air and sea drones to attack 28 Russian-controlled vessels (21 oil tankers, four tugboats, two cargo vessels, one dredging vessel) by July 11. On July 10, Russia forbade all new traffic through the Kerch Strait. If attacks continue and Russia cannot develop countermeasures, the Sea of Azov will remain functionally closed — strangling Russian supplies to Crimea and damaging grain and fuel shipments [25]. The capstone of this accelerating reach is the Omsk strike: Ukraine struck [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html)'s largest oil refinery — over 2,500 km from Ukraine — hitting the crucial ELOU-AVT-11 primary crude oil processing unit (8.4 million tonnes/year capacity), with Ukrainian claims indicating drones flew up to 3,000 km. The refinery, Russia's top petrol producer, halted operations. The strike demonstrates that if Ukraine can reach Omsk, no Russian strategic asset within 2,000 miles of Ukraine is safe [25].

Beyond Crimea, Ukraine's long-range strikes have evolved from targeting energy infrastructure to hitting the military-industrial base that sustains Russia's war effort. The FP-5 Flamingo missile striking the Titan-Barrikady weapons complex in Volgograd (former Stalingrad) represents a significant maturation — Ukraine is no longer limiting its deep strikes to refineries but is now striking missile component manufacturing, advanced components, and war production facilities [4][8][9]. [Zelensky]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/zelensky.html) announced on June 25 a dedicated 40-day intermediate- and long-range strike campaign intended to pressure Russia into ending the war, signaling Kyiv's intent to sustain or intensify the tempo of strikes [15].

[ISW]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/geopolitical-futures.html) documents how the synergy between Ukraine's long-range strikes on Russian oil refineries (reducing production capacity) and its new mid-range strikes against Russian ground lines of communication (disrupting transportation of remaining fuel) produced acute gasoline shortages in occupied Crimea and other occupied Ukrainian territories [10]. The mid-range campaign was the catalyst for shortages in occupied Ukraine specifically, while Russia itself weathered the long-range campaign through export bans and prioritization. ISW further details how Ukraine's intermediate-range strikes have systematically targeted oil depots, storage facilities, and transshipment nodes across occupied Crimea, compounding the refinery campaign's effects [15]. The occupied Luhansk, Donetsk, Zaporizhia, and Kherson oblasts have likewise experienced acute gasoline shortages, forcing occupation authorities to impose sales restrictions [15]. Ukraine has also struck Russian oil transshipment infrastructure near the Baltic Sea in Leningrad Oblast repeatedly in April 2026, curtailing Russia's ability to export oil through its western ports [15]. On the ground, Ukrainian forces have heavily targeted Russian GLOCs throughout occupied southern Ukraine, including strikes against fuel tankers along the M-14 highway linking occupied Berdyansk and Mariupol to Rostov-on-Don [15]. Emerging diesel shortages may pose a more militarily consequential threat than the gasoline crisis [10].

The campaign has accelerated dramatically since March 2026. ISW documents that Ukrainian forces struck 22 Russian oil refineries between January and June 30, 2026, hitting targets as far as 2,000 km from the border. Approximately 78 of 83 federal subjects have experienced fuel shortages, with 48 imposing official purchasing limits. Putin confirmed a complete ban on gasoline and jet fuel exports and is considering a diesel export ban [15]. O'Brien reports that Ukraine claimed to have shut down more than 42% of Russia's oil output by early July, with other analysts estimating closer to one third. Key July strikes include the Slavyansk-na-Kubani refinery, the Ufa refinery complex (~1,300 km from the front), Lukoil-Nizhegorodorgsintez (Russia's fourth-largest refinery and second-largest gasoline producer), and the St. Petersburg oil terminal and Vysotsk port on the Baltic [16]. The compounding spiral is clear: more Ukrainian attacks at greater range → decreased Russian oil revenue → reduced fiscal capacity → strained war economy [16].

Russia's vast territory is both asset and vulnerability. Ekaterina Zolotova (GPF) explains that Ukraine's drone strikes are exploiting Russia's immense landmass — a drone threat was declared for the first time ever in Novosibirsk and Omsk. Russia possesses high-quality air defense systems but cannot field enough of them across its vast territory; just to cover the line of contact with Ukraine, Russia would need as many as 500 Pantsir units. Undefended and uninhabited parts of Russia could even serve as launch sites for Ukrainian strikes, with abandoned Soviet airstrips in remote villages as potential launch platforms. Russia is finally developing a unified information environment to increase situational awareness — almost four and a half years into the war — but the centralized bureaucracy that Russia built to exercise control over its sprawling territory continues to hamper its response. Not until late May 2026 did the Defense Ministry authorize private companies to acquire anti-aircraft systems for their own defense [20]. O'Brien cautions that the adaptation cycle for long-range strike and air interception has been considerably longer than for small, short-range drones, with no sign of new Russian interception systems or the ability to greatly increase anti-air production. Even densely protected areas like Moscow are not invulnerable [16].

The Kremlin's failure to address its air defense vulnerabilities has deep roots. ISW notes that Russia experienced a similar but less severe fuel shortage crisis at the end of summer 2025, when Ukrainian forces had struck 16 of Russia's 38 refineries by October 2025 — yet the Kremlin failed to address the underlying air defense gaps, setting the stage for the more acute shortages now unfolding [15]. The same pattern of unaddressed vulnerabilities appears in the Kremlin's failure to prepare for Ukraine's 2024 Kursk incursion: Russian authorities were reportedly aware of a possible Ukrainian attack into Kursk Oblast as early as late 2023 but took no action. Border units were left understrength, with heavy reliance on conscripts, and fortifications were sparsely manned and poorly equipped — the Kremlin did not regard its border regions as frontline areas, confident that Ukraine could not mount an attack there [15]. ISW concludes that Kremlin mitigation efforts will fail unless Russia systematically strengthens its air defense infrastructure — the one step that would address the root cause rather than the symptoms [15]. The strike campaigns may begin to impact Russian military capabilities on the battlefield in the near future, though the severity remains uncertain, particularly if Russian authorities prioritize military fuel allocation [15].

Frontline effects are becoming visible. Russian authorities may be prioritizing gasoline supplies to the military over the civilian population to cushion the war effort from fuel shortages [15]. Russian forces in the Lyman direction are reportedly facing fuel supply issues, while ammunition and drone deliveries have decreased in the Hulyaipole direction [15]. Ukraine's growing capacity to deny Russia use of Crimea and southern Ukraine for fuel and supply transport will increasingly prevent vehicle movement, cargo transport, and even generator operation — compounding the logistics strain on forward-deployed forces [15]. More broadly, Ukraine's demonstrated ability to identify and exploit Russian vulnerabilities — combined with the Kremlin's persistent pattern of failing to address them — gives Kyiv durable advantages likely to persist for some time [15].

ISW assesses that Ukraine is breaking out of positional warfare through a combination of maturing operational planning (corps structure, Delta battlefield management system), an intensified intermediate-range strike campaign using AI-enabled Hornet drones to degrade Russian GLOCs at 100-160km depth, SEAD/DEAD operations, and tactical drone supremacy. Russian advance rates have plummeted to approximately 3 sq km/day with casualties exceeding recruitment since December 2025. The first mechanized counterattacks since 2023 herald a new phase of the war — Ukraine's partners have a rare, time-constrained opportunity to exploit this momentum before Russia adapts [11].

In late July, Ukraine opened a new strike vector targeting Russian Wildberries warehouses — a major e-commerce logistics network. O'Brien documents that the campaign began on July 18 and escalated to at least 10 strikes within a week, spanning Elektrostal and Koledino (Moscow Oblast), Kotovsk (Tambov Oblast), Krasnodar and Nevinnomyssk (Stavropol Krai), Voronezh, Shushary and Novosaratovka (St Petersburg region), Simferopol (occupied Crimea), and a warehouse in the Urals [40]. O'Brien offers a mixed assessment: the campaign may distract from the refinery strike campaign — only 5 refinery strikes were conducted since it began, and with oil prices rising he believes refinery strikes remain the top priority. The dual-use argument (warehouses may hold components with military application) is tempered by his estimate that the percentage of dual-use goods is small. The strategic benefits he identifies are dispersing Russian air defense and, most likely, undermining [Putin]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/putin.html)'s political standing by attacking the basic "deal" of his regime — "I will provide you goodies if you give me power" — which looks dicey when all the goods are blowing up. However, he cautions that such campaigns often do not work as expected [40].

## The Russian War Economy Under Strain

The [Kiel Institute]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/kiel-institute.html) report "Endgame: The State of the Russian Economy" concludes that Russia's macro stance is "not sustainable." High interest rates stifle the economy while loose fiscal policy props up defense. The Q1 2026 budget deficit exceeded the full-year target in three months. The liquid portion of the sovereign wealth fund has shrunk from 6.5% to 1.8% of GDP. With fiscal buffers spent, Russia's war capacity is directly coupled to hydrocarbon export income — creating a structural vulnerability that Ukraine's strike campaign is actively exploiting [16][17]. [Paul Krugman]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/paul-krugman.html) identifies the refinery campaign as having shut down Russian refined product exports and created a global shortage of refining capacity — roughly 10% of world refining capacity is now out of operation. Russia faces major domestic fuel shortages and gas lines, and may soon be forced to import refined products [23]. The global energy cascade from concurrent refinery disruptions is analyzed in [Energy and Resources]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/energy-and-resources.html).

[RUSI]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/rusi.html) (Jim McLean) frames the strikes as translating battlefield operations into sustained economic and political costs — turning the war from a distant event most Russians could watch from afar into a daily economic burden. After weeks of mounting evidence, Putin publicly acknowledged on 28 June 2026 that Ukrainian attacks on energy infrastructure were creating "problems" and a "certain shortage" of fuel, while insisting the situation was "not critical" and had no impact on military operations, even as shortages spread [29]. The structural trade-off is stark: every step taken to shield consumers from shortages comes at the expense of export revenues and budget stability. Russia exported 0.72 mb/d of gasoil in May 2026 — roughly $2.9 billion in export revenues, or 14% of total oil export earnings that month; a diesel export ban would forgo those revenues to increase domestic supply. The fuel dampener subsidy to refiners rose from approximately $220 million in Q1 2026 to over $2.5 billion in May alone. The Kremlin is negotiating to import approximately 50,000 tonnes of gasoline from [Kazakhstan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/kazakhstan.html). The choice facing Putin narrows: re-enter negotiations with fewer ultimatums (and implicitly accept that Russia cannot conquer Ukraine's east and south), or continue absorbing costs that increasingly reach ordinary Russians [29]. See [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html), [Putin]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/putin.html), and [Energy and Resources]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/energy-and-resources.html).

The sanctions regime designed to constrain this war economy is largely fraudulent. O'Brien documents that [India]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/india.html) is "gorging" on Russian oil — June 2026 purchases hit record highs despite US sanctions supposedly reactivated on June 17. The Indian government has stated it will continue buying Russian oil regardless of US sanctions status. [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) has never paid much attention to Trump's sanctions. The US is "so weak" that major states calculate Trump will never actually enforce sanctions against Putin's customers [16].

## Russian Force Generation and the Paradox of Mass

[CSIS]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/csis.html) (Seth G. Jones and Riley McCabe) provides the most comprehensive data-driven assessment of Russian battlefield performance. Between February 2022 and June 2026, Russian forces suffered approximately 1.4 million battlefield casualties (killed, wounded, and missing) and between 400,000 and 450,000 fatalities. These rates are historically unprecedented: Russian fatalities in Ukraine are more than four times greater than all US fatalities in all wars combined since World War II, and more than nine times greater than all Soviet and Russian fatalities in all wars combined since World War II. In 2026, Russia's monthly casualty rates of 30,000-34,000 likely exceeded its recruitment rate of approximately 27,000 new recruits per month. The Russia-Ukraine casualty ratio has risen to nearly 8:1 in the first half of 2026, up from 2:1 to 3:1 for much of the war, largely due to Ukraine's use of AI-enabled drones [14]. Russian rates of advance have been among the slowest in any war over the last century: approximately 50 meters per day around Kostiantynivka, 70 meters per day around Pokrovsk, and 90 meters per day around Sloviansk. Russia's territorial control in Ukraine *shrank* in spring 2026, with a net loss of roughly 400 square kilometers in April and May — the first monthly net losses since August 2024. Russia currently controls approximately 118,000 square kilometers (about 20% of Ukraine) [14]. CSIS identifies Ukraine's AI-enabled Hornet drone as a transformative system: a one-way autonomous attack drone costing roughly $6,000 with a range of up to 150 km, using AI to analyze live video feeds, identify targets, detect decoys, and strike during the terminal phase without requiring a satellite connection. Over 90% of Russian casualties are from drone attacks rather than human-to-human engagements [14].

O'Brien argues that battles reveal rather than determine outcomes. The Battle of Pokrovsk revealed that the Russian military is hidebound and rigid, while Ukrainians are adjusting better to the tactical environment. Russians are advancing only by slaughtering their own soldiers in frontal assaults, while Ukrainians are fighting with fewer losses through the use of novel tactics including drone-delivered munitions and distributed infantry operations [7].

Greg Whisler and Michael Kofman (War on the Rocks) provide the most detailed account of why Russia's wartime force expansion failed to deliver battlefield success despite sustaining a manpower advantage through most of the war. Russia began the war with one of the smallest armies it had fielded in over 100 years — a force optimized for small-scale contingencies, short of infantry, with no reserve system. Following the initial disaster, Moscow executed the most significant Russian military expansion in decades, growing the deployed force to over 700,000 personnel through partial mobilization (300,000 reservists in September 2022) and massive contract soldier recruitment (~1.3 million contracted over the war's duration). By 2023, the General Staff had created two new combined-arms armies, two new motorized rifle divisions, and 27 new maneuver brigades and regiments — roughly quadrupling the invasion force to over 800 maneuver battalions [22]. And yet the paradox of mass: as Russia fielded more forces, the value of that mass steadily diminished. Two factors explain this. First, cycles of technological innovation and tactical adaptation — particularly Ukrainian investment in [drones]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/drone-warfare.html) — led to greater dispersal on the battlefield, negating traditional advantages from manpower superiority. Second, the Russian military chose a cost-ineffective approach to offensives, assuming quantitative advantage would eventually produce breakthroughs that never came. Russian mass ran into an effective counter: mass precision mixed with traditional capabilities (mines, prepared defenses, artillery) and drone-based firepower that minimized the advantages of manpower overmatch [22]. By 2025, force growth had largely halted — monthly recruitment (~35,000) was offset by similar numbers of killed and seriously wounded. Russian forces abandoned mechanized equipment in favor of infiltration tactics and light motorized attacks with 1-2 man teams, trading equipment losses for higher infantry attrition. The battlefield became a drone-saturated grey zone without cohesive lines, where Russian combat power trickled onto the battlefield in amounts unable to concentrate or exploit breaches. By late 2025, some formations ran short of assault-capable infantry, with Russian advances stalling and Ukraine regaining ground around Kupyansk and Zaporizhzhia [22]. The strategic implication: the steady pipeline of replaceable manpower made senior Russian commanders complacent, reinforcing a cost-ineffective approach. Many Russian combined-arms armies, doctrinally responsible for areas measured in hundreds of square miles, cover 20-30 mile sections of front. Brigades of several thousand troops are responsible for advancing along a single ridgeline or seizing one or two villages. Yet combat power is so disaggregated by drone, mine, and artillery threats that only a tiny fraction of a brigade's strength is usable at any given time. The cracks in Russia's force generation model — rising regional bonuses, fiscal unsustainability — suggest Moscow has passed its high-water mark for offensive capacity [22].

A notable finding: Moscow sustained this force expansion without relying on the historical twin pillars of Russian force generation — conscription and mass mobilization. The roughly 1.3 million contract soldiers recruited dwarf the 300,000 mobilized reservists. This is the first large-scale, extended war Russia has fought using primarily contracted volunteers, challenging prior assumptions that Moscow cannot sustain prolonged conventional war without mass mobilization [22]. The persistence of this expanded force after the war, and its implications for NATO planning, are examined in [NATO]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/organizations/nato.html).

[George Friedman]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/geopolitical-futures.html) (GPF) argues that Russia's military has performed so poorly in [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html) that it cannot mount another attack on a European nation. Dividing already inadequate forces would be suicidal. Poland has developed a substantial, well-armed, and well-trained military — an attack in the Baltics or on Poland would almost certainly trigger a Polish response, and the US has some 10,000 troops in Poland. Putin has behaved predictably for over four years, failing to defeat Ukraine and trying to convince his nation and the world that he is still dangerous. The reason Putin has not expanded the war is that the Russian army cannot [19].

## Russian Strikes and the Ukrainian Air-Defense Crisis

O'Brien documents a dramatic escalation in Russia's ballistic missile campaign against Ukraine, exploiting Patriot interceptor depletion. See [Ukraine Air Defense Crisis 2026]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/ukraine-air-defense-crisis-2026.html) [25]. Russian monthly ballistic missile launches have roughly tripled year-over-year: 28 in a comparable month a year ago versus 70+ now, with January 2026 setting a record of 91 ballistic missiles. Ukraine intercepted 89% of all aerial threats in June but only 40% of ballistic missiles. On July 6, Ukraine admitted it had no or almost no Patriot interceptors left. That day, 29 ballistic missiles fired at Kyiv all struck their targets, killing at least 19-27 people. A third ballistic missile strike on Kyiv followed on July 8, bringing the death toll in Kyiv to at least 60 since July 1 [25]. Russia is escalating its strike rate precisely as Ukrainian interceptor stocks deplete, exploiting the window before Western systems can arrive [25].

[Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) ended all US military aid to Ukraine in 2025. His vague Patriot "license" pledge at the [NATO Ankara summit]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/nato-ankara-summit-2026.html) will take years to materialize, if ever — gifting Putin 1-3 winters of unrestricted ballistic missile bombardment. Japan, after years of licensing, produces only 30 PAC-3 interceptors annually [25]. Trump's broader posture toward the war is examined in [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html).

[Velina Tchakarova]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/velina-tchakarova.html) develops the Patriot and sanctions picture around the funeral of Sen. Lindsey Graham, where Trump met individually with both [Zelensky]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/zelensky.html) and [Netanyahu]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html) in Washington. Zelensky had a clear goal: get Trump to fulfil the license pledge made at the Ankara summit and begin the process of domestic Ukrainian Patriot production. Subsequent Fox News reporting quotes Zelensky: "The President said, I accept that I will be granting the licenses." Ukraine needs twenty-five Patriot systems to defend its cities from Russian drones and ballistic missiles. Zelensky also met with Lockheed Martin executives on "joint production." Before the Trump meeting, Zelensky lobbied dozens of senators before the Senate vote to approve new sanctions on Russia — the Senate passed the measure overwhelmingly [42]. The Patriot bottleneck also operates in reverse on Kyiv: limited production runway means Ukraine is structurally dependent on Washington's industrial base for as long as the war runs.

The shared-Patriot stockpile across Iran and Ukraine theaters is a structural feature of the conflict, not a logistic accident. Both presidents — Zelensky and Netanyahu — were negotiating over the same scarce resource, with the US using its limited Patriots to defend Middle East bases against Iranian missiles while Ukraine needs them to intercept Russian ballistic missiles. Each Patriot interceptor costs $3–5M and there is little room to expand production. The Patriot is one of the few weapons capable of intercepting ballistic missiles, and the same finite stockpile must serve US bases in the Middle East and Ukrainian cities under bombardment. The Patriot picture is the smallest-scale analogue of the broader two-wars-one-continuum logic: the Ukraine and Iran wars share a US industrial base constrained by manufacturing capacity, with the proxy pay the price while Washington controls both the escalatory ladder and the licensing decisions on what gets built where [42].

## Trump's Tactical Pivot Pattern and the Loomer Normalization Warning

[Phillips O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) catalogs ten pro-Ukraine/anti-Russia statements [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) made between January and October 2025, and documents that all ten produced zero policy change. The chronology: 22 January sanctions threat; 7 March sanctions threat; 8 July "bullshit from Putin"; 13 July Patriot/Tomahawk talk and "He talks nice, and then he bombs everybody"; 14 July "50-day tariff deadline"; 28 July–6 August deadline cut to 10–12 days; 13 September NATO sanctions condition, contingent on a coalition [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) knew Orban would block; 23 September "paper tiger" rhetoric after the Zelensky meeting; 12–15 October Tomahawk threat [41]. Every pivot provided Trump with breathing space; none translated into policy. After 18 months of Trump 2.0: all US aid to Ukraine has been stopped, [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html)'s Patriots are depleted, Russia is selling whatever oil it can ship, and Ukraine has no effective defense from Russian ballistic missiles. Trump adjusts when he believes adjustment serves his interest — his recent rhetorical shifts reflect recognition that [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html) has cards and Russia is struggling on the battlefield, not a humanitarian change of heart [41]. See [Political Normalization]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/political-normalization.html).

The procedural variant of the tactic operates through the Graham-Blumenthal sanctions bill. [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) publicly suggested adding Iran to the legislation — a maneuver O'Brien reads as deliberately dilatory: "muddy the waters" and slow the process. Even if the bill passes both chambers, [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) is under no obligation to actually bring in the sanctions; he can do what he wants. The bill's passage without implementation would be a further delaying tactic. [Laura Loomer]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/laura-loomer.html) publicly presented Trump's suggestion as a sign of greater pro-Ukraine commitment — a framing O'Brien calls nonsensical, since Trump does not need legislation to sanction Iran or Russia [41]. See [Political Normalization]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/political-normalization.html).

O'Brien warns in equal measure against normalizing figures from the broader coalition through tactical single-issue endorsements. [Laura Loomer]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/laura-loomer.html)'s late-July 2026 [Ukraine visit]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/laura-loomer-ukraine-visit-2026.html) was framed in some quarters as a "Damascene conversion" to the Ukrainian cause. In O'Brien's reading, the gesture was tactical: Loomer continued to broadcast racism, election-denial, anti-Muslim hatred, and conspiracism throughout the trip, and the interview with [Zelensky]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/zelensky.html) was used to press him to declare Trump "the best president" and to amplify the Russian cocaine-with-Macron propaganda claim. Zelensky, O'Brien judges, "will live to regret" the interview. Praise of one acceptable position spreads the rest of her platform; the broader transatlantic coalition ([Loomer]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/laura-loomer.html), Tommy Robinson, Trump, Orban, Fico, Farage, Carlson) shares the single project of "destroying civil society in the USA and Europe and replacing it with some authoritarian, anti-liberal, discriminatory state" [41]. Europe's gains in the war have come not from flattering Trump but from disregarding the United States and helping Ukraine fight — flattery is a strategy that has demonstrably failed. See [Political Normalization]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/political-normalization.html), [Laura Loomer]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/laura-loomer.html), and [Ideology and Democracy]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/ideology-and-democracy.html).

## Ukrainian Military Adaptation and Industrial Transformation

Dr. Jack Watling (RUSI) describes Ukraine's transformation from an underfunded Soviet-legacy force to a 960,000-person defense force integrating UAVs as a universal fires platform, driven by civil society and a burgeoning private defense industry that rapidly iterates equipment based on frontline feedback. The vulnerability of globalized defense supply chains (Chinese magnets, Russian titanium, Indian cotton) highlights the need for Western governments to define "sufficient" rather than perfect equipment standards. The critical lesson: engaging civil society honestly creates political freedom to solve defense problems [13]. Urbanski ([RUSI]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/rusi.html)) demonstrates with Ukraine-specific evidence that the cost-exchange ratio can be inverted. In Ukraine, Russia — with a 2026 defence budget four times Ukraine's — has failed to achieve its objectives for more than four years. A superpower's overwhelming advantage in troops and platforms failed to deliver decisive results. The weaker party imposed disproportionate costs on the stronger side's munitions stocks, its economy and its political will. Ukraine's naval-drone successes — uncrewed surface vessels costing a few hundred thousand dollars each, less than a single modern torpedo, knocking out around a third of Russia's Black Sea Fleet without a conventional navy — demonstrate the principle in practice [26]. The cross-conflict cost-exchange framework and its implications for NATO procurement are examined in [Drone Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/drone-warfare.html).

Ryvkin (GPF) reports a structural shift in Ukraine's war posture: from aid dependency to defense-industrial reciprocity. Ukraine's defense production capacity grew from roughly 1 billion euros in 2022 to approximately 55 billion euros by 2026, with 82% of army suppliers homegrown by 2025 and Ukraine becoming the world's largest producer of military drones. The Gulf defense partnerships — 10-year agreements with [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html), [Qatar]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/qatar.html), and the [UAE]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/uae.html), plus deployments to [Kuwait]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/kuwait.html) — reposition Ukraine strategically, demonstrating that it exports security solutions rather than only receiving them. This repositioning is made urgent by growing war fatigue in Europe, where aid packages are increasingly difficult to sell politically. The combination of deep strikes against Russian oil infrastructure and the Gulf partnerships is shifting the international perception of Ukraine from victim to strategic partner — a country that supplies technologies and operational experience in exchange for financing and weapons [30]. See [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html) and [Defense Industrial Resilience]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/defense-industrial-resilience.html).

## Wartime Governance and Command Politics

[War on the Rocks]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/war-on-the-rocks.html)' Ukraine Compass documents the July 16, 2026 dismissal of Ukrainian Defense Minister Mykhailo Fedorov — the fifth Defense Minister since the full-scale invasion — as a governance crisis that intersects the war's technology doctrine, mobilization trust deficit, and the post-Ankara aid pipeline. Fedorov was credited with making [drone warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/drone-warfare.html) Ukraine's most lethal capability through rapid innovation. In his farewell address he cited a conflict with Commander-in-Chief Syrskyi, who he said blocked reforms and issued an ultimatum: lay low and stop the massive reforms, or leave. The dismissal came right after the [NATO Ankara summit]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/nato-ankara-summit-2026.html) pledged €70 billion in 2026 Ukraine aid, prompting speculation about a "corporate war" over controlling those funds [28].

[RUSI]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/rusi.html) (Jim McLean) provides the most detailed account of the institutional conflict behind the dismissal. Fedorov arrived at the Defence Ministry with a reform mandate: after auditing Ukraine's 120 brigades, he concluded that roughly a sixth suffered from poor leadership and proposed consolidating them by reallocating personnel to better-led, chronically undermanned units. He sought to strengthen the Army Corps by collocating each corps' constituent brigades — which were distributed in non-contiguous geography along the front, complicating command and rotation. He also wanted to identify commanders best leveraging technology to reduce casualties and empower them with personnel and equipment [36]. General Syrskyi systematically obstructed these reforms. The Ministry directed brigade consolidation; Syrskyi established three additional brigades. The Ministry directed corps consolidation; Syrskyi deployed Ukraine's best units as split formations across opposite ends of the front. When Fedorov's team tried to resource technically innovative units, they found personnel prioritised to 425 Assault Regiment, which persisted in human-centric, high-casualty attacks while stripping equipment from the innovative formations Fedorov had wanted to enable. From Syrskyi's perspective, the Ministry was overstepping its procurement role to directly command units in the field, bypassing the General Staff. For Fedorov, procurement could not be separated from battlefield conduct in a war where innovation was fastest at the edge. Zelensky removed Fedorov but did not side with Syrskyi on substance — directing that the reforms continue under the new minister. The rift remains unresolved, and the civilian-control question it raises — whether a reform mandate from Parliament can override military command autonomy — will persist beyond Fedorov's tenure [36].

The analytical divide inside Ukrainian commentary tracks three positions. The "decision skeptics" (Trehumb, Ukrainska Pravda) warn that replacing a reform-minded, publicly trusted minister with an untrusted appointee risks paralyzing competitive procurement, DOT-Chain Defence, Drone Deal partnerships, FREYJA missile defense, and 2027 funding plans — and that the mobilization crisis is fundamentally a trust problem this dismissal will deepen. The "decision defenders" (Prokopenko, Azov) endorse incoming minister Klymenko, citing his attentiveness to frontline issues, work on the corps-system transition, and mobilization experience. The "systemic blame" position (Bereza, Balyuk, Portnikov) frames the ouster as a symptom of an arbitrary system in which appointment and dismissal run on loyalty rather than parliamentary process — with Bereza predicting the familiar pattern of criminal cases against associates, and Balyuk arguing Fedorov's removal stemmed from his refusal to participate in corruption schemes he had shut down by auditing defense contracts and shifting procurement to open tenders [28].

O'Brien frames the subsequent command change as an illustration of democracy's wartime advantage. Democracies often screw up badly, start slowly, and prepare inefficiently — but they can also be better at adapting and channelling societal commitment. Pressure from Ukrainian civil society forced the political leadership's hand: protests across Ukraine grew in intensity after the [Fedorov dismissal]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/fedorov-dismissal-2026.html), directed not just at restoring Fedorov but at replacing Syrskyi as chief of the General Staff. When Zelensky was confronted with the binary choice between Syrskyi and Fedorov and chose the General, Syrskyi actually found his position weakened — with Fedorov out, Syrskyi became the focus of popular anger. Syrskyi's attempt to pretend he was "surprised to learn that we have a conflict" only deepened the backlash. On Tuesday the following week, Zelensky removed Syrskyi and appointed Mykhailo Drapatyi — promoted from Joint Forces Commander to Commander in Chief — a move widely hailed, with analyst Bielieskov calling it the "best choice of [Zelensky]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/zelensky.html)." A Rating Group poll captured the public verdict: 55% approved the removal, only 15% opposed it [33][40]. See [Fedorov Dismissal (July 2026)]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/fedorov-dismissal-2026.html), [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html), and [Zelensky]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/zelensky.html).

## Regime Resilience vs. Defeat Scenarios

[The Cipher Brief]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/the-cipher-brief.html) argues that the West must prepare for a Russian defeat — not as a prediction but as a scenario whose probability has moved from fringe to serious. Russia's 1.4 million casualties, recruitment shortfall, net territorial loss, and economic fragility (5.9 trillion ruble deficit in four months, sovereign wealth fund at 1.8% of GDP, oil refining capacity down 38%) have made defeat a possibility no longer ignorable [18]. The preparation agenda echoes the 1991 Nunn-Lugar program: (1) secure Russia's ~1,800 strategic warheads through pre-negotiated channels, ideally including [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) and [India]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/india.html); (2) decide in advance which borders and authorities to recognize if the Federation fragments; (3) keep Russian weapons scientists employed to prevent proliferation; (4) define what "victory" means for Ukraine and Europe — a Russia that loses this war and cannot start the next [18].

Yet the same source cautions that three years after Prigozhin's Wagner mutiny, predictions of Putin's imminent collapse have not aged well. Three enduring lessons emerge. First, the mutiny exposed real vulnerabilities — corruption, patronage, institutional rivalries — but these are long-term structural weaknesses, not near-term collapse indicators. Second, weakness and resilience are not mutually exclusive: the war has strengthened the FSB's chokehold, the economy has weathered sanctions, and propaganda keeps the population compliant. Third, Putin's system rests on multiple overlapping centers of coercive power (FSB, Rosgvardia, FSO) deliberately structured to prevent any single institution from becoming too powerful. Prigozhin was not marching to overthrow Putin — he targeted Shoigu and Gerasimov. Elite infighting should not be mistaken for regime collapse. Sustained Western resolve and continued support for Ukraine are required [21]. The synthesis: defeat is a serious scenario that governments must plan for, but the Russian security state has demonstrated durability that should not be underestimated. Structural vulnerabilities and short-term political collapse are not the same thing.

## The War's Termination Logic

O'Brien provides a framework for understanding how the Russia-Ukraine War may end — one that deliberately starts not from military operations but from the actual endpoint and works backward. The relationship between battles and war endings is extremely unstable: military operations are just one consideration, and often a secondary one. The analytical community's habit of drawing straight lines from battlefield outcomes to war termination has produced systematically flawed predictions — from writing off Ukraine in 2021–2022 to assuming overwhelming US force would end the Iran war on American terms [37]. The end step of any war-ending scenario requires both political leaderships to determine that ending the fighting is better than continuing. At no point in the Russia-Ukraine War have both leaderships reached this determination simultaneously. Two pathways can produce it: **changing the leader** (death, overthrow, democratic removal — Attila the Hun's death ended the Hun expansion; Empress Elizabeth's death saved Prussia in the Seven Years' War) or **making the leader believe it is worse to keep fighting** than to make peace. The latter can be driven by military collapse (Ludendorff's admission in 1918), social/political turning (Vietnam, Afghanistan), economic cost (the American Revolution for Britain), or personal survival (Hirohito in August 1945) [37]. O'Brien applies the framework directly: neither [Putin]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/putin.html) nor Zelensky has concluded that ending the war is preferable to continuing in the hope of improving their position. Internal dissension in either state could produce a leadership change that shifts the trajectory, but short of that, the fundamental problem remains. The framework also reinforces O'Brien's critique of escalation-ladder models: different leaderships can blow through an escalation ladder in one step or never go up it at all — there is no way to understand what a state will do by achieving "escalation dominance" [37]. See [Escalation Theory Critique]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/escalation-theory-critique.html).

[Aurelien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/aurelien.html) argues the West fundamentally misread Russia's invasion from the outset, interpreting it as a war of conquest when it was first intended as a political shock and then morphed into an attrition war for which the Russians had prepared. The West "couldn't really be bothered to understand" — assuming the war would soon be over. Russia's strategy targets Ukraine's ability to deploy forces by attacking fuel depots, transport links, and infrastructure: if forces cannot reach the front, they are irrelevant regardless of their nominal strength [32]. The West clings to the fantasy of a peace conference whose terms it can dictate, after which things revert to normal and Russia begs to be readmitted to the "international system." In reality, Aurelien argues, it will be the Russians who decide when the conflict stops, and the form the post-war situation takes is uncertain. The post-war period will likely feature the intimidatory use of military capability, serial political crises within [NATO]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/organizations/nato.html) and between NATO and Russia, and other frictions. Western options for avoiding this are few and diminish with every day of bellicose posturing. The sheer length of the fighting has revealed important industrial weaknesses in the West — the [defense industrial base]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/defense-industrial-resilience.html) cannot sustain attrition warfare. The most likely outcome is a long period of low-level tension suiting Russia and straining Western cohesion [32].

## The Grain Trade and Global Food Chokehold

[Velina Tchakarova]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/velina-tchakarova.html)'s Day 144 diagnostic placed the Russia-Ukraine War domain (S2) at CRITICAL. The deep-sea grain corridor appeared effectively lost, generating up to $900 million in monthly foreign exchange losses for [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html). The governance transition following the [Fedorov dismissal]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/fedorov-dismissal-2026.html) and the Drapatyi command change remained incomplete, adding institutional uncertainty to the military and economic pressures. Critically, [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html)'s Hormuz windfall — the surge in oil export revenues generated by the Iran war's disruption of Gulf supply — is financing the war in Ukraine, creating a direct cross-domain linkage between S1 and S2 [34]. Her Day 150 diagnostic revised the domain **downward** from the RUPTURE-adjacent framing to **CRITICAL-BOUNDED**. The prior characterisation of the deep-sea grain corridor as "effectively lost" overstated the position. Shipowners have suspended vessel calls at Ukraine's Black Sea ports and the country has lost roughly a third of its export capacity, but no formal closure exists. Ukraine's own agriculture ministry states the situation is materially different from the 2022 blockade: Danube ports, rail and road corridors could sustain volumes up to roughly 3.5 million tonnes monthly even without deep-sea access [39]. The correction narrows the S2→S4 food-system encirclement thesis: the output-side pressure is real but bounded, not absolute. The S2→S4 cross-domain pairing has fused into a single food-system encirclement operating on input and output simultaneously — the Black Sea grain export collapse (S2, output) and the Hormuz fertilizer transit closure (S4, input) compress the global food system from both ends. This fusion means the Russia-Ukraine War is no longer merely a regional conflict with global food consequences but a structural component of a unified food-system encirclement that operates across two geographically distinct but functionally linked chokepoints [34]. The correction also reframes the war as a two-directional attrition contest rather than a one-sided Ukrainian degradation story. Ukraine's offensive campaign against Russian oil and export infrastructure — including strikes on the Tuapse terminal and reported attacks on twelve Russian vessels in the Black Sea — demonstrates that Ukraine is imposing costs on Russian export capacity even as its own grain corridor is under pressure. The S2 domain thus remains elevated but bounded: the war is not generating a unilateral supply-side collapse but a mutual cost-imposition dynamic in which both sides' export infrastructure is contestable [39]. See [Global System Rupture]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-rupture.html).

Ekaterina Zolotova ([GPF]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/geopolitical-futures.html)) documents the naval turn the war has taken in the [Sea of Azov]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/sea-of-azov-campaign.html) and Black Sea. Ukrainian forces have struck seagoing vessels, forcing Russia to suspend shipping through the Don-Azov Canal and the Kerch Strait. Moscow has retaliated by destroying Ukrainian infrastructure near Odesa and other Black Sea ports. The fighting has limited both countries' ability to generate export revenue — and the consequences extend globally because these waterways are crucial arteries for grain delivery [38]. The Azov-Black Sea basin accounts for 30 percent of Russia's port cargo turnover, hosting the Novorossiysk, Taman, Rostov, and Tuapse grain terminals. While sanctions have reduced traditional exports like oil and natural gas, grain exports have risen: in the first half of 2026, Russian grain shipments increased to Turkey (+86%), Egypt (+45%), China (+63%), and Iran (+75%). Ukraine's intensification of attacks against energy infrastructure and tankers hurts not only Russia's petroleum sales but its general export capacity [38]. Russian farmers are caught in a compounding squeeze. A fallen drone set winter wheat ablaze in the Krasnosulinsky district. Farmers already lose about 1,000 rubles ($13) per ton of grain due to rising diesel prices; Sea of Azov restrictions effectively double this amount. Continued financial pressure could force reductions in grain plantings, undermining Russia's position as a major exporter. Russia may be unable to deliver as much as 10 million tons of wheat to the global market. Offset options are structurally thin: Far East and Caspian Sea grain terminals are too small to handle redirected Black Sea shipments, and Kazakhstan has imposed a strict six-month ban on Russian wheat imports to protect its domestic market [38]. For Ukraine, the situation is equally dire. More than 90 percent of grain, vegetable oil, and agricultural product shipments pass through just three ports in the Odesa region. Russian missile and drone strikes have taken about a third of Ukrainian grain export capacity offline and reduced storage facilities, limiting export capacity even if a coastal sea route is restored. Ukraine could reroute some exports by river through Danube ports for shipment from Constanta, [Romania]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/europe.html), though European farmers would likely oppose the move [38]. The global context compounds the pressure. The US Department of Agriculture revised its 2026–27 U.S. wheat production forecast to 42.45 million metric tons — the lowest in 54 years — driven by severe drought in the wheat belt. Unusually hot weather in Western Europe and prolonged rains in Canada's Alberta and Saskatchewan provinces raise additional concerns. The [Iran war]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) has raised prices for fuel, fertilizer, and maritime transport, compounding grain production costs. A prolonged suspension of navigation through the peak export period from August to December would significantly worsen matters. See [Food and Agriculture]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/food-and-agriculture.html) [38].

## Regional Dynamics: Belarus, Japan, and European Defence

Belarus has become an exposed flank. O'Brien reports that [Lukashenka]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/lukashenka.html) bent to Ukrainian threats and turned off radio guidance equipment that had been facilitating Russian strikes [5]. The economic consequences are visible: gasoline flows from [Belarus]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/belarus.html) to Russia increased 13-fold and diesel 3-fold, reflecting Russia's growing domestic fuel deficit. Ukraine is warning Belarus against further complicity, and the geographic reality — Minsk is closer to Kyiv than Kyiv is to Crimea — means that vital Belarusian targets are exposed to Ukrainian strike systems [5].

[RUSI]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/rusi.html) (James Brown) documents [Japan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/japan.html)'s dual-track posture toward the Russia-Ukraine War: continued institutional support for Ukraine alongside a growing reengagement trend with Russia. On the support side, Japan joined the NATO Prioritised Ukraine Requirements List (PURL) in May 2026, dispatching four Self-Defence Force personnel to NSATU headquarters in Germany, and sent state ministers to Kyiv in June. On the reengagement side, LDP politicians have attended Russian embassy events, a METI-led business delegation visited Moscow in May, and high-level diplomatic contacts have resumed — driven by advocates of former PM Abe's wedge strategy between Russia and China. PM Takaichi has been largely inactive, neither reversing the support mechanisms nor personally committing to the Ukraine cause. The structural factors limiting rapprochement — Russia's deepening ties with China and North Korea, joint patrols near Japan, and Moscow's overplayed diplomatic demands — suggest Japan's overall policy will not diverge far from the European mainstream despite the ambivalence [35].

On the European defence dimension, Rachel Ellehuus (RUSI Director-General) argues the [Ankara summit]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/nato-ankara-summit-2026.html) confirmed Ukraine's centrality to credible European defence. The declaration's statement that "Ukraine contributes to transatlantic security" formally recognises Ukraine as a security contributor, not just a partner. Promising cooperation initiatives include Project Freya (anti-ballistic missile system with Ukrainian rockets, German Hensoldt radars, Norwegian C2), drone coproduction contracts with European and Gulf nations, and integration of Ukraine's FP5 Flamingo missile with European industry. Through creative coproduction and co-development arrangements, Ukrainian industry can help Europe produce and innovate at scale — and at lower cost [27]. Tchakarova assesses that Russia will face a strong recession scenario due to the war in [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) — the cascading effects of the [Global System Rupture]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-rupture.html) are compounding Russia's existing economic strain from the Ukraine war. Russia will boost its summer offensive, while Ukraine will seek to quickly build antiballistic missile defence systems ahead of the winter. Two needed (real) ceasefires — in Ukraine and Iran — are the mutually strengthening signal toward preventing the Global System Rupture scenario or the fourth systemic risk-driven global crisis [24].

## China's Lessons for National Total War

Wang & Beauchamp-Mustafaga identify the Russia-Ukraine War as [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html)'s best example of national total war in practice. Chinese military analysts derived a key lesson: the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) significantly enhanced Ukrainian military lethality using non-military tools — economic sanctions against Russia, Starlink civilian satellite communication services enabling Ukrainian military command and control, intelligence declassification, cyber capabilities, and coordinated strategic messaging. Chinese analysts interpret the early public assessment by U.S. officials that the war would end quickly as a deliberate strategic deception campaign — a "cognitive smoke bomb" — designed to induce Russian underestimation of Ukrainian forces and contribute to operational defeat. For Beijing, Ukraine demonstrates how national total war integrates civilian and military capabilities across domains, with non-military instruments producing decisive military effects [31]. However, China recognizes it cannot simply replicate the U.S. approach. Beijing lacks broad alliance networks capable of imposing coordinated sanctions, does not control major financial chokepoints like the petrodollar, and has no resilient megaconstellations comparable to Starlink. Beijing also publicly assesses that it lags the United States in strategic messaging and disinformation capabilities. These gaps motivate continued investment in military-civil fusion and civilian capacity-building, with the 15th Five-Year Plan prioritizing the strategic capabilities necessary for executing national total war [31]. General Mykhailo Drapatyi, the new Commander-in-Chief, immediately introduced policies to reduce casualties. Drapatyi's philosophy aligns with Fedorov's: machines and robots must do the fighting whenever possible, not human beings. Fedorov stated in a NYT interview: "I am absolutely convinced that robots and drones should be doing the fighting wherever possible… armies equipped with cheap, smart and mass-produced weapons will survive in a new era of warfare" [43]. The alignment between Drapatyi's military command and Fedorov's technology-first approach represents a potential war-winning combination — but Zelensky's determination to keep Fedorov out limits its realization. ## Ukraine's Air Interdiction Campaign: The CSIS Strike Dataset

[Seth G. Jones]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/seth-g-jones.html) and [Riley McCabe]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/riley-mccabe.html) (CSIS) have compiled and analyzed over 20,000 strike incidents involving Ukrainian strikes against Russian targets, providing the most comprehensive data-driven assessment of Ukraine's air interdiction campaign — classic air interdiction conducted with drones and missiles rather than airplanes [14][44]. The data categorizes strikes by range. Long-range strikes (greater than 300 km from Ukrainian territory) have hit St. Petersburg, Moscow, and Ukrainka Air Base — the latter over 6,000 km from Kyiv. Medium-range strikes (30–300 km) have targeted Kursk, Kaluga, and Belgorod oblasts. Short-range strikes (less than 30 km) have hit thousands of targets just across the border in Russia and in Russian-controlled areas of Ukraine, including [Crimea]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/crimea-isolation-campaign.html). The strikes have caused operational challenges for Russia, including decreased energy refining capacity, gasoline shortages in some areas, the effective removal of Russia's navy from the war, disrupted logistics and supply lines, and attrition of some industrial production. Nevertheless, CSIS assesses that the strikes have not yet crippled Russia's war machine or fully denied Russia's military capacity to wage war [14][44]. See [Drone Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/drone-warfare.html) and [AI Military Race]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/ai-military-race.html).

## Drone-Dominated Warfare: The Kill Zone and AI Competition

CSIS provides the most granular account of how drones have transformed the tactical environment along the more than 1,000-kilometer front. The front line is saturated with drones creating a "kill zone" of approximately 20–40 kilometers in which movement is extremely dangerous. Over 90 percent of Russian casualties are from drone attacks rather than human-to-human engagements. Russian forces typically send as few as a half-dozen soldiers into the kill zone at a time, while Ukrainian forces generally do not send dismounted infantry into the zone. Russian infiltration tactics — moving forward in small groups to slip between Ukrainian positions rather than assault them head-on — avoid the catastrophic losses of massed assault but are lethal in their own way, as many soldiers are detected and struck while crossing the kill zone [14][44].

The AI competition is intensifying on both sides. Beyond the Ukrainian Hornet — a one-way autonomous attack drone costing roughly $6,000 with a range of up to 150 km that uses AI for target detection, decoy discrimination, and terminal strike without requiring a satellite connection [14] — Ukraine has also fielded AI-assisted systems from SkyFall for counter-drone operations, including identifying and destroying Russian first-person-view (FPV) drones [44]. On the Russian side, the Rubikon Center for Advanced Unmanned Systems, a military unit specializing in drone warfare, has been effective at striking Ukrainian drone operators, destroying rear units, and severing supply lines [14][44]. See [AI Military Race]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/ai-military-race.html) and [Drone Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/drone-warfare.html).

## The Shadow Fleet Under Pressure: Economic Lifeline and Interdiction

The shadow fleet — the clandestine network of hundreds of oil tankers acquired or chartered by Russia to evade Western sanctions — remains the economic lifeline of the Russian war economy. Approximately three shadow-fleet tankers per day transit from the Russian Baltic ports of Primorsk and Ust-Luga to [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) and [India]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/india.html) via the Baltic Sea, carrying roughly half of Russia's total oil exports. The three Baltic and Black Sea ports of Ust-Luga, Primorsk, and Novorossiysk together handle approximately 60 percent of Russian seaborne oil exports [46].

European coastal-state actions against the shadow fleet have intensified since early 2026 — including French special forces boarding the tanker "Deyna" by helicopter off the French North Sea coast in March, Swedish coast guard stops of the "Jin Hui" in May, and British elite units storming the "Smyrtos" in the English Channel in June — yet these actions hit only roughly 2 percent of shadow-fleet tankers, with about 10 stops out of approximately 1,000 transits per year [46]. The [Shadow Fleet Boarding Wave]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/shadow-fleet-dawn-raids-2026.html) reflects growing European resolve but remains constrained by international law, which guarantees the right of peaceful passage: vessels can only be stopped for forged papers, manipulated AIS signals, or false flags — not for fleet membership. The German "Eventin" case from January 2025 remains unresolved, with the Federal Fiscal Court invalidating the customs seizure in March 2026 [46].

Ukraine's own drone campaign against the shadow fleet has been far more impactful. Ukrainian sea and underwater drones have damaged or disabled 50–60 oil tankers — 4–5 percent of Putin's approximately 1,300-tanker fleet. Among tankers serving the Black Sea routes, the disablement rate is far higher, estimated at 25–35 percent. KSE Institute estimates that March 2026 drone attacks on Ust-Luga and Primorsk alone cost Russia approximately $1.76 billion in lost oil revenue. Weekly departures from Ust-Luga dropped from 16–17 to just two [46]. [Julian Pawlak]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/julian-pawlak.html) (SWP) calls for a coordinated common European approach to shadow-fleet handling, noting that the current state-by-state approach is undocumented and underutilized — even existing legal frameworks such as false flag enforcement are not being fully exploited by all countries, including Germany [46]. See [Sanctions and Financial Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/sanctions-and-financial-warfare.html).

## Back-Channel Diplomacy: Russia's Road Back Runs Through Berlin

[Andrew Ryvkin]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/andrew-ryvkin.html) (GPF) argues that the media's focus on Russia's negotiations with the United States misses the more consequential European dimension. The [EU]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/organizations/eu.html) shares a border with Russia, EU sanctions have hurt Russia's economy the most, and Europe is Ukraine's key ally. Even after a ceasefire, these realities will remain. Russia's eastward reorientation toward China is driven by necessity rather than strategy: Russia-China trade totaled $228 billion in 2025, compared to Russia-EU trade of $282 billion in 2021 before the invasion. The EU controls the decisive levers for Russia's postwar return — frozen assets, global banking access, and European market access [45].

Germany has maintained back-channel diplomacy with Russia since at least early 2024, holding a series of meetings in Baku and Abu Dhabi formally devoted to reviving the Petersburg Dialogue, the German-Russian forum founded by Gerhard Schroeder and Putin in 2001 and terminated by Berlin in 2021. The German delegation is led by officials from former Chancellor Angela Merkel's cabinet. The Russian side includes Viktor Zubkov (former prime minister and chairman of Gazprom's supervisory board, a longtime Putin confidant from their St. Petersburg days), Valery Fadeyev (head of Russia's Human Rights Council and Putin adviser), and Alexey Gromyko (head of the Institute of Europe at the Russian Academy of Sciences, which advises the Kremlin on EU policy) — figures who carry significantly more political weight inside Russia than Kirill Dmitriev, Putin's envoy to Trump [45]. Russian billionaire [Roman Abramovich]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/roman-abramovich.html) has been identified as another channel, valued for keeping lines of communication open and helping with narrowly defined humanitarian issues rather than for any expectation that he could change Putin's course on territory or the war itself [45].

Putin has signaled unwillingness to compromise on territory and remains committed to continuing the war at least to the point of capturing the Donbas, which while strategically insignificant would allow him to declare victory at home and potentially create a political crisis in Ukraine [45]. There is no clear anti-war faction within the Russian elite: an openly anti-war position would guarantee expulsion from it. The security services (FSB/SVR) remain firmly pro-war and anti-Western, willing to engage only on issues such as prisoner exchanges [45]. If a settlement is eventually reached, these back channels are likely to expand: business leaders, bankers, and former officials would become key figures in discussions over sanctions, frozen Russian assets, investment, reconstruction, and the restoration of commercial ties [45].

## Civil Society, Protests, and the Erosion of Wartime Restraint

[Susan Stewart]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/susan-stewart.html) (SWP) analyzes the [Ukraine Civil Society Protests]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/ukraine-civil-society-protests-2026-07.html) that erupted following the government reshuffle, arguing that both the personnel decisions and the protest willingness point to dangers that could undermine the influence of Ukraine's battlefield successes [47]. For approximately two weeks, Ukrainians protested the dismissal of Defense Minister Fedorov — protests that continued even after [Zelensky]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/zelensky.html) also dismissed [Oleksandr Syrsky]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/oleksandr-syrsky.html) and replaced him with Brig. Gen. [Mykhailo Drapatyi]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/mykhailo-drapatyi.html), demanding Fedorov's return to the Defence Ministry. The conflict between Fedorov and Syrsky was an "open secret," though its full extent only became public in recent weeks [47].

Stewart identifies deepening patterns in Ukrainian politics and society that could bring problems. Zelensky has developed a habit of frequent personnel changes at the highest levels, with reasons only partially transparent — creating an impression that the political system is increasingly shaped by narrow political or personal considerations. A pattern appears to be emerging: individuals who achieve high popularity are removed from their positions. Such changes also initiate phases of reorganization that complicate internal processes and cooperation with other agencies — particularly problematic in wartime [47].

In the first war years, the population showed great restraint, rallying behind the country's leadership to form a common front. Since July 2025, however, this restraint has eroded: street protests, combined with voices from external actors, forced the withdrawal of a law that would have largely disempowered two key anti-corruption institutions. In the Fedorov case, many citizens saw a red line crossed. Having achieved a partial success with Syrsky's dismissal, some feel encouraged to continue protesting — including for political goals beyond Fedorov's return [47].

An active and influential civil society is one of Ukraine's strengths and has contributed to its wartime successes. After nearly four and a half years of war, a growing return to political debate is understandable but also brings higher instability, as societal and political differences become more visible. Russian actors will inevitably exploit these tensions. The timing is particularly bad: Ukraine is currently experiencing a positive phase both on the battlefield and in its relationship with the United States. Although protests are banned under martial law, they have been tolerated. Citizens understand the risks — including Russian disinformation — but the need for political participation is strong [47]. See [Ideology and Democracy]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/ideology-and-democracy.html).

## The Stalemate Hypothesis and the Pressure Campaign

CSIS argues that the war in Ukraine heavily favors the defender — a dynamic that has frustrated Russian offensives but also constrained Ukrainian counterattacks. Russia's progress on the battlefield has been historically poor, with casualties, fatalities, and average rates of advance among the worst of any major power in any war since World War II. Ukraine's best hope may be a stalemate that leads to a peace agreement or ceasefire. Since World War II, interstate wars have ended in one of several ways: 30 percent following a ceasefire, 21 percent through a battlefield victory by one side, 16 percent through a peace agreement, and the rest for a variety of other reasons [14][44].

Despite high losses, Russia continues to fight — a decision resting firmly with Putin, who has given no public indication he intends to slow down. So long as Putin remains willing to pay the price, Russia can continue to tap into a deep manpower pool and a war economy that, while strained, has not yet buckled. The bitter irony, CSIS notes, is that despite Russia's battlefield challenges and economic vulnerabilities, the United States and Europe have failed to fully wield economic or military pressure. The moment is ripe for a pressure campaign pushing the Russian economy toward exhaustion: the EU and US should impose stricter enforcement of sanctions targeting the shadow fleet, including secondary sanctions on banks in [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) and Hong Kong that facilitate shadow-fleet transactions [14][44]. See [Sanctions and Financial Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/sanctions-and-financial-warfare.html) and [Defense Industrial Resilience]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/defense-industrial-resilience.html).
