# Background

The US-Iran War of 2026 pitted the world's dominant conventional military against a geographically entrenched adversary whose theory of victory was narrower — survival rather than conquest — and therefore more resilient [28][67]. The war's central asymmetry was existential: for the IRGC and many Iranians, the conflict concerned regime survival and national sovereignty; for the United States, save for the nuclear program, it did not [67]. This asymmetry meant Iran could absorb far higher costs and maintain political will long after domestic support for the war had eroded in the US — a pattern consistent with Vietnam, Iraq, and Afghanistan [67].

Iran controls three sides of the Strait of Hormuz — a narrow, shallow, crowded waterway through which roughly 20 million barrels per day of petroleum liquids flow, representing approximately 20% of global supply [28][38][66][82]. Iran never needed to achieve sea control; it needed only to make transit uninsurable and unprofitable [38]. The Strait's geography offers no accessible beachhead: Iran's coastline rises into rugged mountains with concealed firing positions, and the country spans roughly 1.65 million square kilometers with a population of about 92 million and roughly one million personnel under arms [52][61]. A Shahed-136 drone is approximately the size of an F-150 truck; Iran has an estimated 2.5–3 million comparable vehicles, making visual identification of launch platforms effectively impossible [52][61]. Destroying 80–90% of Iranian anti-shipping assets is insufficient — all must be suppressed continuously [52].

Iran's doctrine — mosaic defense, formalized in 2005 by the IRGC's Center for Strategy under Mohammad Ali Jafari and the ideologue Hassan Abbasi — is a decentralized command structure designed to survive decapitation strikes [28]. Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi stated: "We've had two decades to study defeats of the U.S. military to our immediate east and west. We've incorporated lessons accordingly" [28]. Iran had studied the force structure it faced and designed its counter-campaign accordingly — targeting not strike aircraft but the tankers, radar networks, communications nodes, and command platforms that sustained them [28]. The cost exchange told its own story: Iran's drones and missiles cost thousands of dollars apiece; the interceptors fired to stop them ran into millions [28].

Over five years before the war, Iran built an economy to evade sanctions — its tanker fleet grew from roughly 70 vessels in 2020 to nearly 550 by 2025, sustained by Chinese refineries that alone accounted for close to 90 percent of Iran's foreign oil sales [28]. This shadow infrastructure allowed Iran to ship roughly 1.5 million barrels of crude per day even during the Hormuz blockade [8].

The United States entered the war relying on expensive, technologically advanced systems in small numbers — Patriots and THAAD air defense costing millions per shot to engage cheap Iranian drones — a force structure optimized for a paradigm of warfare that drone technology had rendered obsolete [2][38]. CSIS estimated the US expended roughly half its Patriot inventory, more than 850 Tomahawk cruise missiles, and over 1,000 air-launched stand-off weapons; rebuilding those stockpiles will take four to five years [38]. The Patriot interceptor is the sole missile that can intercept ballistic missiles, and finite production capacity binds the Iran war and the Russia-Ukraine War into a single resource problem: "There are two theaters, but there is only one bottleneck" [74][89].

The "escort" paradigm for Hormuz epitomizes Western doctrinal obsolescence: the concept derives from World War-era anti-submarine warfare, but today's threat is drone and missile swarms that cannot be defended against at scale — escorts provide more targets, not protection [54]. The purpose of attack is not necessarily to sink ships but to discourage sailing — a political and economic effect, not a kinetic one [54].

The energy disruption cascaded through chemicals and fertilizer into food prices: roughly one-third of global seaborne fertilizer trade passes through Hormuz, and standard trade models underestimated the impact because they missed the bottleneck mechanism [27][91]. The refining bottleneck compounded the crude shortage: around 10% of world refining capacity was offline, primarily from Ukraine's drone campaign against Russian refineries, meaning a Hormuz reopening would add crude but not usable fuel [30][42][87]. The crack spread — the margin between buying crude and selling gasoline and diesel — reached the highest levels ever recorded [87][93].

Israel's long-term goal for Iran is complete regime change toward a secular, business-friendly government; in the short term, Israel views a chaotic Iran — potentially fracturing into ethnic states — as beneficial despite the disaster it would cause for the US [26]. The nuclear issue was a pretext used by Israel to draw the US into a war it could not win alone [26]. This produced a structural US-Israel strategic divergence: Washington seeks to use military gains to create a diplomatic framework constraining Iran, whereas Israel remains skeptical that an ideologically driven regime can be durably moderated through negotiation [23].

Iran and its axis partners — Russia and China — maintained a structured coordination architecture predating the war, including trilateral deputy foreign minister meetings in Beijing in March 2025 and expert-level sessions in Moscow in April 2025 [6]. The axis consultation on the ceasefire was "almost certainly a green light, not an obstacle" [6].

Iran has pursued a military nuclear program since 1987 [84]. The US-Saudi civilian nuclear agreement, concluded during the war, permits Saudi domestic uranium enrichment with less stringent inspections than the UAE's 2009 agreement — eroding non-proliferation precedent and making a Middle East with three, four, or five nuclear powers markedly more probable [84].

## Pre-War Context

US leaders had threatened Iran for a decade, the methods of an air campaign were predictable, and Israel's participation was expected — the outbreak itself was not surprising [21]. Iran was not surprised either: it had expected the attack and developed capabilities to pursue its objective of survival [21]. The war was a personal decision by Donald Trump, made without political pressure and not in the national interest — a finding that directly challenges the assumptions of realism [2]. The United States started the war without informing its European allies, [Japan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/japan.html), or [South Korea]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/south-korea.html), after more than a year of insults, threats, and tariff wars [2]. Aurelien classifies the war as a classic case of "surprise-at-failure": the surprise came because the overall strategic situation was read wrongly, with backwards logic from desired outcome to necessary false assumptions about Iranian state fragility, targetability, and popular uprising [21].

# Current Situation

As of August 2026, the conflict has entered what Norman Roule terms an "operational pause" — not a ceasefire but a "period of coercive restraint" in which Iran continues attacks on shipping while the US restrains its response [83]. The Versailles Memorandum (MoU) of June 17 collapsed around July 13; Trump declared it formally over on July 8 [31][36][50]. Thirteen consecutive nights of US strikes (July 11–23) achieved no strategic gains while further burning through critical munitions [76]. After the US halted strikes on July 23, Iran continued attacking — directly or through proxies — demonstrating that no ceasefire existed [76].

Iran's parliament is reviewing legislation to bar vessels linked to the US, Israel, and other "hostile nations" from the Strait of Hormuz and impose transit fees of 5–7% of cargo value [86]. Iran's Supreme National Security Council has issued six demands before Hormuz reopens: approximately $300 billion for damages, over $100 billion in frozen assets, and ending Israel's occupation of Palestine — terms Pape reads as victor's justice, not bargaining [87]. The period of maximum leverage runs through January 2027: Iran's objective is to "drive a stake through the heart of the Trump presidency" by wrecking the US economy [87].

Tanker traffic through Hormuz remains at roughly an 80% drop in volume, with only one inbound large crude carrier arriving in the past week [78]. No LNG tanker has crossed since July 16; Qatari LNG vessels accumulated in the Gulf, unable to reach export markets [53]. War-risk premiums for vessels transiting the strait rose to 3–10% of hull value — as much as 40 times the prewar rate [53]. VLCC daily earnings hit approximately $470,000, a record [53]. The Houthis separately announced a blockade of Saudi Arabia's Red Sea ports [53]. The State Department has warned American citizens throughout the Middle East to prepare for "unexpected escalation" and consider departing [78].

The war's kinetic footprint has expanded across three maritime fronts: the Persian Gulf, the Red Sea (Bab el-Mandeb), and the Mediterranean (Damietta Port drone strike, July 29) [73][79]. NATO territory has been directly touched: Iran threatened RAF Fairford over its use for B-1 bomber missions, and NATO air defenses engaged Iranian drones near Incirlik Air Base in Turkey [65]. The Caspian basin has become a convergence zone where the US-Iran war and the Russia-Ukraine war overlap — Ukraine struck an Iranian cargo vessel in the Caspian on July 26 [80].

China is preparing to send Iran finished advanced munitions — QW-12 and FN-16 MANPADS, up to 400 shoulder-fired air defense missile launchers — a categorical shift from dual-use components to finished weapons that could arrive and deploy by September [75]. The Triumvirate of Happiness — clandestine great-power coordination between Washington, Beijing, and Moscow designed to keep the war from escalating into Global System Rupture — is a crisis ceiling only as strong as each member's willingness to stay put [74].

Pape diagnoses Stage Four of the Escalation Trap: strategic paralysis. If the US remains in paralysis while Iran consolidates, Iran becomes the rising regional hegemon in the Persian Gulf [87]. The Saudi-Turkey-Pakistan "Mecca pact" defense agreement fills a power vacuum left by US decline [87]. Pape's exit criterion is testable: tactical success must be converted into political success — a stable settlement requiring Iran to accept constraints because it believes the alternative is worse — before January 2029 [88]. The war is likely to continue "at least through January" 2027 [33].

US domestic support has collapsed. As of July 20, the death toll reached 17 killed in action with 427 wounded [55]. The House passed a concurrent resolution to end the war 214–208 on July 23 [63]. The Pentagon's internal cost estimate is approximately $100 billion, against the official $37.5 billion [63]. Just over a third of MAGA voters think the war has been worth the economic cost, down from about half in May [63]. A White House source reported the president is "now fully aware that the only way to the victory that he wants is completely politically impossible" [63].

The rumored 7% cargo-value fee under negotiation mirrors the approximately 7% that marine insurers currently charge for Persian Gulf voyages — institutionalizing the wartime-risk premium rather than eliminating it [85]. Iranian officials dismiss US assertions of bilateral negotiations as "repeated lies" and "desperate cries," maintaining that Tehran engages only via regional mediators [85]. The Iran-Oman mechanism under discussion is, in Roule's assessment, likely overstated; if tolls are charged, a cascade of unresolved questions follows about refusenik shippers, sanctions interaction, and whether American ships are permitted to pay Iran [83].

# Analysis

## Strategic Diagnosis: Why the US Lost and How Iran Won

The war's central question is not who won the battles but who was fighting the right war. Multiple analysts converge on a shared diagnosis: the United States fought the wrong war — bombing the wrong targets, measuring success against the wrong metrics, and pursuing political objectives disconnected from military reality. The assessment runs along two complementary axes: first the diagnosis of American failure — institutional, doctrinal, and political — and then the explanation of Iranian success, where a twenty-year campaign plan absorbed the opening strikes and converted survival into leverage. The trajectory moves from what the US got wrong to how Iran had prepared to win.

The diagnosis begins before the first strike. Three weeks out, on February 5, [Kamran Bokhari]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/kamran-bokhari.html) of [Geopolitical Futures]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/geopolitical-futures.html) judged that "there is no way the regime can avoid a hard landing" [1]. His account of why matters for what followed: a decade of institutional decay and hyperfactionalism inside the conservative establishment had opened space for "a loose coalition of pragmatists to gain a tenuous foothold," and the IRGC's four-decade-old regional strategy had "effectively collapsed" after the October 2023 regional war and the twelve-day Israel-Iran exchange the previous June [1]. Collapse of the strategy was not collapse of the institution — Bokhari is explicit that the IRGC "remains a formidable power center," through its hold on the energy sector, internal security, telecommunications and the defense industry [1]. In that environment the pragmatists had become the regime's most important brokers, working to prevent further systemic destabilization [1].

This is the condition Washington was aiming at, and Bokhari names the theory directly: the objective of military options "would be to induce a domestic political realignment in [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html), using targeted strikes to weaken hardliners and create space for pragmatists to accept concessions," with strikes on IRGC command centers and offensive assets able to "potentially achieve the intended effect" [1]. Both formulations are hedged in the source — a rationale under consideration, not a prediction of success. The war began on February 28, before the internal dynamics Bokhari was tracking had time to resolve, and the diagnoses that follow describe what happened when a theory of induced realignment met an adversary whose preparation is the subject of the second half of this section.

O'Brien argues that the United States bombed girls' schools, failed to protect its own facilities, and achieved no meaningful strategic objectives [2]. He dismisses the sinking of the Iranian Navy in scathing terms: "the sinking of the Iranian Navy is a derisory achievement and if anything might help the Iranian Navy going forward" [2]. The US military lost this war almost from the moment it started — a historic failure (see [Phillips P. O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html)) [2].

The material cost bore out the diagnosis. O'Brien catalogued US aircraft losses at 17 advanced aircraft (fighters, AWACS, special forces helicopters), billions in equipment damage, bases hit extensively, and munitions burned through decades of production in six weeks [3]. A CSIS report confirmed munitions depletion, documenting what was used and how long it takes to rebuild each system [3]. Behind the materiel losses lay institutional decay: O'Brien wrote that "there is evidence that the profound corruption and intellectual and organizational poverty of the Trump administration has percolated down to the military. It would certainly be surprising if it had not" [3].

An earlier Atlantic article by O'Brien had identified the first signs of US military "rot": Iranian drones penetrating American defenses (six soldiers killed in Kuwait), F-15E friendly-fire shootdowns, the destruction of a Fifth Fleet radar in Bahrain by a $30K drone, and the erosion of alliances under [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) — dynamics he compared to Roman imperial decline [4]. Trump declared the strikes were conducted "in honor of" three servicemen killed in action — a formulation that substitutes faux-testimonial sentiment for strategic logic [57]. The "honor" framing is not merely a speech act; it is symptomatic of an institutional culture that celebrates the qualities that do not win wars (see [Revolution in Military Affairs]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/revolution-in-military-affairs.html)) [57].

Aurelien classifies the war as "a classic example of surprise-at-failure, which is one of the different types of surprise" (see [Aurelien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/aurelien.html)) [21]. The outbreak itself was not surprising: US leaders had threatened Iran for a decade, the methods were predictable, and Israel's participation was expected [21]. The surprise came because the overall strategic situation was read wrongly [21]. The dynamic Aurelien identifies is backwards logic from desired outcome to necessary false assumptions: since a ground invasion was infeasible, all faith had to be placed in an air campaign — but the campaign could only succeed if the Iranian state was very fragile, and if main state targets could be reliably hit, and if the Iranian people would rise up once the state was weakened. These assumptions had to be true for the objective to be viable, and therefore the assumptions were treated as true [21]. In the event, "the capability of the Iranian military was not properly understood, and the objectives of the Iranian state were wrongly evaluated" [21]. Unlike the Americans, the Iranians were not surprised — they had expected the attack and developed capabilities to pursue their objective [21]. Aurelien places this pattern alongside Operation Barbarossa (1941), the Iraq invasion (2003), and the Falklands (1982) as cases where the wished-for political outcome generated a chain of necessary false assumptions about military effectiveness [21].

The West cannot understand the Iran conflict, Aurelien argues, because its strategic concepts are outdated and its ruling class lacks military literacy [54]. The "escort" paradigm for Hormuz epitomizes this doctrinal obsolescence: the concept derives from World War-era anti-submarine warfare, when grouping merchant vessels with escorting warships reduced losses from U-boats. Today's threat is not individual submarines but swarms of drones and missiles that cannot be defended against at scale. Escorts provide more targets, not protection (see [Hormuz Toll System]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/hormuz-toll-system.html)) [54]. The purpose of attack is not necessarily to sink ships but to discourage sailing — a political and economic effect, not a kinetic one [54]. Iran holds the initiative and the United States cannot win ([Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html), [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html)) [54]. Western failure resulted from arrogance, inherited military folklore, and a simple disinclination to study Iranian military capability [54]. The most likely outcome is prolonged low-level tension that suits Iran and strains Western cohesion — and it will not be the West that decides when it stops [54].

O'Brien traces the war's origin to a personal decision. Trump's decision to attack Iran was not in the national interest and was made without political pressure to do so — a finding that directly challenges the assumptions of realism [2]. Wars are choices of individual leaders, not state actors pursuing national interest [2]. By May 2026, Trump was "desperate to exit" the war but unable to, with no good military options. The emerging Iran deal was worse than the JCPOA [19]. Trump called on Arab states to join the Abraham Accords — in O'Brien's guess, to appease Netanyahu and GOP hardliners expressing dismay at the prospect of a deal with Iran [19]. China was backing Iran, sending missile components and supporting Iran's ceasefire positions diplomatically [19]. Meanwhile, Trump spoke about the Taiwan arms sale as a "bargaining chip" — "It's a very good negotiating chip for us, frankly" — leaving democratic Taiwan twisting in the wind while China supported its friend Iran ([China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html), [Taiwan Standoff]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/taiwan-standoff.html)) [19]. Pape frames the negotiating dynamic in general terms: "States gaining leverage rarely hurry to end wars. … Tehran believes time increasingly favors Iran. Iran is no longer negotiating to stop losing. It is negotiating to win more" [5].

The domestic landscape compounded the strategic failure. There was no rally-round-the-flag effect during the war. US society remains locked in a partisan death match: one side does whatever Trump wants, while the other opposed the war from the start. The old patriotic ideas that would have generated national cohesion in a wartime setting are gone [2]. The gap between stated objectives and observable outcomes has introduced uncertainty about the credibility and predictability of US policy — shaping how both partners and adversaries will assess future American commitments [28]. The fracturing of Gulf cohesion and the economic volatility generated by the strait's disruption are consequences that will define the strategic environment long after any agreement is reached [28].

The war can be understood as a manifestation of Endless Warfare — the persistent, below-threshold conflict that adversaries wage against the United States (see [Endless Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/endless-warfare.html)) [29]. Iran's network of proxies and surrogates, built over decades, allows Tehran to project power and coerce neighbors at relatively low cost [29]. The war demonstrated that when adversaries practice Endless Warfare, conventional military superiority alone cannot compel outcomes — the adversary's theory of victory may be narrower (survival rather than conquest) and therefore more resilient [29].

The transition from diagnosis to explanation begins with the tempo of the conflict. Trump himself has admitted the United States is in "full-blown reaction-mode," effectively granting Iran control over the tempo of both military operations and peace negotiations [68]. "The current dynamic from the U.S. side is emotionally driven and self-defeating" [68]. The outcome of the war aligns with Iran's strategy all along — not a failure of execution by the US and Israel but a successful implementation of a twenty-year campaign plan [28].

Pnina Shuker and Andrew Milburn argue that Iran fought a different war than the one it was offered: rather than contesting air superiority symmetrically, it moved the contest onto terrain its adversaries were not equipped to contest — maritime coercion, economic disruption, information dominance, and political will [28]. The doctrine behind this has a name: mosaic defense, formalized in 2005 by the IRGC's Center for Strategy under Mohammad Ali Jafari and the ideologue Hassan Abbasi — a decentralized command structure designed to survive exactly the kind of decapitation strikes that opened this war [28]. Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi stated: "We've had two decades to study defeats of the U.S. military to our immediate east and west. We've incorporated lessons accordingly" [28].

Iran's theory of victory was simple: survive long enough to impose costs by striking the Gulf States and playing the Hormuz card [28]. Iran had studied the force structure it faced and designed its counter-campaign accordingly — targeting not strike aircraft but the tankers, radar networks, communications nodes, and command platforms that sustained them [28]. The cost exchange told its own story: Iran's drones and missiles cost thousands of dollars apiece; the interceptors fired to stop them ran into millions [28]. Tehran spent five years building an economy to evade sanctions — its tanker fleet grew from roughly 70 vessels in 2020 to nearly 550 by 2025, sustained by Chinese refineries that alone accounted for close to 90 percent of Iran's foreign oil sales [28].

Iran's kinetic targeting of Gulf Cooperation Council countries served a strategic purpose: demonstrating that supporting the US campaign has a price [28]. When Iran struck the UAE's oil terminal, Kuwait and Saudi Arabia closed their airspace to US military aircraft [28]. Project Freedom — Trump's tanker escort plan — collapsed when Saudi Arabia refused basing, citing lack of confidence in US strategy coherence (see [Project Freedom Collapse]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/project-freedom-collapse.html)) [28].

Pape traces the shift in Iran's negotiating posture: "Iran is no longer negotiating to stop losing. It is negotiating to win more. … Iran gained Hormuz – a source of leverage that no paper deal can diminish since it is built on new military realities of the power of mass drones" [5]. He suggests that "Iran may have discovered that controlled instability is more profitable than total closure. … Limited attacks, periodic disruptions, shipping uncertainty, drone incidents, insurance volatility, and the constant possibility of escalation may already be enough to sustain a large geopolitical risk premium in global energy markets" [16]. This allows Iran to monetize its chokepoint leverage continuously rather than wielding it as a binary weapon [16]. If oil stays above $90, Iran's fiscal position strengthens while Western economies absorb the inflationary cost [16].

Two theories of victory clashed: the US and Israel wanted surrender and Iran's collapse; Iran wanted survival. Tehran's narrower objective gave it the capacity to keep imposing costs and a negotiating position strong enough to turn resilience into political leverage [28]. The asymmetry extended to the negotiating sequence: before Iran has to resolve a single hard issue — the nuclear stockpile, the missile program, or its regional proxies — the United States lifts the naval blockade, withdraws forces, issues sanctions waivers, and makes frozen assets available [28]. "Pay first, verify later is not a strategy. It is leverage leaving the room" [28].

Pape identifies the deeper structural consequence. Washington has stopped bombing, but Iran has not been forced to surrender its leverage over Hormuz. Iranian resistance has constrained the further use of American force — deterrence working in a direction Washington did not anticipate [92]. The restraint that lowers the risk of an imminent spectacular Iranian attack simultaneously allows a regional balance of power to shift without US participation in the balancing process [92]. The driving question is not merely that Iran is gaining leverage, but who is gaining relative power while the United States is not acting [92].

## The Energy War: Operations and Market Dynamics

The energy war opened with an existential asymmetry that would shape every phase of the cascade. For the IRGC and possibly for many Iranians, this was an existential war — regime survival and national sovereignty at stake. For the United States, save for the nuclear program, it was not [67]. This asymmetry meant Iran could absorb far higher costs than the Americans and maintain political will long after domestic support for the war had eroded in the US [67]. The pattern was not new: in Vietnam, Iraq, and Afghanistan, none of the wars were existential for the United States, yet each was treated as existential by the adversary [67]. Friedman's theory is that Iran is prepared for two negotiating options: an agreement with Washington on terms more favorable to Iran, predicated on domestic political considerations at home, or a prolonged war, with Washington ultimately ending it as it did in Vietnam, Afghanistan and Iraq [67].

This strategic asymmetry set the terms for the energy front. The Strait of Hormuz itself would not "return to normal" without either a deal that ends Iranian control or a US-led military operation that forces the strait open to prevent Iranian control [66]. Iran could maintain its stranglehold for as long as it was willing to attack, harass, and threaten shipping there; protraction served Iran's interests by normalizing its de facto control of transit through the strait [66]. The Iranians are likely aware of that fact, which is one of the reasons they are stalling and delaying the negotiations process [66]. Iran never achieved sea control in the Strait of Hormuz; it did not need to — it needed only to make transit uninsurable and unprofitable [38]. Within days, crude oil exports fell 95% and LNG 99% — the largest supply disruption in the history of the oil market, in the International Energy Agency's judgement — achieved with technologically unsophisticated means: sea mines, short-range missiles, swarming fast boats, and enough demonstrated resolve to convince insurers, shipowners, and energy markets that transit was not worth the risk [38].

### The Week-by-Week Cascade

**Week 1 (early March).** Tanker traffic through the strait crashed from 40 vessels per day transiting in January to a single tanker making the trip on March 3, according to Vortexa data [7]. Iranian drone strikes forced QatarEnergy to halt production on March 2, taking one-fifth of global LNG export capacity offline in an instant — a blow to [Qatar]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/qatar.html) and the wider [Middle East]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html) [7]. Egypt faced a potentially existential crisis in a country wholly dependent on Israeli and Gulf Arab energy imports [7]. Europe's gas storage was already at a 5-year low, with [France]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/france.html) and [Germany]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/germany.html) hitting record lows [7]. Diesel and heating oil prices jumped almost 42% in just a few days, far outpacing increases in crude oil prices — the diesel and jet fuel crisis had begun [7].

**Week 2 (mid-March).** The shortfall became visible. Gulf countries affected by the blockade had exported 14 million barrels of crude and 6 million barrels of refined products through the strait; the world market was now in a 10 million barrel shortfall in crude and a full 6 million in refined fuels — 16% of all liquid fuels produced and consumed worldwide [8]. Pipeline diversion to Yanbu on Saudi Arabia's Red Sea coast increased to around 2.5 million barrels per day from 786,000 in February, but was nowhere near enough [8]. Iran was still able to ship oil — loading about 1.5 million barrels of crude a day, with China receiving about 1.25 million barrels daily, per Kpler data [8]. The Bab-el-Mandeb chokepoint remained under Houthi control, who could resume the blockade at any time [8]. Operation Prosperity Guardian, launched in December 2023 to unblock the waterway, was confirmed a failure — "a colossal blunder" in the words of the National Security Institute at George Mason University [8]. Insurance companies were unlikely to offer policies and shipping companies unlikely to risk their crews and vessels [8].

**Week 4–5 (early April).** Nearly 500 million barrels had been lost cumulatively — 250 very large crude carriers' worth [9]. Iran's missile hit rate against US bases reached 80%, even according to Israeli sources [9]. The US was running out of munitions, with most remaining targets buried deep inside mountains beyond bunker-buster reach [9]. Iran proposed a $2 million toll per tanker transit, payable in rials [9]. Trump threatened to escalate to wholesale destruction: "We're going to bring them back to the Stone Ages, where they belong" [9].

**Week 6 (mid-April).** The US imposed a naval blockade on Iranian ports — a counter-blockade, enforced "impartially against vessels of all nations" per CENTCOM (see [US Naval Blockade of Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/us-naval-blockade-iran.html)) [10]. Secondary sanctions were threatened against Chinese banks handling Iranian money [10]. Iran warned it would block the Red Sea along with the Persian Gulf and the Sea of Oman if the US blockade continued [10]. Iran's South Pars gas field was hit (again) by Israel together with its oil terminal on Kharg island — a strike on the Iranian part of the world's largest gas field, the other half belonging to Qatar [10]. The Pentagon sought help from US automakers for military production [10].

[Endtropy]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/endtropy.html) drew the structural parallel: this historical analysis was obviously a result of Trump's now active blockade, and the structural similarities to Napoleon's Continental System should be clear [13]. The Continental System had failed from economic and military impossibility to police it [13]. The neutrals were making their own deals — Japan in direct contact with Iran was a huge deal, being one of the US' strongest allies [13] (see [Monetary System Transition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/monetary-system-transition.html)). The counter-blockade revealed the war's true objective: controlling oil flow to [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html), not uranium enrichment [10].

**Week 8 (late April).** The "polycrisis" framing crystallized. The war had passed the two-month mark, and the damage to supply chains of energy, fertilizer, food, and industrial inputs was already done — just not registered by the Western ruling classes [11]. A super El Niño in the pipeline could produce concurrent crop failures, potentially yielding the biggest food crisis in post-WWII history [11].

**Week 10 (mid-May).** Cumulative supply losses from Gulf producers exceeded 1 billion barrels, with more than 14.4 mb/d of oil shut in [12]. OECD inventories were projected to hit "operational minimums" between May 9 and May 30, according to JPMorgan analysts [12]. SPR releases were unsustainable — the US had released 8.6 million barrels in a single week, the largest ever weekly release, surpassing the 2022 peak rate [12]. Iran retained roughly 70% of its prewar missile stockpile [12].

### The Borrowed Calm: Three Buffers and the Price-Physical Divergence

By June, the market had stabilized — but the calm masked a borrowed balance. Tchakarova identified three buffers absorbing the Hormuz shock simultaneously [14][15]. The American buffer: record crude exports averaging 5.35 million barrels per day in May, plus a 66-million-barrel SPR drawdown, stocks at 349 million approaching 1983 lows [14]. The Chinese buffer: China's 7.8 million barrels per day against its 2025 average of 11.6 represented a 33 percent demand gap filled by inventory draws, not absent consumption [14]. The Russian buffer: Moscow forced to cut exports as Ukrainian strikes crippled its refining system [14]. The calm was real — and it was engineered, temporary, and depleting [14]. Each buffer was finite: the SPR approached 1983 lows, Chinese inventories drew down daily, Russia's export decline removed supply structurally [14]. The world was not balanced; it was borrowed [14].

By early July, Brent had fallen from a wartime peak above $180 to around $72, with forward curves moving into contango [22]. But the physical data told a different story: Persian Gulf production remained roughly 45% below its pre-war level, regional exports sat around 25% below February levels, and shipping companies remained reluctant to send vessels back through Hormuz [22]. The price decline was driven by four distinct offsets working simultaneously: physical rerouting; coordinated reserve releases of more than 300 million barrels — the largest emergency drawdown in history; China demand destruction, with crude imports falling roughly 40%, close to five million barrels a day; and broader demand erosion, with global oil demand contracting an estimated 5.5 million barrels a day in Q2 2026 [22].

Full restoration of export capacity was expected to take many months, with Qatar's damaged LNG facilities potentially requiring years [22]. But shipping companies remained wary of sending vessels back into the strait given unresolved insurance costs, lingering concerns about sea mines, and uncertainty over the durability of the ceasefire framework itself [22]. Scenario modeling assigned roughly 50% probability to a fragile equilibrium (oil $70–85 while the memorandum holds despite recurring friction); 30% to a renewed rupture (a serious incident sends prices back toward $100+); and 20% to durable stabilization (a lasting settlement allows prices to settle near pre-war levels) [22]. The price collapse was best read as evidence that global demand management and reserve coordination could now absorb shocks that would have been unmanageable a decade ago — but Iran's retained leverage over Hormuz meant the underlying rupture had not closed, only been priced as if it had [22].

### The Refining Bottleneck

The binding constraint had shifted. Krugman argued that a true reopening of the Strait would have made more crude oil available to the global economy, but that wouldn't have done the global economy much good in the short run, because the world didn't have the capacity to turn that crude into usable products [30]. Around 10% of world refining capacity was offline, primarily from Ukraine's drone campaign against Russian refineries in the parallel [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html) [30]. The effective consumer price of oil rose approximately $75/barrel during the war, while crude rose only $45 — high consumer prices kept demand depressed even when crude prices nearly normalized. A Hormuz reopening would add crude but not usable fuel [30].

The Honest Sorcerer's supply-chain data confirmed the refining crunch. According to the IEA, nearly 3 million bpd of Middle East refining capacity had been shut down; refineries globally had cut back by 9 million bpd — more than 10% of global capacity, per Kpler's Matt Smith [42]. China's refinery runs crashed to pandemic lows as crude imports collapsed by more than 40% in June year-on-year [42]. Ultra-low sulfur diesel reached $168 per barrel against ~$80 crude [42]. Russian diesel exports collapsed from 800,000 bpd to zero under the Ukrainian drone campaign [42]. The SPR drawdown rate fell from ~1.3 million bpd in mid-June to 0.43 million by July 10, trending toward zero by early August (see [SPR Depletion Crisis]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/spr-depletion-crisis.html)) [42]. Basing economic or military decisions on crude prices alone — in a world stripped of refining capacity — had become an outright civilizational risk [42].

### The Renewed Blockade and the Insurance Transformation

The ceasefire did not hold. By mid-July, the renewed blockade sent war-risk premiums surging. Premiums for vessels transiting the strait rose from approximately 2% to 3–10% of hull value — as much as 40 times the prewar rate, per a senior Marsh executive [53]. VLCC daily earnings hit approximately $470,000, a record level [53]. Only 4–9 commercial vessels were transiting Hormuz per day, and no LNG tanker had crossed since July 16; Qatari LNG vessels accumulated in the Gulf, unable to reach export markets [53]. The Houthis separately announced a blockade of Saudi Arabia's Red Sea ports, compounding the chokepoint pressure on both major Middle East oil routes simultaneously [53].

The insurance market underwent a structural transformation. War-risk premiums surged — in some cases by more than 1,000% — forcing a shift from annual policies to voyage-by-voyage underwriting [27]. Risk, once pooled broadly, was now assessed in granular, transactional terms [27]. As private insurers reduced exposure, governments stepped in with sovereign-backed mechanisms: the US International Development Finance Corporation supported maritime insurance capacity, [India]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/india.html) developed sovereign-backed insurance pools, and major insurers formed new consortia to maintain coverage in high-risk corridors [27] (see [Insurance and Trade Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/insurance-and-trade-warfare.html)). The emergence of regionalized insurance systems meant that the ability to insure trade was becoming a strategic tool, determining which routes remained viable and which became economically infeasible [27].

### The Double Bottleneck

In August, Pape's live briefing with energy analyst [Jeff Currie]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/jeff-currie.html) identified a double bottleneck constraining the global fuel supply [87]. The first bottleneck was crude — the Hormuz disruption itself. The second was refining capacity: Russia's refining capacity was collapsing under [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html)'s drone campaign, constraining refined products globally [87]. The crack spread — the margin between buying crude and selling gasoline and diesel — reached $70 per barrel, the highest ever recorded [87]. Higher consumer fuel prices were possible even before crude buffers fully ran out, because the refining shortage added pain on top of the crude shortage. [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) possessed refining surplus capacity; the US did not [87].

Currie's TGS 229 conversation sharpened the picture. The 3-2-1 crack spread hit $60 per barrel — a three-decade high — with diesel products up 81% year-to-date against a 34% broader index gain [93]. The effective consumer price of oil was not the crude price but the refined-product price: diesel at $160 per barrel effective against ~$100 Brent [93] (see [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html)). Trump himself reportedly stated the US was "this close to running out of diesel" [93]. Currie assessed the conditional probability of $150/bbl Brent above $100 as "pretty high" [93]. Russia had converted from the world's largest product and crude exporter to a net importer — now importing jet fuel from Japan and diesel from India [93]. Fiber-optic drone navigation technology was transferring from Ukraine's battlefield innovations to Russian and then Iranian operators, compressing the learning curve across theaters [93].

### The Cascade to Food: The Kiel Institute Model

[The Kiel Institute]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/kiel-institute.html)'s KPB 206 (March 2026) simulation modeled the cascade from its origin. The shutdown blocked roughly one-fifth of the world's oil and one-quarter of its LNG, and standard trade models underestimated the impact because they missed the bottleneck mechanism: energy disruptions cascaded through chemicals and fertilizer production into food prices, amplifying losses for the world's poorest countries [91]. The [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) lost just −0.07% of welfare under the short-run full-closure scenario, while countries in South Asia and [Africa]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/africa.html) faced losses 10–20 times larger ([Zambia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/zambia.html) −5.49%, Sri Lanka −3.47%, India −1.78%) [91]. Global oil prices rose +11.94%, chemicals +5.75%, and food +2.75% in the short run [91]. Military analysts assessed that just two well-placed attacks would suffice to halt commercial transit, making forced reopening effectively impossible without sustained large-scale military operations [91]. The 1980–88 "Tanker War" precedent underscored the difficulty of suppressing dispersed asymmetric maritime threats [91]. Only Chinese-flagged tankers carrying yuan-denominated cargo were permitted sporadic passage during the closure — direct evidence of [Global System Bifurcation]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-bifurcation.html) operating in real time [91].

## Diplomatic and Regional Realignment

The war fractured the Western alliance framework from the inside out and then reordered the region around non-Western guarantors. The movement runs from the initial breach with [Europe]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/europe.html), through a US-Israel strategic divergence that proved unbridgeable, to the emergence of a regional diplomatic architecture in which Washington is one player among several.

[Phillips O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) emphasizes that the United States threw its European allies, Japan, and South Korea under the bus by starting the war without informing them — after more than a year of insults, threats, and tariff wars. In O'Brien's assessment, "the idea of the US as an irresistible force is over—possibly for good" [2].

[Velina Tchakarova]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/velina-tchakarova.html) reframes the ceasefire not as American coercive success but as axis course correction. Within the logic of Cold War 2.0, [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) and [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) recognized that an uncontrolled escalation spiral risked tipping into [Global System Rupture]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-rupture.html) territory: a systemic breakdown that would damage their own economic and strategic foundations as much as the West's. Iran was therefore pressed, by its own partners, to accept a framework that preserves the bloc's coherence and long-term material support while denying Washington a genuine strategic victory. The axis consultation was "almost certainly a green light, not an obstacle" — conducted through a structured consultation architecture, a standing coordination mechanism, trilateral deputy foreign minister meetings in Beijing in March 2025, expert-level sessions in Moscow in April 2025, and coordination meetings at the UN [6].

The diplomatic impasse that followed was not a negotiation gap but an ontological one. [Aurelien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/aurelien.html)'s framing, as quoted by [The Honest Sorcerer]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/the-honest-sorcerer.html), is that the US and Israeli positions — which seek to damage and if possible destroy Iran as a functioning state — can simply never be reconciled with Iran's. Since there is zero overlap between the desired outcomes, there is nothing to be negotiated. The topic of nuclear enrichment is thus nothing but theater, an excuse why not to admit that there is nothing to talk about [11][17].

The war produced a lasting strategic divergence between the United States and Israel. Washington seeks to use military gains to create a diplomatic framework that constrains Iran while stabilizing the region, whereas Israel remains skeptical that an ideologically driven regime can be durably moderated through negotiation. The disagreements reflect not a breakdown in the alliance but the reemergence of a strategic divergence temporarily papered over by the exigencies of war [23].

The fracture surfaced publicly when JD Vance admonished Israeli critics of US diplomacy at a White House briefing on June 18, stating that Israel "might not be attacking the only powerful ally that I have anywhere left in the entire world" [23]. Washington conducted ceasefire negotiations through Pakistani mediation without Israeli participation, despite Israel bearing much of the war's cost and risk [23]. Israel has shifted its operational focus toward Lebanon, where Hezbollah represents a more immediate threat, and fears that any Iranian financial relief will rebuild Hezbollah [23].

Joe Kent reveals that Israel's long-term goal for Iran is complete regime change toward a secular, business-friendly government. In the short term, Israel views a chaotic Iran — potentially fracturing into ethnic states — as beneficial, despite the disaster it would cause for the US [26]. The nuclear issue was a pretext used by Israel to draw the US into a war to neutralize Iran, a country Israel couldn't defeat alone [26].

The ceasefire deal crystallized Israel's exclusion. Israel was not a party to the talks and, by its own officials' account, had not even seen the text of the agreement it would have to live with. Hizballah gains new protection from Israeli strikes under the deal's terms, and the missile program Israel considers existential goes unaddressed [28]. The Gulf states Israel hoped would drift toward Israel have done the opposite — maintaining and in some cases deepening their ties with Tehran [28]. When Netanyahu pressed for strikes in Beirut that risked the deal, Trump reportedly told him he "would be in prison" without American protection [28]. One Israeli diplomat summed up the result as "a glorious failure" [28].

Beyond the US-Israel fracture, regional powers began negotiating their own stability through non-Western channels. Rumors increasingly talked about the [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html)ns having secret negotiations with Iran through [Pakistan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/pakistan.html), with the full support of Russia and China. After seeing how much US security guarantees are worth — or rather, how risky it is to support a bombing campaign on Iran in light of the promised retaliations on desalination plants and oil refineries — the Saudis seem desperate to find a new way of counterbalancing both Iran and the US. This raises an intriguing possibility: what if Iran and Saudi Arabia manage to agree on a non-aggression pact in exchange for Iran not building the bomb? [18]. [Oman]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/oman.html) tries hard to remain non-aligned but in reality leans toward Iran [18]. The emerging [Hormuz Toll System]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/hormuz-toll-system.html) is one institutional expression of this reordering [18].

As [The Honest Sorcerer]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/the-honest-sorcerer.html) frames the broader picture: "I believe what we see in geopolitics is nothing short of such a tectonic shift: from Russia's invasion of Ukraine to the war on Iran and the blockade of the Strait of Hormuz, or from an increasingly unhinged US foreign policy to the rapid rise of China world politics have become unrecognizable. After almost eight decades of unquestioned Western dominance the world order is turning upside down and inside out" [18].

China's positioning runs through this reordering. Kent argues China is exponentially benefiting from the conflict, positioning itself as a more appealing partner to Middle Eastern states by offering deals instead of regime change [26]. The war erodes US security guarantees and the petrodollar system, demonstrating US limitations to the world and bolstering China's position in [great power competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html), including regarding Taiwan [26].

George Friedman (GPF) argues the most important long-term aspect is how the war will affect US-China bilateral relations. China's abstention from the conflict is twofold: China needs to rehabilitate economic ties to the US — still its largest customer — and is heavily dependent on oil and LNG imports through Hormuz [27]. Friedman notes a historical parallel: after World War II, the US helped Germany and Japan evolve economically out of self-interest. A shared interest in reaching an understanding on trade and the muted reaction to the Iran war are important indicators that a US-China agreement on trade is emerging [27].

By August the realignment had a working mechanism, and [Velina Tchakarova]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/velina-tchakarova.html) maps how its diplomatic and coercive tracks ran in parallel rather than in sequence. On August 1 [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) said he would strike Iran "very hard" and the United States dispatched security alerts to ten countries in the region, warning of possible flight cancellations; the same day a tanker was targeted in the strait and [Kuwait]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/kuwait.html) said it had repelled Iranian drones [90]. Three days later [Qatar]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/qatar.html) distributed a draft plan for restarting talks while Trump told [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) to reach a Hormuz solution with [Oman]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/oman.html) or face "devastating air strikes" [90]. Tchakarova reads the pairing as "the anatomy of controlled escalation" — coercive signalling and secret diplomacy running at once — and is careful to mark the judgment as her own: what she sees is "not de-escalation, it is a holding pattern" in which any single maritime event could return the system to open combat [90].

The mechanism under discussion allocates authority rather than restoring passage. According to reports Tchakarova cites, Iran would hold authority over inbound traffic and Iran and Oman jointly over outbound shipping, with Qatar acting as the political conduit for the August 4 offer and for implementation of a US-Iran memorandum that includes freedom of navigation [90]. Iran's foreign ministry spokesman Baghaei qualified the arrangement pointedly: "A settlement between Iran and Oman does not necessarily mean free transit when the United States' 'naval blockade' and other hostile operations against Iran continue" [90]. Tchakarova's assessment of what a completed deal would signify is conditional and should stay that way — *if* a stable Iran-Oman agreement is reached, it would be "the first instance since 1945 in which a US enemy has been able to dictate terms at a strategic chokepoint" [90]. The same conditional governs who else gains: should Iran secure even partial control of the inbound stream, Beijing continues buying discounted Iranian crude while Moscow collects a risk premium on all seaborne oil leaving the region, whatever the price — the [DragonBear]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/cold-war-2.html) acquiring an asset it did not have to fight for [90].

## The Escalation Trap: From Ceasefire Collapse to Exit Criterion

The war's escalation ladder runs from a collapsed ceasefire through self-reinforcing strategic and revenge dynamics, through operational thresholds crossed one after another, through institutionalized Iranian control of Hormuz, to strategic paralysis and a formalized escape test. Each rung narrows the available off-ramps until the only question is whether tactical success can be converted into political success — and whether that conversion can happen before January 2029.

### The Ceasefire Collapses and the Trap Becomes Self-Reinforcing

The Versailles Memorandum did not hold. Around July 13, 2026, the ceasefire collapsed — [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) reinstated the blockade; Iran resumed drone and missile strikes on shipping [31][36] ([Hormuz Ceasefire Collapse 2026]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/hormuz-ceasefire-collapse-2026.html)). Iran, emboldened by its success in defying America, demanded sovereignty over the Strait of Hormuz [30]. Trump declared he would collect 20% tolls on all shipping traffic, then shifted to demanding that countries invest in the United States as a condition for passage — both proposals wildly illegal and impossible to enforce [30][31]. Trump declared the United States the "Guardian of the Strait of Hormuz," said America will "probably run it," and announced plans to charge a 20% fee on cargo — a political commitment that makes backing down significantly harder [34].

Pape argues that the ceasefire collapse is not simply another round of strikes but evidence that the conflict has entered a fundamentally new phase — the Escalation Trap is becoming self-reinforcing [32][33]. "Both governments are becoming politically invested in objectives that cannot both be achieved at the same time" [32][33]. Pape describes Trump wanting to "take control of the Strait of Hormuz and charge a 20% fee" and carrier redeployment per CENTCOM, and "Trump has moved from punishment to reversing the defeat" [33]. On the Iranian side, the new Supreme Leader's nationally broadcast statement has transformed revenge into an independent political objective [33]. Pape describes the Supreme Leader's call to avenge the blood of leaders and all martyrs, red flags for blood revenge, and mass demonstrations [33]. This is not temporary rhetoric but a structural feature of the conflict: even if one engine of escalation slows, the other can continue driving escalation. Most analysts are treating these as separate problems; they are not — they are beginning to interact in ways that fundamentally change the logic of the war [33].

Three pressures converge on Trump's decision calculus. The first is the Hoover risk: Trump signed the MoU to avoid an economic crisis, explicitly saying he did not want to be "Herbert Hoover." But the MoU failed to stop the oil inventory drawdown — the world gained only about 14 days of supply from a couple hundred million barrels released [33]. The SPR salt caverns face a structural risk: at low levels, water eats away the cavern walls causing collapse. "We're getting into new territory" [33]. The second is MAGA turnout: according to the Reagan Institute's Summer Survey, 51% of self-identified MAGA Republicans favor replacing Iran's government; only 25% prefer a negotiated settlement [35]. If Trump fails to open Hormuz, a significant portion of MAGA will not show up at the polls [33][35]. The third is the revenge spiral itself: Pape draws on 25 years of suicide terrorism research — 95% of suicide attacks coerce democratic withdrawal from territory. The Rushdie fatwa (1989) is the closest historical parallel — it produced attacks and deaths for decades. The current call to action has significantly greater strategic implications [33]. Every US bombing campaign kills more Iranians who are then portrayed as martyrs, strengthening the legitimacy of the revenge call. If even one limited revenge attack succeeds, public pressure for decisive retaliation would intensify, giving Trump even stronger incentives to authorize larger operations including ground options [33].

Iran's political objectives have expanded through three phases: Iran is moving aggressively from survival to ambition to now revenge [33].

### The Funeral Mobilization and the August Shock

The funeral of Iran's Supreme Leader produced perhaps the largest public mobilization in the history of the Islamic Republic, with millions filling the streets chanting "Kill Trump" and "Revenge" [25] ([Supreme Leader Funeral 2026]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/supreme-leader-funeral-2026.html)). Massive nationalist mobilization changes Tehran's bargaining position by increasing the political costs of moderation. The funeral has enabled a new, more ambitious Iranian war aim: weakening and ultimately ending the permanent American military presence in the Gulf [25]. Iran is appealing directly to Gulf monarchies — offering lower transit fees for states that distance themselves from Washington [25].

Pape forecasts that August could become the decisive month: the SPR falling toward its operational floor as hurricane season threatens Gulf Coast production, and Iran possessing growing nationalist confidence, continuing Hormuz influence, and an opponent with diminishing economic room [25].

### Crossing the Anti-Ship Missile Threshold

On July 15, 2026, Iran crossed a discrete threshold: the use of heavy anti-ship missiles with up to 1,000-pound warheads against tankers in the Strait of Hormuz [46]. Numerous US Navy ships had been dangerously repositioned close to Iran's coast, well within Iran's anti-ship cruise missile range [46]. Even 30–40 US casualties would instantly shatter diplomatic options, forcing an uncontrollable, public-fueled demand for payback that could lock both sides into a costly spiral and make Stage Three politically difficult to avoid [46].

### Shaping Operations and the Point of No Return

On the evening of July 17, US Central Command conducted multiple waves of airstrikes against [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html), targeting key bridges, railway junctures, and power lines connecting key cities, particularly in Hormozgan province and around the major port city of Bandar Abbas [48]. Pape assesses these as classic "shaping" operations in preparation for a ground assault [48]. Once the United States begins shaping the battlefield for possible ground operations, stopping becomes politically harder than continuing: having already accepted the risks of sustained escalation, Washington faces growing pressure to demonstrate that those costs will produce tangible strategic gains [48]. Iran's trap deepens in parallel: having survived six days of US attacks while still contesting Hormuz, Tehran faces mounting pressure to demonstrate that American escalation cannot succeed. Standing down after these strikes would risk signaling weakness both domestically and across the region [48].

### The Civilian-Infrastructure Threshold

Iran crossed another threshold: instead of concentrating on military bases, Iranian missile and drone strikes hit a vital power and water desalination plant in [Kuwait]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/kuwait.html) — part of the system that produces roughly 90% of the country's drinking water. Unlike past fragments that had inadvertently hit desalination plants, this plant was directly targeted [49]. Pape identifies the strike as a categorical escalation: civilian survival infrastructure is becoming a direct battlefield target [49]. Drawing on his Bombing to Win research, Pape's central finding is that when civilians suddenly lose access to life's basic necessities, survival rapidly overwhelms every other concern. Instead of rallying around the flag, people become consumed by the immediate struggle to keep themselves and their families alive — "anger comes after survival" [49]. The strike proves the concept for a vulnerability pattern that extends across the Gulf: [Qatar]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/qatar.html) and Bahrain each rely on only three to five desalination plants, while [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html) and the [UAE]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/uae.html) rely on 30 and 70 respectively [49] ([Geoeconomics of Water]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/geoeconomics-of-water.html)).

The logic of civilian-infrastructure targeting runs in both directions. Escalating military strikes on energy facilities and civilian infrastructure would serve only to alienate the Iranian population and distract from the Iranian leadership's appalling incompetence, strengthening the regime — the opposite of the intended political effect [68]. The current MoU must be abandoned and recast in a more evenhanded manner, leading to permanent peace. Adhering to it as currently written will only ensure we will be forced to return in the future [68].

Trump's declaration that Iranians "have not suffered enough yet" signals a potential shift from military-denial targeting to civilian-punishment targeting — bridges and electrical power infrastructure whose principal purpose is not military denial but mass suffering [62]. The technical mechanics are stark: destroying transformers and transmission substations can produce widespread blackouts but is often repaired within days or weeks, while destroying the generating units themselves produces prolonged outages lasting months or years, since these are custom-engineered, weigh hundreds of tons, and are not kept as spare parts [62]. For Tehran — a city of roughly ten million experiencing July temperatures near 100°F — the consequences of a prolonged blackout would be catastrophic. Water pumps serving 800 million gallons daily would fail within hours. Roughly one-quarter of perishable food would be lost when cold chains collapse [62]. The 1995 Chicago heat wave killed 739 people in five days with 2.7 million residents; a one-to-three-month blackout in Tehran would produce casualties on a far greater scale [62]. History strongly suggests that inflicting massive civilian suffering through electric power targeting rarely produces rapid political capitulation when governments believe their survival is at stake [62].

### The Nuclear Dimension: Pickaxe Mountain and the Ground Power Dilemma

[Israeli]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html) intelligence reported that Iran had moved thousands of uranium-enrichment centrifuges into deeply fortified tunnels beneath Pickaxe Mountain near Natanz, buried 300–450 feet under solid rock — beyond the reach of existing conventional bunker-busting munitions [59]. Iran denied IAEA access for verification. Trump identified the facility as a potential military target, but it remained untouched [59]. Airpower did not solve the strategic problem. It transformed it. If Israeli intelligence is correct, Iran responded not by surrendering its nuclear program but by moving its surviving enrichment capability deeper underground [59]. Pape frames this as "the Ground Power Dilemma": if Washington concludes Pickaxe Mountain has become Iran's protected nuclear sanctuary, the choice narrows to tolerating a hardened nuclear program or accepting far greater risks of operations beyond an air campaign [59] ([United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html)). Drawing on the 2003 Iraq WMD precedent, Pape argues that leaders escalate based on what they believe adversaries are capable of, not what later proves true. Uncertainty favors escalation [59].

### Ground-Operations Pre-Positioning

Two carrier strike groups moved from the Indian Ocean into the Gulf of Oman — placing 5,000–6,000 personnel within Iranian missile and drone range [33]. Pape identifies two operational plans: Plan A — carriers provide air cover for the "Southern Passage" near Oman; Plan B — if air cover is insufficient, take territory on the coast, specifically Chabahar. "This would be the worst losses we've seen since Vietnam." Any ground war will likely last at least six months [33].

### The Suppression Problem and the No-Third-Choice Framework

Pape's analysis (July 20, 2026) argues that America is unlikely to reopen the Strait of Hormuz before the November midterm elections [52]. The distinction between tactical and strategic success is foundational: tactical success measures military performance — launchers destroyed, attacks declined — while strategic success requires the political objective of restoring normal commercial shipping. The judge of strategic victory is the market itself [52]. Only 50–100 drones or missiles are needed to swarm ships, and Iran possesses tens of thousands; a single successful strike can reverse months of restored confidence [52]. Iran spans roughly 1.65 million square kilometers — more than three times the size of Iraq and about four times California — with a population of about 92 million and roughly one million personnel [52][61]. Destroying 80–90% of these assets is insufficient — all must be suppressed continuously. A Shahed-136 drone is approximately the size of an F-150 truck; Iran has an estimated 2.5–3 million comparable vehicles, making visual identification of launch platforms effectively impossible [52][61]. Iran's coastline rises into rugged mountains with concealed firing positions — there is no accessible beachhead. A Marine Expeditionary Unit of roughly 2,200–2,500 Marines controls only a one-by-two-mile area [52].

Pape's framework is stark: there is no third choice between surrender and escalation. The war is likely to continue "at least through January" 2027 [33]. [Lindsey Graham]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/lindsey-graham.html)'s call for nuclear strikes is "clickbait" — a nuclear detonation in Gaza or the West Bank would kill tens of thousands of Jews, echoing the bombing of Auschwitz dilemma [33].

A parallel US-Israel deal in [Lebanon]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/lebanon.html) undermines the MoU with Iran — the two documents became contradictory. Russia will face a strong recession scenario and boost its summer offensive in Ukraine [37] ([Global System Rupture]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-rupture.html)).

### Spiral Phase Oscillation: The Illusion of Control

Pape's Breaking Analysis #10 (August 3, 2026) reframes [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html)'s abrupt cancellation of planned strikes on Iran as not de-escalation but oscillation [78] ([Middle East]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html)). Iranian officials denied any negotiations were underway, and the structural indicators continued to deteriorate: commercial tanker traffic through Hormuz had slowed to an estimated 80% drop in volume, with only one inbound large crude carrier arriving in the past week [78] ([Red Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/red-sea.html)). The Bab el-Mandeb remained under severe pressure as unofficial Houthi blockades forced multiple commercial vessels to turn back. State Department security alerts warned American citizens throughout the Middle East to prepare for "unexpected escalation" and consider departing the region [78].

In the Spiral Phase of the Escalation Trap, tactical pauses are not off-ramps. They are moments when political leaders search for alternatives because the available military options have become costlier while the underlying strategic problem remains unresolved [78]. Airpower has not restored secure commercial transit through Hormuz, has not broken Iran's regional leverage, and has not produced a political settlement. The failure on all three measures creates an increasingly difficult ground power dilemma for Washington [78]. The American political calendar is shifting incentives in ways that favor Iran, which treats time itself as a weapon. A pause without a political settlement allows Tehran to keep consolidating its emerging security resistance belt stretching from the Mediterranean through the Red Sea to the Persian Gulf [78] ([Iran Security Resistance Belt]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/iran-security-resistance-belt.html), [Escalation Theory Critique]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/escalation-theory-critique.html)). The indicators to watch are not presidential rhetoric but Hormuz and Red Sea commercial traffic, Chinese purchases of discounted Iranian oil, and tightening global oil and refined-product inventories [78].

### The Risk-Pricing Reframe and Legislative Escalation

Pape's August 5 analysis reframes the negotiating surface from shipping to risk. The 7% cargo-value fee under discussion is materially identical to the approximately 7% of cargo value that marine insurers currently charge for Persian Gulf voyages — meaning the rumored deal institutionalizes the wartime-risk premium rather than eliminating it [85]. A typical VLCC carries roughly 2 million barrels of crude worth about $160 million at $80 per barrel. Even if only a fraction of prewar Gulf exports resumed, a 7% levy applied across Hormuz traffic could generate well over $100 billion annually — on the order of $140 billion under prewar shipping assumptions — what Pape terms "one of the largest geopolitical wealth transfers in modern history" [85]. It is difficult to imagine any American president accepting a permanent arrangement that effectively institutionalizes Iranian geopolitical leverage over the world's most important energy chokepoint [85]. Pape's base case: either no agreement is reached, or any deal that leaves the chokepoint-fee issue unresolved is fragile from the outset [85]. A genuine breakthrough requires bilateral public confirmation. As of writing, Iranian officials dismiss US assertions of bilateral negotiations as "repeated lies" and "desperate cries," maintaining that Tehran engages only via regional mediators [85].

On August 7, Iran's parliament began reviewing the "Strategic Action for the Security and Sustainable Development of the Strait of Hormuz and the Persian Gulf" bill, barring vessels and cargo linked to the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html), [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html), and other "hostile nations" and imposing transit fees of 5–7% of cargo value [86]. If enacted, Iran would no longer be threatening to control the strait — it would be legislating that control as the normal operating rule for international commerce [86]. Pape frames this as a profound strategic shift from military coercion to institutionalized maritime authority. Air power alone rarely compels a determined adversary to abandon what it regards as a core national interest. It can destroy military capabilities, raise economic costs, and delay strategic programs. But it seldom changes the opponent's underlying political objective [86].

The legislation connects to what Pape calls the "Hail Mary" ground war threshold. As costs to Americans rise from Iran's sustained Hormuz control, a president with little to lose may gamble on a limited ground operation for symbolic victory before the midterms [86]. The 600-mile Iranian coastline cannot be reliably controlled from the air; territory ultimately requires forces on the ground [86].

### Stage Four: Strategic Paralysis and Iran's Victor's-Justice Demands

Pape's August 9 briefing introduces Stage Four of the Escalation Trap: strategic paralysis. If the US remains in paralysis — doing nothing while Iran consolidates — Iran becomes the rising regional hegemon in the Persian Gulf, the fourth center of world power [87]. Stage Four would see Iran escalate to toppling [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html)'s MBS, organizing assassination attempts against MBS and [UAE]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/uae.html) leaders, and projecting power through the security resistance belt from the Mediterranean through the Red Sea to the Gulf [87] ([Iran Security Resistance Belt]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/iran-security-resistance-belt.html)). The period of maximum leverage runs through January 2027: Iran's objective is to "drive a stake through the heart of the Trump presidency" by wrecking the US economy, not merely wounding it temporarily [87].

Iran's Supreme National Security Council has issued six demands before Hormuz reopens — terms Pape reads as victor's justice, not bargaining. The new demands require everything up front: approximately $300 billion for damages, over $100 billion in frozen assets, and ending Israel's occupation of Palestine (not negotiating — ending) [87]. Iran is "playing balance-of-power politics like a black belt" while the US behaves "like green belts" — a persistent analytical error of underestimating Iran [87].

The [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html)-[Turkey]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/turkey.html)-[Pakistan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/pakistan.html) defense agreement — the "Mecca pact" — fills a power vacuum left by US decline. It is not "a new NATO": it lacks an integrated command structure, Turkey has 18 million Kurds as internal vulnerability, and the pact is defensive in framing [87]. Its emergence confirms the Escalation Trap's prediction: when the dominant power becomes less willing or able to guarantee order, regional states hedge, build coalitions, and diversify security relationships [87].

Beneath the surface of paralysis, military signals accumulate. Trump told 800 generals and admirals at the Pentagon on September 30, 2025 that the US military would treat liberal cities as training grounds and that they could "get on board or get out" [87]. The CENTCOM top air commander's retirement mid-war is an unusual signal. B-1 and B-52 bomber wings have been brought to bases with weekends canceled. Pape warns that what appears to have been paralysis may start to look more like action [87].

### The Exit Criterion and the 2029 Regional-Balance Test

Pape's August 10 essay formalizes the Escalation Trap into a testable theory by defining the escape criterion: tactical success converted into political success [88] ([Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html)). A stable settlement requires Iran to accept constraints because it believes the alternative is worse, while the US and its regional partners accept a balance of power they can sustain. Until that conversion happens, any pause in bombing or temporary agreement is just a pause inside the trap [88]. A reduction in violence does not falsify the trap; neither does another temporary agreement [88].

The standard Pape sets for judging US policy is not whether [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) can defeat Iran, but whether by January 2029 he has left behind a regional system more stable than the one the US entered in 2026 [88] ([Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html), [UAE]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/uae.html), [Turkey]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/turkey.html), [Pakistan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/pakistan.html)). The goal is a regional balance in which Iran has enough power and incentive to accept balance rather than continue trying to expand its leverage, and in which Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Turkey, Pakistan, and other regional powers have enough capability to constrain Iran without requiring permanent American military dominance [88]. The choice is not between "victory" and "withdrawal" — it is between continuing to escalate without a credible theory of political victory and using remaining American leverage to construct something that can actually last [88].

Falsification conditions are explicit: Pape would change his assessment if sustained Iranian military degradation produced genuine political concessions, if Tehran accepted a durable settlement addressing the central disputes, and if Washington could reduce military pressure without triggering another cycle of Iranian escalation [88]. The war is Trump's "Hurricane Katrina": the political sequence — war → energy shock → inflation and shortages → economic pain → political blame → pressure to escalate → deeper strategic failure — is one Pape has been warning about [88].

## The Ceasefire Collapse and the Thirteen-Day Campaign

The [Versailles Memorandum]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/iran-ceasefire-mou.html) was a dead letter almost from the moment it was signed. [O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) documents the day-by-day military operations that reignited the war in mid-July, framing them as the inevitable product of a "consequence-free world" the US chose to inhabit [50]. The memorandum was a sham: Iran gave up nothing, Trump lacked any foundation to declare victory, and when the truth dawned he faced the binary choice between admitting the greatest strategic defeat in US history or restarting the bombing. He chose the latter [50]. Trump declared the ceasefire formally over on July 8 at the [Ankara Summit]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/nato-ankara-summit-2026.html) [50].

It was, in O'Brien's assessment, an extraordinary gamble: restarting the bombing in a weaker strategic position than when operations ended in April, with the midterm elections approaching and 60–66% of Americans opposing the war [50]. On July 11, Trump tweeted that the US military was "ready, willing, and able, for a one year period of time, subject to extension, to completely decimate and destroy all areas of Iran." O'Brien juxtaposes this against Trump's own celebration of the assassination of Khamenei at the start of the war — the same policy of leader assassination now makes Trump himself a legitimate target for Iran [50].

### Five Consecutive Days (July 11–16)

What followed was a sustained campaign of mutual strikes, documented day by day by O'Brien [50]:

- **July 11:** CENTCOM struck approximately 140 targets and set terms to end hostilities. The IRGC struck the container ship *GFS Galaxy* in Hormuz and declared the strait closed.
- **July 12:** US strikes on Qeshm Island, Bandar Abbas, and Hajiabad; first use of one-way attack drones this campaign. Iran fired its widest missile/drone volley yet at UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain, Oman, and Jordan.
- **July 13:** Five-hour US strike wave on Bushehr, Chah Bahar, Jask, Konarak, Abu Musa, Bandar Abbas. IRGC missiles struck two UAE-flagged tankers in Omani waters — one Indian crew member killed, several injured; the oil price spiked almost 10%.
- **July 14:** Third consecutive night of coastal strikes; Trump reinstated the naval blockade and threatened infrastructure next.
- **July 15:** Seven-hour fourth-night strike wave plus a separate round on Greater Tunb Island; an empty tanker was disabled with Hellfire missiles for ignoring the blockade.
- **July 16:** Fifth consecutive day of strikes; Trump reportedly weighing ground operations and bombing an underground site linked to Iran's nuclear program.

### The Escalation Arc

[Richardson]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/heather-cox-richardson.html) documents the erratic decision-making behind the strikes. Trump announced he was reimposing a blockade on Monday; yesterday he reversed course, claiming that Gulf allies told him they would rather invest directly in the U.S. than pay tolls [44]. Yet Barak Ravid of Axios, who often has inside information from the White House, reported that Trump had convened a Situation Room meeting with his top national security team — Vice President Vance, Secretary of State Rubio, Defense Secretary Hegseth, Chairman of the Joint Chiefs Caine, CIA Director Ratcliffe, Middle East envoy Witkoff, and other senior officials — to discuss "new plans for devastating strikes" against Iran [44].

Before the meeting, Trump told the Fox News Channel: "next week comes the power plants. Next week comes the bridges. We're gonna knock out all their power plants. We're gonna knock out all their bridges unless they get to the table and negotiate" [44]. Nate Swanson, a former member of Trump's negotiating team, told foreign policy specialist Laura Rozen of *Diplomatic* that the escalation was probably a ploy to kick-start further negotiations — "a very risky and low probability gamble, but nothing else makes sense" [44].

The threatened strikes on Iran's power network triggered a second-strait dimension. Parisa Hafezi, Samia Nakhoul, and Jonathan Saul of Reuters reported that Iranian leaders had asked the Houthis they back in Yemen to close the [Bab el-Mandeb]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/red-sea.html) strait in response [45]. The Strait of Hormuz and the Bab el-Mandeb are the two main routes for oil exports from the Middle East. The closure of the second strait would exacerbate energy shortages even as the [U.S. oil reserve]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/spr-depletion-crisis.html) drops to its lowest level since 1983 [45].

[Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) had ignited the [Hormuz ceasefire collapse]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/hormuz-ceasefire-collapse-2026.html) [50].

### The Aqaba Pipeline Crisis

Pape identifies a new escalation vector: Iran's threat to [Aqaba]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/jordan.html), Jordan's only coastal city on the northern tip of the Red Sea [51]. The US Embassy in Amman issued an emergency alert advising Americans to avoid Aqaba's airport and seaport due to a specific and credible threat, and air raid sirens went off in Jordan warning of incoming Iranian missiles [51].

The threat follows a Washington meeting between [Iraq]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iraq.html)'s prime minister and Jordan's foreign minister to accelerate the Basra-Aqaba pipeline — a 1,600-kilometer, $18 billion project designed to carry 2.25 million barrels per day from southern Iraq to the Red Sea [51]. The Iran war had cut southern Iraqi exports by approximately 70%, exposing Baghdad's dependence on the Strait of Hormuz and making the bypass pipeline a strategic imperative [51].

Under the Escalation Trap framework, Pape argues that Iran has powerful incentives to strike any infrastructure that could permanently reduce its coercive leverage. A functioning Basra-Aqaba pipeline would undercut Iran's [security resistance belt]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/iran-security-resistance-belt.html) by creating a permanent alternative outlet for Iraqi oil [51].

### The Inconclusive Contest

Davidson (GPF) frames the renewed fighting as an unequal but ultimately inconclusive contest. Washington can weaken Iran's ability to threaten shipping but cannot cheaply guarantee normal commercial passage or force Iran to surrender its remaining leverage without accepting a prolonged campaign [43]. If Iran cannot withstand the campaign indefinitely, it can preserve enough uncertainty to deny the US a clean political victory. Washington must provide security continuously; Iran needs only intermittent success [43].

Iran's margin for victory is shrinking as US strikes degrade the military systems needed to sustain disruption and the reinstated [naval blockade]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/us-naval-blockade-iran.html) constrains seaborne oil exports [43]. Iran's strongest remaining escalation option is also the one most likely to unite its adversaries: attacking Gulf energy infrastructure or expanding maritime disruption to the Bab el-Mandeb strait — either of which could end the Gulf states' role as mediators, deepen their military cooperation with Washington, and bring [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html) back into the campaign [43]. The US strike on the Aq Taqeh Khan railway bridge in Aqqala County — on a corridor linking Iran to [Central Asia]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/central-asia.html) and onward to [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) and [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) — demonstrated that pressure would not remain confined to the waterways [43].

The likely outcome is a truce rather than a settlement. Washington can keep winning the strikes, and Iran can keep denying Washington a clean victory, but neither can sustain that contradiction indefinitely [43].

### Eleven Nights and Sideways Escalation

By July 22, O'Brien documents the continuation of US strikes through eleven consecutive nights [58]. The US has no actual military operation to achieve what it wants, beyond a full invasion and deposition of the Iranian regime [58]. General Barry McCaffrey estimates a full invasion would require 600,000 troops and one year — a commitment that, as O'Brien notes, is not happening [58].

Iran's response has been what O'Brien calls a form of "sideways" escalation: rather than attacking only US forces, many of Iran's attacks over the previous ten days have focussed on the resources of US allies in the region. What they need to do is make the war so politically costly for the US that [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) emerges from the war in a stronger position [58]. The timeline from July 12–21 documents this widening pattern of strikes across Qatar, UAE, Bahrain, [Kuwait]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/kuwait.html), [Jordan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/jordan.html), Oman, and Saudi Arabia — including desalination infrastructure, air bases, and oil tankers [58]. The IRGC also claimed to have hit an Amazon data center in Bahrain; this report is still awaiting verification [58]. [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html) was drawn deeper into the widening exchange as Iran struck Jordan's Muwaffaq Salti Air Base in Azraq, killing at least two US service members, and a ballistic missile struck a US base in Saudi Arabia — the first Iranian attack on Saudi Arabia in over three months [58].

### The Thirteen-Day Campaign Endgame

On July 23, the US launched its last day of direct attacks in this phase — thirteen consecutive nights of strikes that achieved no strategic gains while further burning through critical munitions [76]. The White House leaked the stockpile depletion problem even as Ambassador Mike Waltz publicly contradicted himself on *Meet the Press*: first blaming the Biden administration for the munitions shortfall, then denying it existed and saying the military had "everything that it needs," then pivoting again to admit the shortfall by saying those leaking the information should go to jail [76]. Trump's threats of large reinforcements were, in O'Brien's assessment, always a bluff: the US has already burned through the advanced systems that can be fired from a distance, and escalating with ground forces would blow through what remains of its air defense stockpiles in a very short time [76].

After the US halted strikes on July 23, Iran continued attacking — either directly or through its proxies — demonstrating that no ceasefire existed [76]. O'Brien's framing is blunt: "Iran is toying with the USA." Trump wants out, desperately, and Iran will not let him [76].

### Iran's Strategic Framework

O'Brien offers a speculative framework for Iran's strategy, prefacing it: "I have no real idea of what their strategy is, and will describe what it might be simply from observing what is happening" [76]. The framework runs on an Aims/Ways/Means logic:

- **Aims:** Control over the Strait of Hormuz, a weakened US military presence in the region, the ability to restart the nuclear program, and the dissolution of the US-led coalition.
- **Ways:** Keep fighting, deplete US munitions, demonstrate US weakness to allies, sustain high oil prices, and delay talks.
- **Means:** Existing cruise and ballistic missiles supplemented by cheap drones and Chinese/Russian deliveries.

It seems that Iran has calculated that time is on their side: the longer they can draw this out, the closer to November midterms, the more desperate Trump becomes, the stronger Iran's leverage. They can see how unpopular the war is in the USA — arguably now the least popular war in US history [76].

### The Likely Shape of Peace

O'Brien sketches the probable endgame. Iran would issue a non-concession statement of no intent to develop nuclear weapons. Iranian control over the Strait of Hormuz would be tacitly acknowledged, with Iran charging "administrative fees" rather than "tolls." Iranian assets would be unfrozen, sanctions wound down, and the US would begin a partial withdrawal from the immediate vicinity of Iran. Trump would package it all as a "massive victory" [76]. In O'Brien's judgment: "It will actually represent the quickest and arguably the most decisive defeat in US history" [76].

## Horizontal Escalation: Theater Widening from Gulf to Caspian

The US-Iran war began as a Gulf-centered confrontation. Over five months its kinetic footprint expanded outward in concentric rings — from the Persian Gulf to the Red Sea, into NATO territory, across Jordan's dispersal architecture, into the Mediterranean, and finally to the Caspian basin. Each step outward forced Washington to defend a new piece of the regional system without reducing pressure on any prior front. The trajectory runs from the original Gulf theater to a two-front maritime structural trap and beyond, into a basin where two geographically distant wars converge.

### The Gulf's Digital Frontier

Even within the Gulf itself, Iran's targeting expanded beyond the energy and military assets that had defined the war's opening phase. Iran struck data centers in the [UAE]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/uae.html) providing Amazon Web Services, causing them to go down for several months at minimum [69]. AI infrastructure is particularly at risk to such a looming threat; data centers going down for several months at a minimum [69].

### The Red Sea Front

The first step outward came through the Bab el-Mandeb gateway. Within 48 hours of July 16, Yemen's Iran-backed Houthis launched a coordinated barrage of ballistic missiles and drones targeting [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html)'s Abha International Airport in direct retaliation for airstrikes on Sanaa [47]. Pape assessed that this assault heavily risks horizontal escalation that would widen the Iran war by opening a second maritime front at the strategic Bab el-Mandeb gateway [47]. [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) gains additional leverage without directly committing its own forces [47].

[Geopolitical Futures]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/geopolitical-futures.html)' Bokhari reframes the Houthi entry against the prevailing assumption that the group acts as Iran's proxy. The Houthis had avoided escalation throughout the worst crisis the Iranian regime has faced since its inception — a restraint that revealed their independent calculus [64]. Their central objective is consolidating power as Yemen's dominant political and military force; every move serves that goal. Their belated entry into the Iran war was an opportunistic exploitation of regional instability, not an ideological commitment to Tehran's conflict [64]. Iran supplies resources that bolster Houthi capabilities, but it does not exercise the command and control associated with a traditional proxy relationship. The Houthis retain considerable freedom to operate as they see fit [64]. As Iran's position deteriorates, Houthi participation will likely remain measured — the movement will exploit opportunities so long as benefits exceed costs, but will pull back once sustained escalation threatens its domestic standing or invites unacceptable retaliation [64]. The Houthis remember Operation Rough Rider, the 52-day US campaign in 2025 that consisted of more than 1,000 strikes against them [64].

### Crossing into NATO Territory

By late July, the war's geography had crossed another threshold. Tchakarova reports that horizontal escalation had crossed into [NATO]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/organizations/nato.html) territory: Iran has threatened RAF Fairford in the [United Kingdom]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-kingdom.html) over its use for B-1 bomber missions, and NATO air defences engaged Iranian drones and missiles near Incirlik Air Base in [Turkey]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/turkey.html) [65]. The geography of the war has now touched a NATO member state directly — a different order of escalation risk than the Gulf-and-Yemen theatre tracked since February [65].

The [Red Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/red-sea.html) blockade had also produced its first confirmed kinetic strikes on tankers rather than diversions alone: the Houthis claimed missile and drone strikes on two Saudi oil tankers, setting one ablaze [65] — a development tracked in the [Global System Rupture]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-rupture.html) framework as the transition from blockade declaration to enforcement.

### The Two-Front Maritime Trap

[Robert Pape]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/robert-pape.html)'s July 28 analysis reframes the war as a two-front maritime contest. By opening an active [Red Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/red-sea.html) front through its regional partners while keeping Hormuz closed, Iran is creating a two-front war problem in strategically linked theaters; the escape route itself is coming under sustained attack [70].

The three-buffer framework decomposes the ~16 mbpd that offset the ~20 mbpd Hormuz baseline: roughly 3–4 mbpd from SPR releases, roughly 4–5 mbpd from Chinese demand destruction, and roughly 7 mbpd from physical bypass infrastructure — [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html)'s East-West Pipeline ~5 mbpd to Yanbu, Abu Dhabi pipeline ~2 mbpd to Fujairah [70]. Each buffer has a distinct vulnerability profile. SPR releases are finite and approaching the congressional mandated minimum. Chinese demand destruction is now reversing. Pipeline bypass depends on the security of Red Sea and Gulf of Oman shipping lanes [70]. Over 40% of Saudi oil bypassing Hormuz via the Red Sea is not reaching the global market after Houthi attacks. By the end of August, those offsets may have fallen by more than half [70]. Pape's forecast: "shortages, not just disruption, are about to begin in earnest" [70] — a milestone tracked in the [SPR Depletion Crisis]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/spr-depletion-crisis.html).

The precedent is sobering. Operation Rough Rider (March 15 – May 6, 2025) — seven weeks, 1,000+ strikes — tactically dominated but only fought the Houthis to a tie. Shipping through the Red Sea remains down 60% a year later [70]. If reopening one corridor required months of sustained combat and still failed to restore commercial traffic, analysts should begin by assuming that reopening two corridors simultaneously will take at least as long—and probably longer [70].

The carrier arithmetic underscores the constraint. Of America's eleven carriers, only four are deployed worldwide, two are already committed to Iran combat operations, and at most two more could be shifted — producing a maximum of four carriers available to split across both theaters. The United States does not have the deployable forces for two long naval wars [70] — a constraint with direct implications for [Energy and Resources]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/energy-and-resources.html).

[Javier Blas]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/javier-blas.html) frames the bypass problem in pipeline-capacity arithmetic. The Sumed pipeline has a capacity of about 2.5 million barrels a day, roughly half of the current flow from Yanbu. Adding the Israeli pipeline would bring an extra 1.2 million barrels a day of transport capacity, narrowing — but not closing — the gap [71]. VLCCs cannot transit Suez fully laden. The required workaround is lightering: supertankers offload part of their cargo into Sumed, transit with reduced draft, and reload on the Mediterranean side [71]. Oil tankers heading to Asia after emerging through Sumed's Mediterranean terminus are routed through the eastern Mediterranean rather than near the Indian Ocean, adding ~25 days to the trip from Saudi Arabia to Japan [71].

Two conditions could invalidate the planning assumption: a political settlement — Washington accepts terms that persuade Tehran and its partners to stop threatening shipping, likely full military withdrawal of all US forces and bases from the region (implausible as of today) — or a massive new naval force generation. Absent either, the battles to reopen both the Strait of Hormuz and the [Red Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/red-sea.html) are likely to last for months, not weeks [70] — a timeline tracked in the [Insurance and Trade Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/insurance-and-trade-warfare.html) framework.

### The Dispersal Architecture: Jordan as Target

The next step outward struck not a chokepoint but the architecture that sustains US power projection itself. Pape's Breaking Analysis #8 (July 29, 2026) frames the strike that hit [Jordan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/jordan.html)'s Muwaffaq Salti Air Base as confirming a June 15 "Period of Maximum Leverage" forecast and the entry of [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html)'s war into the spiral phase [72] — an event tracked as the [Iran-Jordan Strike]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/iran-jordan-strike-2026-07-29.html).

The IRGC explicitly stated that its Aerospace Force targeted a US military CENTCOM center inside Jordan. All incoming projectiles were successfully intercepted by air defenses. Global oil prices rose 5% [72].

The attack occurred shortly after Trump's meeting with Netanyahu. Iran is willing to take the fight to the US on its own terms rather than merely retaliating for previous American attacks [72].

The Pentagon has dispersed assets away from vulnerable Gulf bases to Jordan — Muwaffaq Salti now hosts roughly 4,000 US personnel, advanced fighters, drones, intelligence assets, and air-defense systems. Jordan has become a pillar of the geographic architecture that allows the United States to sustain military operations while reducing the vulnerability of its frontline Gulf facilities [72].

Iran's objective has shifted from destroying American military power to making that power progressively more expensive, politically difficult, and operationally distant [72]. Iran has begun contesting not merely American forces, but the entire regional system through which the United States projects military power; no location supporting U.S. military operations should be considered beyond reach [72]. The costs of sustaining America's regional military presence are likely to continue rising even if no decisive battlefield breakthrough occurs [72].

### The Mediterranean Extension: Damietta

The war's kinetic footprint expanded into the Mediterranean on July 29. Two ships caught fire after an explosion at Damietta Port on [Egypt]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/egypt.html)'s Mediterranean coast — the first recorded drone attack on commercial shipping at a Mediterranean Egyptian port and the first expansion of the [US-Iran War 2026]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html)'s kinetic footprint into the Mediterranean approach corridor [79].

Three maritime tracking firms corroborated different parts of the event. Kpler reported both vessels had been moved away from Damietta Port. Ambrey stated the explosion was believed to be caused by a drone strike. Egypt's petroleum ministry confirmed no injuries but did not officially confirm drone causation [73]. The vessel that caught fire was the Energos Winter, a US-owned LNG storage tanker owned by Energos Infrastructure via Apollo Global Management-managed funds. The second ship, GasLog Salem, is an LNG carrier operated by Greek firm GasLog [73]. Trump told reporters he had been briefed on the explosion and implied Iran was responsible, completing the response with a pledge to retaliate — "it's our turn to hit them" [73]. Windward reports that the number of vessels passing through Bab al-Mandeb has decreased by one-fifth since July 20, and traffic through the Strait of Hormuz remains at less than one-tenth of prewar levels [73] — data tracked in the [Insurance and Trade Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/insurance-and-trade-warfare.html) framework.

Pape's Breaking Analysis #9 (July 31, 2026) frames the Damietta strike as the most important strategic-map event of the war so far: the strike extends the war's geography into a third front, demonstrating for the first time that [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) or its proxies can threaten both ends of the Middle East's export system [79] — a doctrine tracked under the [Iran Security Resistance Belt]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/iran-security-resistance-belt.html). The IRGC's operational doctrine envisions a security resistance belt connecting the Persian Gulf, Red Sea, and Mediterranean into a single strategic theater; every new front forces Washington to defend another piece of the global energy system without reducing pressure anywhere else [79]. The strike's target profile — a US-owned civilian LNG vessel — signals that American commercial assets are no longer insulated from the conflict, and that the war is evolving from a contest over military access to the Gulf into a broader contest over who controls the economic arteries of globalization [79]. In June, the dilemma was to concede Hormuz or escalate; by August, the dilemma has expanded to conceding all three avenues of access to Persian Gulf energy or escalating — a trap that makes Trump progressively more locked into escalation rather than less [79], an assessment tracked in the [Damietta Port Drone Strike]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/damietta-port-drone-strike-2026-07-29.html) event file.

### The Caspian Theater

The final geographic expansion reached the Caspian basin. The war's geographic footprint is expanding beyond the Gulf and the Black Sea into the [Caspian]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/caspian.html) Sea, where two geographically distant conflicts — the US-vs-Iran war and the Russia-Ukraine war — are converging in a single basin [80], a dynamic identified by [Geopolitical Futures]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/geopolitical-futures.html).

The first Caspian strike occurred on March 18–19, 2026, when [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html) struck [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html)'s Bandar Anzali port on the Caspian coast — damaging naval vessels, headquarters facilities, and a shipyard [80], an event recorded as the [Bandar Anzali Strike]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/bandar-anzali-strike-2026-03-19.html). The strike aimed at disrupting the [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html)-[Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) arms-smuggling route: Iran supplies Russia with Shahed drones, drone-production expertise, artillery shells, and short-range ballistic missiles, receiving in return advanced drones and tactical intelligence [80]. [Zelensky]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/zelensky.html) has increasingly portrayed Iran as an active co-belligerent, welcoming U.S.-Israeli strikes against Iran; the strike was reportedly aimed at disrupting a key Iran-Russia arms smuggling route [80].

On July 26, Ukraine struck an Iranian cargo vessel in the Caspian Sea, killing a civilian sailor. The episode highlights how the interconnected nature of the Russia-Iran partnership and the wider regional confrontation is creating new pressure points [80] — a development tracked as the [Ukraine Caspian Vessel Strike]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/ukraine-caspian-vessel-strike-2026-07-26.html). [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html)'s direct action in the Caspian marked a new dimension of the convergence.

If Tehran continues expanding the conflict across the Arab world and pressure continues to mount on Washington to force Iranian concessions, the United States could ultimately seek to apply pressure against Iranian assets in the Caspian region itself [80].

The Caspian theater also carries a diplomatic dimension. [Azerbaijan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/azerbaijan.html)'s position as a Caspian littoral state with close Israeli ties and a long Iranian border makes it a key node in this expanding theater — and the [South Caucasus]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/south-caucasus.html) corridor connecting the Caspian to Turkey and Europe via the Trump Route adds further strategic weight to the basin [80]. [Kazakhstan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/kazakhstan.html) has joined the Abraham Accords, and Kazakhstan, Azerbaijan, and Armenia participate in the Gaza Board of Peace — signaling that trans-Caspian states are engaging with Middle East diplomatic frameworks even as the military dimension of the Caspian grows [80]. Israel's opening of its first permanent embassy in Ashgabat (Turkmenistan) in April 2023, approximately 20km from the Iranian border, extended the diplomatic ring around Iran's Caspian flank [80].

## Domestic Politics and the War's Legitimacy

The erosion of domestic legitimacy for the US-Iran war follows a widening arc: from the human costs the administration tried to conceal, through the financial costs it tried to obscure, to the congressional revolt and political collapse that made the war's continuation untenable. The trajectory runs from casualty concealment through financial opacity to constitutional crisis.

[Heather Cox Richardson]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/heather-cox-richardson.html) opens the account with the war's human toll. As of July 20, 2026, the US death toll reached 17 killed in action since Trump launched strikes on February 28, with 427 service members wounded [55]. An Iranian attack on a US base in Jordan killed two soldiers and left one missing; another died in northern Iraq [55]. The Jordan strike was the fourth in five days — demonstrating, as *New York Times* journalists Greg Jaffe, Julian E. Barnes, and Jonathan Swan reported from official sources, that Iranian forces still retain substantial missile inventories and are becoming more skilled at evading US air defense systems [55].

The Pentagon under Defense Secretary Hegseth did not disclose the earlier strikes, casualties, or damage, citing operational security; no major briefing has been held since early May [55]. CBS News reported that Hegseth's claim that "almost 90%" of injuries were minor gives a misleading picture — the Army classifies a soldier as "seriously injured" or "very seriously injured" only if at risk of dying from wounds within 72 hours, meaning the classification system itself minimizes the apparent severity [55]. The administration did not consult Congress before launching the war, as the Constitution requires, and has exceeded the 60-day time limit for the use of military force against an "imminent threat" without congressional approval (see [Iran Ceasefire MoU]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/iran-ceasefire-mou.html)) [55].

The financial costs compounded the legitimacy deficit. The Pentagon is seeking a $67 billion supplemental appropriation. Hegseth claimed the Iran war has cost $37.5 billion, though independent analysts assess the true figure is significantly higher [56] (see [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html)) [56]. The Navy and Air Force will exhaust their operational funds by the end of July 2026; the Pentagon is already diverting equipment and maintenance money and canceling exercises to sustain the campaign [56].

The gap between the official figure and reality widened as further reporting emerged. While Hegseth told Congress the war had cost $37.5 billion, Gordon Lubold, Mosheh Gains, and Courtney Kube of NBC News reported on July 14 that the internal Pentagon estimate is about $100 billion [63]. Walker notes that when economists at Moody's added higher gas prices to the cost of the war, they estimated that it has cost U.S. households about $1,100 each [63]. The spending unfolds against a backdrop of fiscal strain: Steve Rattner of MS NOW reported that under Trump, the debt held by the federal government has surpassed 100% of GDP, and by 2030 will shoot past its all-time record from WWII [63]. Domestic austerity continues alongside war spending — Medicaid, SNAP, foreign aid, scientific research, and CDC funding are being slashed while the Pentagon seeks more [63].

The political backlash built from these costs. Sen. Murkowski (R-AK) raised the possibility that the administration was using the supplemental funding request as a backdoor way to circumvent the War Powers Resolution — specifically, the constitutional and legal requirement for Congress to approve military action in Iran [63]. On July 23, the House passed a concurrent resolution to end the war in Iran by a vote of 214 to 208, with four Republicans — Thomas Massie of Kentucky, Brian Fitzpatrick of Pennsylvania, Warren Davidson of Ohio, and Tom Barrett of Michigan — joining the Democrats [63]. The Senate declined to advance Van Hollen's S.J. Res. 180 [63].

Public opinion mirrored the congressional revolt. Megan Messerly of *Politico* reported that just over a third of MAGA voters think the Iran war has been worth the economic cost, down from about half in May [63]. A person close to the White House told Messerly that White House officials are "at a maximum level of frustration right now, and I was told by very senior people that the president is now fully aware that the only way to the victory that he wants is completely politically impossible. The American people just will not support the kind of escalation it would take at this point. We've got more dead Americans and a politically impossible situation" (see [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html)) [63].

## Technology, Cost, and Systemic Effects

The war exposed a structural asymmetry in military technology that widens from the tactical exchange ratio through industrial-base depletion to global systemic cascades. At the battlefield level, cheap mass defeated expensive small-batch platforms; at the industrial level, munitions depletion timelines stretched to half a decade; at the systemic level, the war's effects propagated through covert operations, energy-to-food linkages, sanctions erosion, and the Global System Rupture diagnostic. The trajectory runs from the drone-vs-Patriot cost imbalance to Tchakarova's assessment that political order itself is approaching rupture.

O'Brien identifies the core problem at the tactical level: the United States went into the war relying on the very pricey Patriots or THAAD air defense systems that cost millions per shot to engage not only cheaper Iranian ballistic missiles but even super cheap Iranian drones. It was a costly mistake. It meant that the US could not fight for that long [2]. The broader conclusion O'Brien draws is that the US war machine is built on wrong principles — expensive, technologically advanced systems in small numbers, optimized for a paradigm of warfare that [drone warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/drone-warfare.html) has rendered obsolete [2].

Urbanski ([RUSI]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/rusi.html)) argues that the Iran war and the Ukraine war yield one coherent set of lessons for NATO's budget planners. Neither found a way to defeat a superpower decisively on the battlefield. Both found ways to make a superpower's victory expensive — and potentially unaffordable — by shifting the contest into domains where the Western alliance has under-invested: the economics of munitions, the protection of infrastructure far behind the front line, commercial risk, and the public's confidence in everyday security [38]. The scale of munitions depletion is quantified in CSIS's estimate: the US expended roughly half its Patriot inventory, more than 850 Tomahawk cruise missiles, and over 1,000 air-launched stand-off weapons. Rebuilding those stockpiles will take four to five years [38].

The war also marked the emergence of AI as a central operational tool. RUSI (Carneiro Júnior) documents the role of Palantir's Maven Smart System during Operation Epic Fury. The platform — descended from Project Maven, which provoked internal revolt at Google before Palantir took it over — fused satellite reconnaissance, persistent drone surveillance, signals intelligence, and population pattern-of-life analysis to identify, prioritise, and recommend strike packages. By April 9, US Central Command reported more than 13,000 strikes against Iranian targets, roughly 1,000 on the opening day alone [41]. Palantir's CTO told Bloomberg Television the conflict would be remembered as the first major war in which AI played a central role. The juxtaposition is analytically significant: a war fought substantially over the survival of Iran's nuclear programme is difficult evidence for Palantir's manifesto claim that "One age of deterrence, the atomic age, is ending, and a new era of deterrence built on AI is set to begin." See [AI Military Race]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/ai-military-race.html) [41].

The defense industrial base bore the cost of these technological and operational demands. CSIS (Cancian and Park) analyze the Trump administration's $87.6 billion supplemental request to Congress (June 24, 2026). About one-third of the request appears to be driven by the costs of the Iran war [40]. A major omission is base repair — 228 destroyed structures identified by The Washington Post [40].

Beyond the battlefield, the war's systemic effects propagated through covert operations, energy, food, and sanctions architecture. Levitt documents how post-war Iran has escalated external operations in Europe through the IRGC's "gig economy" model — recruiting disposable agents via gaming platforms for assassination plots and surveillance. These operations are funded through an oil-backed shadow banking system that circumvents Western financial architecture. See [Organised Crime and Statecraft]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/organised-crime-and-statecraft.html) [24]. Davidson (GPF) identifies a structural vulnerability in the defense industry that extends beyond munitions depletion: in U.S. Army deployments, more than 70 percent of logistics tonnage is fuel; some 55 percent is consumed by the logistics systems themselves. Disruptions caused by the war in Iran — particularly the closure of the Strait of Hormuz but also mining and attacks on nearby vessels — showed how quickly energy supply chains can be disrupted. See [Defense Industrial Resilience]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/defense-industrial-resilience.html) [27].

Colibasanu (GPF) identifies the energy-to-food cascade as a defining second-order effect. From 2023 to 2025, the Gulf was the world's largest regional exporter of urea and ammonia and the second-largest exporter of DAP and MAP. Around one-third of global seaborne fertilizer trade passes through Hormuz. See [Food and Agriculture]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/food-and-agriculture.html) [27]. Colibasanu frames the broader lesson: the key pattern across all regions is not collapse but persistent cost inflation and volatility — resilience is replacing efficiency, and the cost of resilience is permanent [27].

The US focus on Iran also eroded the sanctions architecture against Russia. The US issued a general licence to authorise the delivery and sale of Russian crude oil and petroleum products already loaded on vessels during the Hormuz crisis, kneecapping G7 price cap efforts just as they were biting. Nearly 30% of Russian crude in April 2026 was transported on G7 tankers. The RUSI commentary author recommends that the EU and UK should pursue a more ambitious objective and resume their plans to replace the price cap for a full maritime services ban [39].

Tchakarova's domain diagnostics place the US-Iran War domain (S1) at RUPTURE — the most severe status in her seven-domain framework. Her Day 144 diagnostic identifies kinetic enforcement of a routing regime as the defining operational pattern, with a dual blockade active. See [Iran Ceasefire MoU]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/iran-ceasefire-mou.html) and [Red Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/red-sea.html) [60]. The campaign had struck 95 locations across 12 cities. The S1 domain feeds the S5 (Global Supply Chain Fracture) domain generatively: strikes on Iranian power infrastructure produced the Red Sea blockade through Houthi third-party action [60]. Her Day 150 diagnostic confirms the tactical binary has been practically abandoned. The United States has conducted airstrikes against Iran on a nightly basis without interruption, reaching a thirteenth consecutive night on 24 July. The S1 domain holds at RUPTURE, but the war is no longer bilateral-with-retaliation but multilateral-with-NATO-adjacency. The S7 domain approaches RUPTURE. See [Global System Rupture]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-rupture.html) [65].

## Great-Power Restraint and Cross-Theater Coupling

The US-Iran war did not unfold in isolation. It ran along fault lines connecting three great powers and two shooting wars, and the tension between coordinated restraint and structural escalation pressures across linked theaters defines the arc of great-power engagement from June through August 2026. The progression runs from a quiet crisis ceiling holding the worst outcomes at bay, through a shared munitions bottleneck binding both wars into a single resource problem, to an active Chinese military-support escalation that threatens to break the ceiling from below.

### The Triumvirate of Happiness: Great-Power Restraint on the Iran File

Tchakarova frames the great-power coordination around the Iran file as the "Triumvirate of Happiness" — the moniker she assigned to clandestine discussions between Washington, Beijing, and Moscow designed to keep the Iran war from escalating and pulling the world down into a [Global System Rupture]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-rupture.html) [74]. In her own analytic voice, [Tchakarova]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/velina-tchakarova.html) describes it as neither an alliance nor a peace but a crisis ceiling [74].

On July 24, Trump released a declaration stating that "both Xi and [Putin]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/putin.html) have now confirmed to me that they will not sell any weapons to Iran," including Chinese companies. "I took them both at their word," he said [74]. This came just days after Secretary Rubio held separate discussions with Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi and Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov about the Persian Gulf — along with the South China Sea and Ukraine — on the sidelines of the [ASEAN]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/organizations/asean.html) ministerial in Manila [74].

The shared interest holding the Triumvirate together is negative: none of the three wants a complete break in oil flows out of the Gulf, a spike in energy prices globally, and the subsequent energy, fertilizer, and food consequences of a prolonged shutdown of the Strait of Hormuz and Bab-al-Mandab [74]. What is the Triumvirate? "It isn't an alliance, and it isn't a peace. It is a crisis ceiling," Tchakarova writes — cooperation without binding commitment, a floor that is only as strong as the willingness of all three to accept that the systemic costs of rupturing it outweigh the costs of staying put. It is sustainable precisely because no member has to recognize it, allowing each state to cooperate with the others on Iran while avoiding being tied down in any other arena. If and when one or more members decides to stop abiding by the ceiling, it collapses, releasing the very tensions the three have been keeping at bay [74].

Trump's publicizing of Xi's and Putin's promises is, in itself, an insurance policy against such fragility: he is trying to turn informal off-the-record agreements into public ones that are costly to break in terms of reputation. But the move also reveals the deal's weakness. Neither Beijing nor Moscow has offered anything to back up Trump's version of events, and Russia has plenty of reason to keep vague its intentions in supporting Tehran [74].

### The Patriot Bottleneck and the Two-War Continuum

The Iran war and the [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html) share a binding constraint: finite US Patriot interceptor missiles, each costing $3–5M, with little room to increase production. Ukraine wants Patriots to shoot down Russian missiles; the US is currently using the Patriots to defend its bases in the Middle East against Iranian missiles. The Patriot is one of the only weapons available that can intercept ballistic missiles. "There are two theaters, but there is only one bottleneck" [74].

The bottleneck places the escalatory ladder in Washington, not in the capitals of its partners. Netanyahu's point was that Israel would obey Washington in its war with Iran: "We know the situation, if we're attacked we will respond, but we will also rely on President Trump's decision and timing," he told Trump, according to one Israeli official. Several Israeli commentators pointed out bluntly that the US was now running the war and Israel was sitting in the passenger seat [74]. [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html) deferred to the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) on the timing and scope of strikes [74].

[Zelensky]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/zelensky.html) had a clear goal when he met with Trump: to secure fulfillment of a promise made during a NATO summit meeting at the White House to issue the necessary licenses for Ukraine to produce Patriot interceptor missiles domestically. Zelensky later told Fox News that Trump had agreed: "The President said, I accept that I will be granting the licenses." Ukraine needs twenty-five Patriot systems to help defend its cities from Russian drones and ballistic missiles. Zelensky also met with executives from Lockheed Martin and spoke of "joint production" discussions [74]. The proxy fights and pays the price; Washington controls the escalatory ladder and the industrial base needed to fight the war [74].

Both proxy presidents returned home weaker than when they arrived. Netanyahu returned to Israel a week after the bombing campaign started, frustrated that he could not continue his attacks on Iran as Trump negotiated a deal. He had wanted to continue his fight, but instead he was sidelined. Zelensky returned to a country still being bombarded, relying on an American supply chain that can only be established domestically over several years [74].

### Iran-Ukraine Infrastructure Strike Coupling

Beyond the Patriot bottleneck, Tchakarova identifies a parallel in targeting logic between the two theaters. Since March 2, Iran has pursued a retaliatory approach of targeting energy and water desalination facilities in the Gulf region [89]. Meanwhile, Ukrainian Prime Minister Shmyhal expects that this winter the Russian Armed Forces will also target water supply facilities. "Russia's goal now is to destroy our water supply," he said — noting that last winter Russia targeted electricity and heating, but it was possible to survive as long as gas and water remained available during power outages [89].

But the deepest structural coupling between the two auxiliary wars is not their targeting parallel. Tchakarova identifies the binding constraint as the shared Patriot interceptor stockpile: "The Iran war has drained United States stockpiles of Patriot interceptors, the same PAC-3 munitions that provide Ukraine its only reliable defense against Russian ballistic missiles. This is the clearest and most consequential Simultaneity coupling in the entire system, and it converts two geographically distant wars into a single coupled problem governed by one finite resource" [89]. "There are two theaters, but there is only one bottleneck" [89].

### China's Escalating Military Support and the MANPADS Threshold

[O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) documents the progression of Chinese military support to [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) through the first six months of the war [75]. [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html)'s assistance consisted of missile propellant chemicals — sodium perchlorate shipped via IRISL-owned vessels from the Gaolan Port chemical terminal in Zhuhai, with at least five shipments tracked and analysts estimating the combined cargo could support roughly 785 additional missiles; satellite intelligence, including the TEE-01B spy satellite used to monitor US bases across Saudi Arabia, Jordan, Bahrain, Kuwait, Djibouti, Oman, the UAE, and Iraq; AI-enabled analysis of satellite, flight, and shipping data on US assets in the region; BeiDou navigation data that Iran may be using for targeting; and dual-use chipmaking equipment supplied by SMIC to Iran's military-industrial sector [75]. NBC reported on 29 May that the F-15 downed over southwestern Iran in April was probably hit by a Chinese-made shoulder-launched missile [75].

In late July 2026, Reuters reported that China was preparing to send Iran finished, advanced munitions — QW-12 and FN-16 MANPADS, comparable to the Stinger. Iran is expected to receive within weeks a first shipment out of up to 400 Chinese-made shoulder-fired air defense missile launchers, according to three sources familiar with the deal [75].

O'Brien frames this as a categorical shift from dual-use components to finished advanced weapons. "If they are about to transfer fully built, advanced weapons, that could be a game changer (and the start of a process which sees much more go to Iran)." These MANPADS seem almost perfectly suited to thwart any Trumpist escalation: if Trump decided to insert US ground troops anywhere in Iran — be it around the Strait, to try and find Iranian nuclear materials, or to seize Kharg Island — the US would have to maintain a regular flow of low-level flights. Troops and supplies would have to be flown in, air patrols mounted to protect those troops, and injured troops evacuated [75]. The weapons could arrive and deploy by September, creating a binary outcome: Trump might either be forced into an immediate escalation before they arrive, or have to admit that he cannot afford to escalate at all [75].

Even as members of his Cabinet have had to admit to Congress that China and Russia are indeed enabling the Iranian war effort, Trump posted on Truth Social that Xi and Putin had told him they would not "partake," and that he trusted them. "I think you know I trust them," he wrote. "I don't think they'd want to have me disappointed" [75].

O'Brien compares the moment to 1861, the first year of the US Civil War, when outside powers were willing to directly threaten the US in ways that China is doing now — and the US had to take it. The difference: then, the US recovered and was still on an upward trajectory. "It definitely is not on an upward trajectory now" [75]. For O'Brien, this marks the US's most vulnerable moment against external powers since the Civil War, situated within the broader frame of [great-power competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html) [75].

## The Post-War Strategic Landscape

The war left behind a strategic landscape that no party had designed and none could fully control. Its shape runs along a line from Washington's deliberate posture through Tehran's internal paradox, the military constraints binding both sides, the current operational dynamics, and the regional defense architecture now emerging from the wreckage.

### The Offloading Doctrine and Post-War Architecture

Bokhari reads the United States as executing a deliberate structural transition from Middle East hegemon to "offshore actor with its finger on the scales" — a shift that predates the war [81]. [Kamran Bokhari]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/kamran-bokhari.html) frames this as a geostrategic doctrine the Trump administration undertook well before the first strikes, and one whose endpoint is categorical: there is no returning to an era in which Washington is the region's principal security guarantor. Hinote corroborates the motivation — the US wants to be "done with the Middle East" to execute its pivot to Asia and shed expensive basing infrastructure. The theory of success is to put the Iranian regime into "some sort of state where we don't have to go back anytime soon" [82].

Yet Hinote diverges on whether the execution matches the theory. US forces are accomplishing their military objectives: the Iranian Navy, Air Force, air defenses, and missile and drone production capabilities are being destroyed. But the conversion of tactical success into strategic outcomes is, in Hinote's judgment, "the weakness in this entire operation" [82] — a gap that resonates with broader critiques of [Endless Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/endless-warfare.html) and [Escalation Theory Critique]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/escalation-theory-critique.html). The architecture being built to replace US hegemony is a regional core of [Turkey]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/turkey.html), [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html), [Pakistan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/pakistan.html), and [Egypt]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/egypt.html), supported by [Qatar]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/qatar.html), the [UAE]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/uae.html), [Jordan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/jordan.html), and [Azerbaijan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/azerbaijan.html). The defining challenge is aligning these states and integrating them into a workable relationship with [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html). The Middle East is a "blank canvas": regional integration mechanisms must be constructed from scratch [81]. A "fundamental psychological shift" has accompanied this architectural challenge — Iran attacking Arab states is "the new normal," and many Arab governments view Iran as a more fundamental strategic threat than Israeli policies toward the Palestinian territories, Lebanon, and Syria [81].

### Proxy Network Collapse and Regime Resilience

If Washington's posture is a deliberate withdrawal, Tehran's is an unintended collapse. Iran's regional position collapsed even as the regime itself survived — a paradox that defines the post-war landscape. [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html)'s 2024 campaigns degraded Hamas and Hezbollah as military forces, and the resulting vacuum allowed Turkey to shape a new Sunni- and Kurdish-aligned political order in Syria [81]. Iran's once-rock-solid position in [Iraq]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iraq.html) is increasingly vulnerable as Baghdad and Washington curb pro-Iranian Shiite militias and strengthen state control over armed groups [81]. Hinote calls the US-Israel operation "maybe the most successful decapitation operation in history" — yet there was no strategic paralysis and no internal uprising, despite [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html)'s public call for the Iranian people to "take over your government" and the IRGC to "lay down your weapons" [82]. Bokhari assesses near-term survival as near-certain so long as the US declines ground intervention and no viable domestic insurgent alternative exists — but over time, the war, the death of senior leadership, and the devolution within the IRGC will force Tehran to rethink its position [81]. The tension between these two facts — regional position collapsing while the regime itself survives — is not a contradiction but the war's central strategic paradox [82].

### The Geographic and Doctrinal Intractability

That paradox runs into a wall of military constraints. The war's intractability has three interlocking facets — geography that offers no great options for reopening Hormuz, a last-2% problem that no military solution can close, and US-Israeli theories of victory that may work against each other [82]. [James Slife]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/james-slife.html) frames the Strait of Hormuz as a problem military planners have struggled with for years: Iran controls three sides of a narrow, shallow, crowded waterway. "If there was a great option for how air power could unblock the Strait of Hormuz, it would probably be unblocked at this point" — but "geography just doesn't favor us in this regard" [82]. Hinote identifies the structural dilemma: the US joint force can prevent the majority of attacks on shipping — even into the 98% range — but "that last 2%" is what the insurance industry cannot absorb, because Iran's mountainous shores provide protective cover for intermittent attacks [82] — a dynamic at the core of [Insurance and Trade Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/insurance-and-trade-warfare.html).

Slife notes that the US and Israel pursued "very different target sets which may tell us something about what our respective national aims are" [82]. Hinote articulates the Israeli theory: Israel believes the Iranian regime will always be hostile and must go back repeatedly to re-strike regime targets — a strategy of "mowing the grass." The US theory is a coercion campaign demanding the strait open and Iran give up its nuclear aspirations [82]. The two aims "not only were they not necessarily totally aligned, they may have in certain circumstances worked against each other." The intractability stems from the disparity between what the US is trying to achieve militarily and what those military means can actually produce politically [82].

### Service-Reliability Drag and the F-15E Rescue

The operational tempo has imposed its own delayed costs. Heavy deployments have exceeded what the Air Force and Navy can routinely sustain, producing a delayed cumulative effect on readiness. The pace of operations "has probably exceeded the Air Force and the Navy's ability to maintain levels of readiness going forward" — an impact Slife says we will need to "pay close attention to" over the next three to five years [82]. Hinote highlights the F-15E back-seater rescue as a critical but underreported success that may stand as "one of the best we've ever seen" — comparable in significance to Operation Eagle Claw (1980). The operation required "tremendous integration across multiple types of forces in a very difficult time frame" [82]. Had the pilot been captured and paraded on Iranian television, "any political objectives that we were trying to achieve would be all the more difficult to achieve" — the rescue preserved not just a pilot but the political conditions for the campaign [82].

### The Operational Pause and Its Hidden Costs

The current state is not a ceasefire but what [Norman Roule]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/norman-roule.html) terms an "operational pause": a "period of coercive restraint" in which Iran continues attacks on ships while the US restrains its response [83]. The US is not approaching a significant core agreement. The diplomacy exhibits a consistent pattern: the US announces negotiations; Iran denies them; indirect negotiations follow through regional parties [83]. The Iran-Oman mechanism being discussed is likely overstated. If tolls are charged, a cascade of unresolved questions follows: what happens to a shipper who refuses to pay? How does it interact with US sanctions? Are American ships allowed to pay Iran? [83]. If Iran acquires formal control over the strait, money paid by shippers would "inevitably go to its missile program and proxies," giving Tehran billions in revenue and "a permanent foot on the throat of the international community" — with direct implications for [Kuwait]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/kuwait.html)'s food security and the broader [Hormuz Toll System]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/hormuz-toll-system.html) [83]. The idea that the US can "build on trust" with an IRGC government "has no evidence in reality" — though that does not mean it may not happen [83].

Iran's cyber activities have continued unabated since the conflict began, fitting with the past pattern of Iranian cyber activity against SCADA and other systems in the United States [83]. Roule argues the appropriate response is an appropriate proportional response "just done differently" — congruent with multiple administrations' views and statements as to how they will treat Iranian aggression [83].

### Gulf State Defense and Moderation

Roule assesses Gulf States' defense as "very, very successful" — the product of years of engagement with the US and the American private sector [83]. [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html) joined US strikes on Iraq — a first — demonstrating the quality of Saudi Air Forces and the closeness of military partnership [83]. The Gulf States talked [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) out of imminent escalation; if they face further strikes, they will demand a voice in any major attack on Iran. "It is their neighborhood. They know their region better than we do. We should respect that." [83].

## Saudi Nuclear Deal: Iran War Context and Proliferation Risk

The US-Saudi civilian nuclear agreement moves along a single axis: from the war that created its urgency, through the terms that weaken non-proliferation precedent, to the proliferation cascade those terms unlock, and finally to Iran's continued nuclear pursuit — which, combined with the Saudi track, makes a multi-nuclear Middle East markedly more probable. [Guido Steinberg]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/guido-steinberg.html) is the sole analyst carrying this assessment (see [Nuclear Proliferation and Arms Control]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/nuclear-proliferation-and-arms-control.html)).

The agreement was concluded during the ongoing fighting in the Persian Gulf. Iran's military responded to the latest US strike waves by firing rockets and drones at Saudi Arabia and other Arab Gulf states — a pattern maintained since the war's beginning in late February. The timing was not incidental: the war, the Strait of Hormuz closure, and Iranian bombardment have underscored Saudi vulnerability, giving the nuclear track its strategic motivation [84]. Despite deep dissatisfaction in Riyadh with the poorly planned US war effort, MBS and his circle see no alternative to the alliance with Washington [84].

Two details of the agreement are "wegweisend" (path-breaking): Saudi Arabia is to be allowed to enrich uranium domestically, and inspections of the program are to be less stringent than those under the neighboring UAE's 2009 agreement with the United States [84]. That 2009 UAE accord included a renunciation of domestic enrichment and accession to the Additional Protocol of the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty, which provides for especially rigorous inspections. The UAE agreement set standards ("setzte Maßstäbe") whose binding force could now erode against the US-Saudi accord [84]. Abu Dhabi will likely seek the same conditions for its own program retroactively; Turkey, as Riyadh's rival for leadership positions in the Middle East, could be tempted to respond to Saudi efforts with its own nuclear program; and Iran will point to Saudi conditions in future negotiations, insisting on its own right to enrichment at least as persistently as before [84]. The result is that a nuclear-power Saudi Arabia ("Atommacht Saudi-Arabien") has become, at minimum, a possibility [84].

Iran's new, heavily militarized leadership will likely do everything to deter opponents such as the US and Israel more effectively in the future. That it now holds the Strait of Hormuz as a new weapon will likely not dissuade it from a military nuclear program it has pursued since 1987 [84]. The combination of Iran's continued nuclear pursuit and the US-Saudi nuclear agreement makes a Middle East with three, four, or five nuclear powers very much more probable than before [84].

## Contested: Is US Iran strategy a deliberate offloading doctrine or adrift improvisation?

[Kamran Bokhari]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/kamran-bokhari.html) reads US strategy as a coherent, deliberate, and irreversible transition from hegemon to offshore balancer — the Trump administration's geostrategic doctrine of offloading posture predated the Iran war, and US assets will not return to prior posture. [81]

[S. Clinton Hinote]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/s-clinton-hinote.html) reads the same phenomena as effectively adrift — US forces are accomplishing military objectives but the conversion to strategic success is the weakness of the entire operation. The outcome "would not have been in the US theory of success" and reflects a branch plan "not going so well." [82]

The split is methodological, not evidential — Bokhari reads structural doctrine (grand strategy as structural inevitability); Hinote reads operational outcomes (tactical success without strategic conversion). Neither disputes the other's facts.

**Tiebreaker:** whether the post-ceasefire architecture (Oman mechanism, Gulf state custody transfer) coheres into a durable doctrine or dissolves into ad hoc management through Q1 2027.
**Status:** open fork.

## US Strategic Defeat: O'Brien's Assessment

O'Brien assesses that the United States has already lost the war regardless of any eventual agreement text. The strategic results are irreversible: the Iranian regime was not overthrown and has arguably been strengthened, US alliances have weakened, US forces have withdrawn from key facilities, and many damaged bases may never be repaired [94]. The Carter Doctrine — the 1980 commitment to use military force to keep the Persian Gulf open — has been demolished by the paradox that US military force itself caused another power to close the Gulf [94]. Advanced missile interceptor stockpiles have been depleted by approximately 80%, with ATACMS and PrSM long-range ground-launched missiles "virtually all" expended [94][95]. Rebuilding these stockpiles will take years, possibly more than a decade [94].

The US is already withdrawing forces from the Gulf. The *Wall Street Journal* reported precipitous pullouts from Kuwait — the country the US liberated in 1991 and used as a major regional base — because American forces cannot be protected against even cheap Iranian strike capabilities [94]. Forces withdrawn from the Gulf have been partially redeployed to Jordan, which has in turn drawn regular Iranian attacks, making Jordanians question the US role [94]. Saudi Arabia is openly "hedging" — losing confidence in the US as ultimate security guarantor and recognizing that Washington cannot protect it from Iran or even help fight the Houthis [94].

The Hormuz negotiation terms crystallize the strategic defeat. Under discussion is an Iran-Oman mechanism: inbound ships from the Indian Ocean would route through Iranian territorial waters, while outbound vessels would transit near Oman's coast. Rather than formal tolls, "service fees" for environmental response, security, and labor costs would be split equally between Iran and Oman — with the US further marginalized from the governance structure [94]. If the US concedes on Hormuz, the nuclear question becomes academic: with military stocks so low, Washington will have little or no leverage remaining [94].

By mid-August, the war had lost all strategic purpose for the US [95]. Trump appeared willing to extend the blockade through the November midterms — a shift from earlier assessments — because Iran was giving him nothing with which to claim victory, and he preferred continued war over accepting visible defeat [95]. The White House narrative pivoted to minimizing the disruption: Treasury Secretary Bessent claimed 8-9 million barrels per day were getting through, while the actual data showed fewer than 10 tanker transits per day compared to the pre-war 130-140 [95]. Oil markets had internalized months of promises that a deal was imminent, keeping prices below what the physical disruption warranted — but if markets concluded Trump would truly maintain the blockade through the election, prices would adjust sharply [95].

The state of the US military is, in O'Brien's assessment, the worst since 1945. The USS *Abraham Lincoln* has been at sea for over 265 days on a deployment planned to end in May, with reports of a mental health crisis including sailors attempting to go overboard, and Navy representatives compelled to meet with distressed crew families [95]. In a training exercise in Germany, Ukrainian drone operators easily spotted and defeated US Army troops from Fort Hood, suffering what the *Wall Street Journal* termed a "drubbing" [95]. The US has spent approximately $9 trillion on defense since 2016 and produced a deeply flawed, deteriorating force [95].

## Iran's Endgame: Waiting Out Trump

Pape identifies Iran's endgame as explicitly waiting out the Trump presidency. Majid Shakeri, an adviser to Iran's Parliament Speaker, stated that Tehran would "accompany [Trump] until his term ends" [96]. Foreign Minister Araghchi confirmed no direct negotiations, limiting communications to an indirect "exchange of messages" [96]. Iran's parliament is advancing legislation — the "Strategic Action for the Security and Sustainable Development of the Strait of Hormuz and the Persian Gulf" — that would exclude vessels linked to the US, Israel, and other hostile states and impose transit fees of 5-7% of cargo value [96]. Tehran is simultaneously reorganizing its military leadership for prolonged confrontation [96]. Iran's objective is not battlefield victory but hegemonic recognition: creating conditions that no future American president can reverse — a new president who fully accepts Iran as the regional hegemon [96].

Pape projects a three-year trajectory if Iran maintains this position through January 2029: persistent risk premiums in global oil prices with repeated spikes and widespread recession fears; front-line Gulf states delaying or abandoning bypass pipeline plans under Iranian pressure, with demands to dismantle US military bases on their territory; and expansion of non-dollar energy and financial settlement with Russia and China, making US financial stability increasingly dependent on decisions in Tehran, Moscow, and Beijing [96]. The cumulative result would make Iran the "4th center of world power" [96].

## Hormuz Legal Framework

[SWP Berlin]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/swp.html) provides the legal framework for the Hormuz conflict. The core legal dispute predates the war: Iran maintains that only innocent passage — not transit passage — applies in the Strait, granting it wider regulatory authority. Iran is not a party to UNCLOS, while Oman is [97]. On February 28, 2026, the same day as the US-Israeli air strikes, the IRGC radioed ships in the region that transit was no longer permitted; on March 2, a senior IRGC commander publicly declared the Strait closed [97]. On April 8, Araghchi announced a partial ceasefire with reopening — but Iran had already begun permitting selected ships to pass on specific routes while charging fees [97]. On April 13, CENTCOM announced the US blockade of all shipping to and from Iranian ports (not a blockade of strait traffic itself) [97]. The June 14-point plan collapsed by late July as Trump declared it failed [97]. Iran established the Persian Gulf Strait Authority (PGSA) to administer the fee system, with reports of approximately $2 million per ship [97]. UKMTO recorded roughly 100 maritime incidents between February 28 and July 31, 2026 [97]. UNSC Resolution 2817 affirmed that blocking transit constitutes a threat to world peace, but efforts to authorize military measures failed due to Chinese and Russian vetoes [97]. Under UNCLOS Article 26, Iran may not charge fees for the mere passage of foreign ships through its territorial sea — making the proposed toll system illegal under international law regardless of which passage regime applies [97].

## Iran's Cyber Posture

[CSIS]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/csis.html) assesses Iran's cyber posture as enabling rather than escalatory. The CyberAv3ngers — an IRGC-linked hacktivist group — have claimed responsibility for attacks on US water infrastructure across at least 12 states and 100 municipalities [98]. Iran's military doctrine prioritizes the information domain as a key battlespace, using cyber operations for cognitive effect: sowing fear and projecting power into the US homeland far beyond Iran's actual military means [98]. Hormuz remains Iran's primary form of leverage and escalation; cyber operations serve a signaling and information-warfare function rather than constituting a strategic turning point [98]. The Iranian regime's political fragmentation and degraded communications infrastructure mean it is unclear whether the cyberattacks were ordered by senior leadership or undertaken by mid-ranking operators — possibly even one faction seeking to undermine another by disrupting fragile negotiations [98].

## Caspian Theater Expansion

The Caspian theater continued to expand in late July. On July 25, 2026, Ukraine attacked an Iranian merchant vessel in the Caspian Sea, extending Ukrainian drone strikes into a basin previously considered comparatively safe [99]. The Caspian became the fastest-growing Russian sea basin in the first half of 2026 and forms part of the International North-South Transport Corridor linking Russia with Iran and Central Asia [99]. The strike signals that shipping security in the Caspian can no longer be assumed, with implications for insurance premiums, delivery timelines, and the broader INSTC architecture [99].

## The Con: US Patriot Leverage as a Russian Asset

O'Brien documents a structural dynamic in which the United States uses its residual military leverage over [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html) to benefit [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html). By continuing to dribble small numbers of Patriot interceptors to Ukraine through European intermediaries, the Trump administration maintains decisive leverage over Ukrainian operational decisions — a control mechanism more valuable to Moscow than a complete cutoff would be, since ending supplies entirely would remove the restraint that currently binds Ukrainian strategy [100].

The mechanism was demonstrated on July 31, when Vice President Vance called Zelensky to demand Ukraine halt its campaign against Russian oil exports through the Black Sea port of Novorossiysk — specifically targeting the CPC terminal through which Kazakh oil flows [100]. Ukraine complied, ceasing strikes on CPC infrastructure and non-Russian-flagged vessels [100]. The "non-Russian" designation is effectively meaningless: Russia's shadow tanker fleet deliberately uses non-Russian flags to evade sanctions, so the US was instructing Ukraine to stop a campaign that had been materially degrading Russian oil revenue [100]. The oil-price justification is unconvincing — if the US truly cared about commodity inflation, it might equally urge Russia to halt its growing campaign against Ukrainian grain shipments from Odesa, which threatens global food supply chains [100]. The episode reveals how Patriot rationing functions as a pro-Russian tool: keeping Ukraine dependent on a trickle of interceptors gives Washington the leverage to shut down Ukrainian operations at Russia's request, while the drip-feed is small enough to avoid giving Ukraine a decisive edge [100].
