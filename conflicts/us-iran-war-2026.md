# Background

The US-Iran War began on February 28, 2026, when the United States initiated military action against Iran. Despite the scale of the intervention, Iran retained control of the Strait of Hormuz throughout the conflict — a chokepoint through which roughly 20 million barrels per day (mbpd) of petroleum liquids flow, representing approximately 20% of global supply. The war resulted in the loss of approximately 10 mbpd of effective daily supply against the Hormuz baseline. Pipeline bypass options proved structurally thin: Saudi and UAE limited capacity was an order of magnitude short of what would be needed to compensate for the disrupted Hormuz throughput.

The conflict drew in multiple actors and exposed structural vulnerabilities in the global energy system, US military posture, and the Western alliance framework. The [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) initiated hostilities as a personal decision by [Donald Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html), while [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) leveraged its geographic control of Hormuz to maintain coercive leverage even under bombardment. [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html) operated as a parallel actor whose interests overlapped with but did not align perfectly with US objectives.

## Pre-War Context

In the weeks before the war, [Geopolitical Futures]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/geopolitical-futures.html) assessed that Iran's regime was "headed for a hard landing." The 47th anniversary of the 1979 revolution exposed elite factionalism, the erosion of the IRGC, and the rise of pragmatists seeking a deal with the Trump administration. The internal crisis created both an opportunity for diplomatic resolution and a risk that military action would be seen as a way to resolve it externally. The war launched before the internal dynamics could play out [1].

# Current Situation

The kinetic phase of the war was formally concluded by the June 17 Versailles Memorandum (MoU), an arrangement that produced an immediate market response: Brent crude fell from approximately $126 to below $73 per barrel. The [FACE Intelligence]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/energy-and-resources.html) Simultaneity reading dropped from S5-S6 to S3-S4, reflecting a reduction in systemic crisis intensity.

Critically, Iran retained effective leverage over who controls, inspects, escorts, and charges for Hormuz passage. The MoU did not resolve the underlying balance of power — it paused the kinetic exchange while leaving Iran's coercive infrastructure intact. Secondary cascades — fertilizer/food, emerging-market debt, and EU gas storage deficits — continue propagating on 6–12 month lags; see [Global System Rupture]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-rupture.html) for the cascade framework. Post-MoU escalation events (IRGC drone attacks on commercial vessels, US reprisal strikes, attacks on Bahrain facilities) confirmed the fragility from the outset. As [Robert Pape]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/robert-pape.html) observed, "The Iran War ended without resolving the underlying balance of power." See [Iran Ceasefire MoU]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/iran-ceasefire-mou.html) for details on the diplomatic instrument.

As of early August 2026, the conflict has entered what [Norman T. Roule]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/norman-roule.html) terms an "operational pause" — not a ceasefire but a "period of coercive restraint" in which [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) continues attacks on ships while the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) restrains its response to give diplomacy a chance. The Iran-Oman mechanism under discussion raises unresolved questions about refusenik shippers, sanctions interaction, permitted US vessels, payment channels, and Iran's parliament-driven ban on "enemy countries" transiting the Gulf. Meanwhile, a broader post-war strategic transition is underway: [Kamran Bokhari]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/kamran-bokhari.html) reads US strategy as a deliberate, irreversible offloading of security responsibilities to regional partners, while [S. Clinton Hinote]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/s-clinton-hinote.html) reads the same phenomena as strategically adrift — tactical success without strategic conversion. The [US-Saudi civilian nuclear agreement]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/nuclear-proliferation-and-arms-control.html), concluded during the ongoing Gulf fighting, adds a proliferation dimension to the post-war architecture [81][82][83][84].

# Analysis

## Strategic Assessment: Why the US Lost

The central analytical question of the war is how a superpower with overwhelming conventional superiority failed to compel outcomes from a geographically entrenched adversary with a fraction of its military budget. Multiple analysts converge on a shared diagnosis: the United States fought the wrong war — bombing the wrong targets, measuring success against the wrong metrics, and pursuing political objectives disconnected from military reality.

[Phillips O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) offers the most scathing assessment. He argues the United States bombed girls' schools, failed to protect its own facilities, and achieved no meaningful strategic objectives. He dismisses the sinking of the Iranian Navy as a "derisory achievement" given that those vessels were not the source of Iran's coercive power — control of Hormuz was exercised through geography, anti-ship missile threat, and drone swarms, not surface combatants [2]. O'Brien catalogued US aircraft losses at 17 advanced aircraft (fighters, AWACS, special forces helicopters), billions in equipment damage, bases hit extensively, and munitions burned through decades of production in six weeks. A CSIS report confirmed munitions depletion. The Trump administration's corruption has "percolated to the military," degrading institutional competence [3]. An earlier Atlantic article by O'Brien identified the first signs of US military "rot": Iranian drones penetrating US defenses (6 soldiers killed in Kuwait), F-15E friendly-fire shootdowns, the destruction of a Fifth Fleet radar in Bahrain by a $30K drone, and the erosion of alliances under Trump — dynamics he compared to Roman imperial decline [4].

O'Brien's diagnosis extends to the cultural and institutional level. He identifies a rhetorical dimension of the July 20 strikes that illustrates the broader dysfunction: [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) declared the strikes were conducted "in honor of" three servicemen killed in action — a formulation that substitutes faux-testimonial sentiment for strategic logic. The move is characteristic of an administration that wraps military violence in movie-script commemoration rather than articulating operational objectives. O'Brien frames this as continuous with Secretary Hegseth's broader romanticization of sacrifice — testosterone screening, hyper-masculine bravery rhetoric — while the actual determinants of battlefield success (logistics, technology, education, adaptability) are neglected. The "honor" framing is not merely a speech act; it is symptomatic of an institutional culture that celebrates the qualities that do not win wars [57]. See [Revolution in Military Affairs]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/revolution-in-military-affairs.html).

[Aurelien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/aurelien.html) classifies the war as a classic case of "surprise-at-failure" — the most common type of strategic surprise. The outbreak was not surprising: US leaders had threatened Iran for a decade, the methods were predictable, and Israel's participation was expected. The surprise came because the *overall strategic situation* was read wrongly [21]. The dynamic Aurelien identifies is "backwards logic from desired outcome to necessary false assumptions": since a ground invasion was infeasible, all faith had to be placed in an air campaign — but the campaign could only succeed *if* the Iranian state was very fragile, *and* if main state targets could be reliably hit, *and* if the Iranian people would rise up once the state was weakened. These assumptions *had* to be true for the objective to be viable, and therefore the assumptions were treated as true. The US appreciation of Iran's conventional weakness was broadly correct, but Iran's *capability* (asymmetric, geographic, drone-based) was not properly understood, and the *objectives* of the Iranian state (survival and retaliation) were wrongly evaluated. Unlike the Americans, the Iranians were not surprised — they had expected the attack and developed capabilities to pursue their objective [21]. Aurelien places this pattern alongside Operation Barbarossa (1941), the Iraq invasion (2003), and the Falklands (1982) as cases where the wished-for political outcome generated a chain of necessary false assumptions about military effectiveness [21].

Aurelien extends this critique to the Western strategic framework itself, arguing the West cannot understand the Iran conflict because its strategic concepts are outdated and its ruling class lacks military literacy. The post-1945 legal framework created a grey area where wars are no longer "declared," yet politicians and pundits continue using century-old vocabulary — "acts of war," "belligerents," *casus belli* — that no longer applies. This conceptual confusion compounds the practical inability to assess what is happening on the ground [54]. The "escort" paradigm for [Hormuz]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/hormuz-toll-system.html) epitomizes doctrinal obsolescence: the concept derives from World War-era anti-submarine warfare, when grouping merchant vessels with escorting warships reduced losses from U-boats. Today's threat is not individual submarines but swarms of drones and missiles that cannot be defended against at scale. Escorts provide more targets, not protection. The purpose of attack is not necessarily to sink ships but to discourage sailing — a political and economic effect, not a kinetic one [54]. Aurelien's assessment is stark: [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) holds the initiative and the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) cannot win. Western failure resulted from arrogance, inherited military folklore, and a simple disinclination to study Iranian military capability. The prospect of a renewed conflict with Iran, as some US pundits already map out, is not possible. Future evolution of the crisis depends primarily on Iran. The most likely outcome is prolonged low-level tension that suits Iran and strains Western cohesion — and it will not be the West that decides when it stops [54].

O'Brien argues that Trump's personal decision to attack Iran was not in the national interest and was made without political pressure to do so — a finding that directly challenges the assumptions of realism. Wars are choices of individual leaders, not state actors pursuing national interest [2]. By May 2026, O'Brien reports Trump was "desperate to exit" the war but unable to, with no good military options. The emerging Iran deal was worse than the JCPOA. Trump called on Arab states to join the Abraham Accords to appease Netanyahu and GOP hardliners, while [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) was backing Iran and treating [Taiwan]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/taiwan-standoff.html) as a "chip" in the broader negotiation [19]. Pape reinforces this by noting that states gaining leverage rarely hurry to end wars. Once Iran recognized it held the dominant position, it had no incentive to concede quickly [5].

On the home front, O'Brien observes that there was no rally-round-the-flag effect during the war. US society remains locked in a partisan death match: one side does whatever Trump wants, while the other opposed the war from the start. The old patriotic ideas that would have generated national cohesion in a wartime setting are gone [2]. The gap between stated objectives and observable outcomes has introduced uncertainty about the credibility and predictability of US policy — shaping how both partners and adversaries will assess future American commitments. The fracturing of Gulf cohesion and the economic volatility generated by the strait's disruption are consequences that will define the strategic environment long after any agreement is reached [28].

The war can be understood as a manifestation of [Endless Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/endless-warfare.html) — the persistent, below-threshold conflict that adversaries wage against the United States. Iran's network of proxies and surrogates, built over decades, allows Tehran to project power and coerce neighbors at relatively low cost. The war demonstrated that when adversaries practice Endless Warfare, conventional military superiority alone cannot compel outcomes — the adversary's theory of victory may be narrower (survival rather than conquest) and therefore more resilient [29].

## Iran's Theory of Victory

A Cipher Brief assessment underscores the degree to which the initiative has shifted entirely to Tehran. Trump himself has admitted the United States is in "full-blown reaction-mode," effectively granting Iran control over the tempo of both military operations and peace negotiations. The question is whether Iran can continue forcing Washington to react rather than act — and for now, that answer appears to be yes. The current US dynamic is emotionally driven and self-defeating, with no coherent strategic framework connecting military action to political outcomes [68].

The outcome of the war aligns with Iran's strategy all along — not a failure of execution by the US and Israel but a successful implementation of a twenty-year campaign plan. Pnina Shuker and Andrew Milburn (War on the Rocks) document how Iran fought a different war than the one it was offered: rather than contesting air superiority symmetrically, it moved the contest onto terrain its adversaries were not equipped to contest — maritime coercion, economic disruption, information dominance, and political will [28].

The doctrine behind this has a name: mosaic defense, formalized in 2005 by the IRGC's Center for Strategy under Mohammad Ali Jafari and the ideologue Hassan Abbasi — a decentralized command structure designed to survive exactly the kind of decapitation strikes that opened this war. Iranian Foreign Minister Araghchi stated: "We've had two decades to study defeats of the U.S. military to our immediate east and west. We've incorporated lessons accordingly." Iran's theory of victory was simple: survive long enough to impose costs by striking the Gulf States and playing the Hormuz card [28]. Iran had studied the force structure it faced and designed its counter-campaign accordingly — targeting not strike aircraft but the tankers, radar networks, communications nodes, and command platforms that sustained them. The cost exchange told its own story: Iran's drones and missiles cost thousands of dollars apiece; the interceptors fired to stop them ran into millions [28].

Economically, Tehran spent five years building a sanctions-proof economy — its tanker fleet grew from roughly 70 vessels in 2020 to nearly 550 by 2025, sustained by Chinese refineries that alone accounted for close to 90 percent of Iran's foreign oil sales. Iran's kinetic targeting of Gulf states served a wedge strategy: demonstrating that supporting the US campaign has a price. When Iran struck the UAE's oil terminal, Kuwait and Saudi Arabia closed their airspace to US military aircraft. [Project Freedom]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/project-freedom-collapse.html) — Trump's tanker escort plan — collapsed when Saudi Arabia refused basing, citing lack of confidence in US strategy coherence [28].

Pape frames the dynamic as a shift in Iran's negotiating posture: Iran moved from negotiating to stop losing to negotiating to win more — a transition enabled by the realization that the US had no viable path to compelling Iranian compliance on Hormuz [5]. He introduces the concept of "controlled instability" as Iran's post-war strategic doctrine: persistent low-level disruptions — drone incidents, insurance volatility, shipping harassment — that sustain a geopolitical risk premium on oil without requiring total Hormuz closure. This allows Iran to monetize its chokepoint leverage continuously rather than wielding it as a binary weapon. If oil stays above $90, Iran's fiscal position strengthens while Western economies absorb the inflationary cost [16]. Two theories of victory clashed: the US and Israel wanted surrender and Iran's collapse; Iran wanted survival. Tehran's narrower objective gave it the capacity to keep imposing costs and a negotiating position strong enough to turn resilience into political leverage. The deal's own sequence settles the question: before Iran has to resolve a single hard issue — the nuclear stockpile, the missile program, or its regional proxies — the United States lifts the naval blockade, withdraws forces, issues sanctions waivers, and makes frozen assets available. "Pay first, verify later is not a strategy. It is leverage leaving the room" [28].

## The Energy War: Operations and Market Dynamics

The existential asymmetry between the two combatants is a structural driver of the war's logic. For the IRGC and possibly for many Iranians, this is an existential war — regime survival and national sovereignty are at stake. For the United States, save for the nuclear program, it is not. This asymmetry means Iran can absorb far higher costs than the Americans and maintain political will long after domestic support for the war has eroded in the US [67]. The pattern is historically consistent: in Vietnam, Iraq, and Afghanistan, none of the wars were existential for the United States, yet each was treated as existential by the adversary. As the wars dragged on, American domestic support waned, and the adversary maintained resistance and imposed costs until US political will eroded. This analogy frames the most likely path to how the US-Iran war ends — not through military decision but through the exhaustion of American political will [67].

The Strait of Hormuz itself will not "return to normal" without either a deal that explicitly ends Iranian de facto control or a US-led military operation that forcibly opens it. Iran can maintain its stranglehold as long as it is willing to attack, harass, and threaten shipping. Protraction serves Iran's interests by normalizing its de facto control — every month the current state persists, Tehran's authority over the chokepoint becomes more entrenched. Iran is likely stalling negotiations deliberately, using the diplomatic process to buy time while consolidating its position [66].

The energy dimension was the war's primary battlefield. Iran never needed to achieve sea control in the Strait of Hormuz; it needed only to make transit uninsurable and unprofitable. Within days, crude oil exports fell 95% and LNG 99% — the largest supply disruption in the history of the oil market — achieved with technologically unsophisticated means: sea mines, short-range missiles, swarming fast boats, and enough demonstrated resolve to convince insurers, shipowners, and energy markets that transit was not worth the risk [38].

[The Honest Sorcerer]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/the-honest-sorcerer.html) provides the most detailed week-by-week tracking of the cascade:

- **Week 1 (early March)**: The Strait shutdown reduced tanker traffic from 40/day to 1. The diesel/jet fuel crisis began. LNG disruption took [Qatar]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/qatar.html)'s 1/5 of global capacity offline. [Egypt]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html)'s existential crisis began as Suez transit collapsed. European gas storage dropped below seasonal norms [7].
- **Week 2 (mid-March)**: The 16M barrel/day shortfall (crude + refined) became visible. Pipeline diversion to Yanbu was insufficient. Iran continued exports to China. The Bab-el-Mandeb chokepoint was affected by Houthi activity. Operation Prosperity Guardian's failure was confirmed. Insurance withdrawal from Gulf shipping [8].
- **Week 4-5 (early April)**: 500m barrels lost cumulatively. Iran's 80% missile hit rate against US bases. The US running out of munitions. Iran proposed a $2M toll per tanker transit (paid in rials). Trump threatened to escalate to wholesale destruction [9].
- **Week 6 (mid-April)**: The US imposed a [naval blockade]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/us-naval-blockade-iran.html) on Iranian ports — a counter-blockade. Secondary sanctions on Chinese banks. Iran threatened to block the Red Sea. The US struck the South Pars gas field (shared with Qatar). The Pentagon sought help from automakers for military production [10].
- **Week 8 (late April)**: The "polycrisis" framing crystallized. The damage to supply chains of energy, fertilizer, food, and industrial inputs was "already done." A super El Nino threatened concurrent crop failures [11].
- **Week 10 (mid-May)**: Cumulative 1B+ barrels lost. 14.4 mb/d shut in. OECD inventories hitting operational minimums. SPR releases unsustainable. Iran retained 70% of its missile stockpile [12].

[Endtropy]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/endtropy.html) draws a structural parallel between the US-Iran counter-blockade dynamics and Napoleonic warfare. Napoleon's Continental System — the attempt to blockade British trade — generated adaptive responses (smuggling, redirecting trade through neutral ports, financial workarounds) that ultimately undermined the blockade's effectiveness. The US counter-blockade of Iranian ports produced analogous responses: Iran redirected oil exports through non-dollar channels, Chinese banks developed alternative settlement systems, and the [monetary system transition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/monetary-system-transition.html) accelerated [13]. The counter-blockade revealed the war's true objective: controlling oil flow to [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html), not uranium enrichment. By blockading Iranian ports while imposing secondary sanctions on Chinese banks, the US attempted to sever the energy pipeline sustaining the DragonBear axis [10][13].

Why didn't oil hit $150 despite three-plus months of Hormuz disruption? [Velina Tchakarova]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/velina-tchakarova.html) identifies three buffers absorbing the shock: the US (record exports + 66M SPR drawdown, stocks at 349M approaching 1983 lows), China (crude imports collapsed to 8-year low of 7.8 mb/d, 29% YoY decline — demand destruction), and Russia (forced to cut exports as Ukrainian strikes cripple refineries). The calm is "engineered, temporary, and depleting" — each buffer is a one-time measure, not a structural fix [14][15]. See [SPR Depletion Crisis]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/spr-depletion-crisis.html).

Tchakarova's follow-up analysis dismantles the market narrative that the oil price collapse signals a Hormuz recovery. Brent fell from a wartime peak above $180 to around $72, with the forward curves moving into contango. But the physical data tell a different story: Persian Gulf production remains roughly 45% below its pre-war level, regional exports sit around 25% below February levels, and shipping companies remain reluctant to send vessels back through Hormuz given unresolved insurance costs and residual mine risk [22]. The price decline was driven by four distinct offsets working simultaneously: physical rerouting (Saudi Arabia through its East-West pipeline, UAE through its bypass route, Iran itself exporting more oil via shadow fleets), coordinated reserve releases (more than 300 million barrels — the largest emergency drawdown in history), China demand destruction (crude imports fell roughly 40%, close to five million barrels a day), and broader demand erosion (global oil demand contracted an estimated 5.5 million barrels a day in Q2 2026) [22]. Full restoration of export capacity is expected to take many months, with Qatar's damaged LNG facilities potentially requiring years. Shipping costs in waters south of Iran have climbed to roughly 5.5 times pre-war levels, and tanker charter rates have surged to nearly nine times pre-war norms [22].

Scenario modeling built around this evidence assigns the following probability weights [22]: fragile equilibrium (50%, oil $70–85 while the memorandum holds despite recurring friction); renewed rupture (30%, a serious incident sends prices back toward $100+); and durable stabilization (20%, a lasting settlement allows prices to settle near pre-war levels). The price collapse is best read as evidence that global demand management and reserve coordination can now absorb shocks that would have been unmanageable a decade ago — but Iran's retained leverage over Hormuz means the underlying rupture has not closed, only been priced as if it has.

After the ceasefire collapse in July, [Paul Krugman]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/paul-krugman.html) argued the binding constraint shifted from crude supply to refining capacity. Roughly 10% of world refining capacity is offline, primarily from Ukraine's drone campaign against Russian refineries in the parallel [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html). The effective consumer price of oil (weighted gasoline + diesel) rose approximately $75/barrel during the war, while crude rose only $45 — high consumer prices kept demand depressed even when crude prices nearly normalized. A Hormuz reopening would add crude but not usable fuel [30]. The Honest Sorcerer extends this with physical supply chain data: nearly 3 million bpd of Middle East refining capacity shut down; refineries globally cut back by 9 million bpd (more than 10% of global capacity). China's refinery runs crashed to pandemic lows. Ultra-low sulfur diesel reached $168 per barrel against ~$80 crude. Russian diesel exports collapsed from 800,000 bpd to zero under the Ukrainian drone campaign. The [SPR]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/spr-depletion-crisis.html) drawdown rate fell from ~1.3 million bpd in mid-June to 0.43 million by July 10, trending toward zero by early August. Basing decisions on crude prices alone — in a world stripped of refining capacity — has become a civilizational risk [42].

The renewed blockade in mid-July compounded the economic damage. [Geopolitical Futures]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/geopolitical-futures.html) (Colibasanu) documents that war-risk premiums for vessels transiting the strait rose from approximately 2% to 3–10% of hull value — a 40-fold increase over prewar rates. VLCC (very large crude carrier) daily earnings hit approximately $470,000, a record level reflecting the acute scarcity of willing tonnage. By mid-July, only 4–9 commercial vessels were transiting Hormuz per day, and no LNG tanker had crossed since July 16. Qatari LNG vessels accumulated in the Gulf, unable to reach export markets. The Houthis separately announced a blockade of Red Sea ports, compounding the chokepoint pressure on both major Middle East oil routes simultaneously [53].

The war's most consequential structural impact on energy markets may be the transformation of insurance. War-risk premiums in the Strait of Hormuz surged — in some cases by more than 1,000% — forcing a shift from annual policies to voyage-by-voyage underwriting. Risk, once pooled broadly, is now assessed in granular, transactional terms. As private insurers reduced exposure, governments stepped in with sovereign-backed mechanisms: the US International Development Finance Corporation supported maritime insurance capacity, [India]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/india.html) developed sovereign-backed insurance pools, and major insurers formed new consortia to maintain coverage in high-risk corridors. The emergence of regionalized insurance systems means that the ability to insure trade is becoming a strategic tool. See [Insurance and Trade Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/insurance-and-trade-warfare.html) [27].

## Diplomatic and Regional Realignment

The war produced lasting diplomatic fractures on multiple fronts. O'Brien emphasizes that the United States threw its European allies, Japan, and South Korea under the bus by starting the war without informing them — a decision that inflicted lasting damage on alliance trust and cohesion [2]. Tchakarova reframes the ceasefire not as an American coercive success but as an axis course correction: [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) and [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) green-lit the MoU through the [DragonBear]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-rupture.html) coordination mechanism because uncontrolled escalation risked a Global System Rupture that would damage their own foundations [6]. Aurelien provides the sharpest framing of the diplomatic impasse: the US and Israeli positions are fundamentally unreconcilable with Iran's. These positions have zero overlap — there is nothing to negotiate. Diplomacy is theater, and the nuclear enrichment issue is an excuse, not a real subject of bargaining [11][17].

The war produced a lasting strategic divergence between the United States and Israel. Washington seeks to use military gains to create a diplomatic framework that constrains Iran while stabilizing the region, whereas Israel remains skeptical that an ideologically driven regime can be durably moderated through negotiation. VP Vance publicly rebuked Israeli cabinet members for criticizing U.S. diplomacy, stating that Israel "might not be attacking the only powerful ally that I have anywhere left in the entire world." Washington conducted ceasefire negotiations through Pakistani mediation without Israeli participation, despite Israel bearing much of the war's cost and risk. Israel has shifted operational focus to Lebanon, where Hezbollah represents a more immediate threat, and fears that any Iranian financial relief will rebuild Hezbollah [23]. Joe Kent reveals that Israel's long-term goal for Iran is complete regime change toward a secular, business-friendly government. In the short term, Israel views a chaotic Iran — potentially fracturing into ethnic states — as beneficial, despite the disaster it would cause for the US. The nuclear issue was a pretext used by Israel to draw the US into a war to neutralize Iran, a country Israel couldn't defeat alone [26].

For Israel, the reckoning is stark. Israel was not a party to the talks and, by its own officials' account, had not even seen the text of the agreement it would have to live with. Hizballah gains new protection from Israeli strikes under the deal's terms, and the missile program Israel considers existential goes unaddressed. The Gulf states Israel hoped would drift toward Israel have done the opposite — maintaining and in some cases deepening ties with Tehran. When Netanyahu pressed for strikes in Beirut that risked the deal, Trump reportedly told him he "would be in prison" without American protection. One Israeli diplomat summed up the result as "a glorious failure" [28].

The war accelerated regional diplomatic realignment. [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html) conducted secret negotiations with Iran through [Pakistan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/pakistan.html), with Russian and Chinese support, aiming at a non-aggression pact. [Oman]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/oman.html) co-negotiated a [permanent toll system]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/hormuz-toll-system.html) for Hormuz. These initiatives signal the end of the US monopoly on Middle East security architecture — regional powers are negotiating their own stability with non-Western guarantors [18].

Kent asserts that China is exponentially benefiting from the conflict, positioning itself as a more appealing partner to Middle Eastern states by offering deals instead of regime change. The war erodes US security guarantees and the petrodollar system, demonstrating US limitations to the world and bolstering China's position in [great power competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html), including regarding Taiwan [26]. George Friedman (GPF) argues the most important long-term aspect is how the war will affect US-China bilateral relations. China's abstention from the conflict is twofold: China needs to rehabilitate economic ties to the US — still its largest customer — and is heavily dependent on oil and LNG imports through Hormuz. Beijing criticized the war and called on Washington to end it but otherwise stayed out of it, assigning its ambassador in Pakistan to attend mediation talks in Islamabad. Friedman notes a historical parallel: after World War II, the US helped Germany and Japan evolve economically out of self-interest. A shared interest in reaching an understanding on trade and the muted reaction to the Iran war are important indicators that a US-China agreement on trade is emerging [27].

## Escalation Dynamics: The Trap Deepens

The Versailles Memorandum did not hold. Around July 13, 2026, the ceasefire collapsed — [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) reinstated the blockade; Iran resumed drone and missile strikes on shipping. Iran, emboldened by its success in defying America, demanded sovereignty over the Strait of Hormuz. Trump declared he would collect 20% tolls on all shipping traffic, then shifted to demanding that countries invest in the United States as a condition for passage — both proposals wildly illegal and impossible to enforce. The full tactical timeline and market repricing of the collapse are tracked in [Hormuz Ceasefire Collapse]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/hormuz-ceasefire-collapse-2026.html) [30][31][36].

Pape argues that the ceasefire collapse is not simply another round of strikes but evidence that the conflict has entered a fundamentally new phase — the Escalation Trap is becoming self-reinforcing. Each side is no longer trying merely to impose military costs; each is attempting to reverse what it sees as an unacceptable political defeat. "Both governments are becoming politically invested in objectives that cannot both be achieved at the same time" [32][33].

The war is now driven by two separate, mutually reinforcing engines. **Strategic escalation**: Iran's Revolutionary Guard insists only Tehran will control Hormuz; US Central Command continues preparing to guarantee freedom of navigation. Trump has moved from punishment to reversing the strategic defeat — his goal is no longer to coerce Iran but to undo the perception that the US lost [33]. **Revenge escalation**: The new Supreme Leader's nationally broadcast statement — "We pledge to avenge your pure blood and the blood of all the martyrs... Soon individuals among the freedom-seekers across the world will each carry out part of this divine mission" — has transformed revenge into an independent political objective. The Iranian Intelligence Ministry released an official decree stating vengeance is "a national demand." This is not temporary rhetoric but a structural feature of the conflict [33]. The critical insight: even if one engine slows, the other can continue driving escalation. Most analysts are treating these as separate problems; they are not — they are beginning to interact in ways that fundamentally change the logic of the war [33].

Trump's decision to declare the ceasefire over, reimpose a blockade, and announce American control over Hormuz fundamentally changes the political objectives. He declared the United States the "Guardian of the Strait of Hormuz," said America will "probably run it," and announced plans to charge a 20% fee on cargo — a political commitment that makes backing down significantly harder [34]. Pape identifies two factors driving Trump's escalation despite midterm risks. **The Hoover risk**: Trump signed the MoU to avoid an economic crisis, explicitly saying he did not want to be "Herbert Hoover." But the MoU failed to stop the oil inventory drawdown — the world gained only about 14 days of supply from a couple hundred million barrels released. The SPR salt caverns face a structural risk: at low levels, water eats away the cavern walls causing collapse. "We're getting into new territory" [33]. **MAGA turnout risk**: According to the Reagan Institute's Summer Survey, 51% of self-identified MAGA Republicans favor replacing Iran's government; only 25% prefer a negotiated settlement. If Trump fails to open Hormuz, a significant portion of MAGA will not show up at the polls — losing 5–10% of the voting base means losing many seats and becoming "the lamest of lame ducks" [33].

Iran's political objectives have expanded through three phases: **Survival** (Feb–Mar 2026, preserving the regime after decapitation of senior leadership), **Ambition** (Apr–Jun 2026, using Hormuz leverage to pursue a new regional security order from Lebanon through the Gulf to Yemen), and **Revenge** (Jul 2026–, the Supreme Leader's call to action adding vengeance as a third objective). Pape draws on 25 years of suicide terrorism research: 95% of suicide attacks coerce democratic withdrawal from territory. The Rushdie fatwa (1989) is the closest historical parallel — it produced attacks and deaths for decades. The current call to action has significantly greater strategic implications [33]. The self-reinforcing spiral works as follows: every US bombing campaign kills more Iranians who are then portrayed as martyrs, strengthening the legitimacy of the revenge call. If even one limited revenge attack succeeds — against an American official, business executives, a commercial airliner — public pressure for decisive retaliation would intensify, giving Trump even stronger incentives to authorize larger operations including ground options [33].

The [funeral of Iran's Supreme Leader]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/supreme-leader-funeral-2026.html) produced perhaps the largest public mobilization in the history of the Islamic Republic, with millions filling the streets chanting "Kill Trump" and "Revenge." Pape argues this is not political spectacle but strategic shift: massive nationalist mobilization changes Tehran's bargaining position by increasing the political costs of moderation. The funeral has enabled a new, more ambitious Iranian war aim: weakening and ultimately ending the permanent American military presence in the Gulf. Iran is appealing directly to Gulf monarchies — offering lower transit fees for states that distance themselves from Washington. Pape forecasts that August could become the decisive month: the SPR falling toward its operational floor as hurricane season threatens Gulf Coast production, and Iran possessing growing nationalist confidence, continuing Hormuz influence, and an opponent with diminishing economic room [25].

As the conflict intensified, multiple escalation thresholds were crossed in rapid succession. Pape identifies a discrete threshold crossed on July 15, 2026: Iran's use of heavy anti-ship missiles with up to 1,000-pound warheads against tankers in the Strait of Hormuz — weapons capable of catastrophic, ship-sinking destruction far eclipsing minor drone strikes. Numerous US Navy ships had been dangerously repositioned close to Iran's coast, well within Iran's anti-ship cruise missile range. Pape's assessment is that even 30–40 US casualties would instantly shatter diplomatic options, forcing an uncontrollable, public-fueled demand for payback that could lock both sides into a costly spiral and make Stage Three politically difficult to avoid. From that moment forward, the political logic of the war changes: the imperative becomes responding to American deaths, not just protecting freedom of navigation. Every death — and every successful retaliation — creates new domestic pressure to escalate further, including for ground operations as payback rather than merely to "reopen" Hormuz. Neither Washington nor Tehran fully controls what happens once those casualties occur. The shift is from controlled brinkmanship to high-risk, uncontrolled escalation [46].

On the evening of July 17, US Central Command conducted multiple waves of airstrikes against [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html), targeting key bridges, railway junctures, and power lines connecting key cities, particularly in Hormozgan province and around the major port city of Bandar Abbas. Pape assesses these as classic "shaping" operations in preparation for a ground assault, consistent with the air strikes over all six days in the previous week. The latest strikes disrupt the logistics and mobility of Iranian military forces in the south, while the prior strikes degrade coastal missile storage and launch sites, air defense systems, and surveillance facilities from Chabahar to Bushehr — along hundreds of miles of coastline that complicates the defender's problem in stopping any landing or seizure of specific islands [48]. The shaping operations deepen both sides' Escalation Traps: once the United States begins shaping the battlefield for possible ground operations, stopping becomes politically harder than continuing: having already accepted the risks of sustained escalation, Washington faces growing pressure to demonstrate that those costs will produce tangible strategic gains. Every additional strike raises the political price of restraint while making a larger military commitment appear increasingly necessary to justify what has already been done. Iran's trap deepens in parallel: having survived six days of US attacks while still contesting Hormuz, Tehran faces mounting pressure to demonstrate that American escalation cannot succeed. Standing down after these strikes would risk signaling weakness both domestically and across the region, so the political incentives for Iranian leaders increasingly favor further escalation even as the military and economic costs mount. Pape expects multi-faceted escalation in the near future [48].

The next day, Iran crossed another threshold: instead of concentrating on military bases, Iranian missile and drone strikes hit a vital power and water desalination plant in [Kuwait]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/kuwait.html) — part of the system that produces roughly 90% of the country's drinking water. Unlike past fragments from Iranian strikes that had inadvertently hit desalination plants, this plant was directly targeted. Pape identifies the strike as a categorical escalation: civilian survival infrastructure is becoming a direct battlefield target [49]. The strategic logic runs counter to the conventional expectation that targeting civilians breaks their will. Drawing on his *Bombing to Win* research, Pape's central finding is that when civilians suddenly lose access to life's basic necessities, survival rapidly overwhelms every other concern in the affected areas. Instead of rallying around the flag, people become consumed by the immediate struggle to keep themselves and their families alive — "anger comes after survival." But the Escalation Trap operates in the opposite direction from civilian morale: leaders confronted with collapsing civilian conditions often seek to shift public anger outward, while neighboring states face growing pressure to respond to humanitarian and security spillovers. The result could be a wider regional war whose consequences become increasingly difficult for any side to control. The strike proves the concept for a vulnerability pattern that extends across the Gulf: [Qatar]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/qatar.html) and Bahrain each rely on only three to five desalination plants, while [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html) and the [UAE]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/uae.html) rely on 30 and 70 respectively. See [Geoeconomics of Water]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/geoeconomics-of-water.html) [49].

The operational logic points toward a focused effort on degrading Iran's coastal offensive capabilities rather than escalating strikes on energy and civilian infrastructure. Escalating to mass civilian punishment would alienate the Iranian population and strengthen the regime — the opposite of the intended political effect. The current Memorandum of Understanding must be abandoned and recast on terms that align military means with achievable political objectives, rather than perpetuating an arrangement that preserved Iran's coercive infrastructure while pausing the kinetic exchange [68].

Trump's threatened escalation to mass civilian punishment would, if carried out, produce consequences that Pape analyzes in granular operational and historical detail. Trump's declaration that Iranians "have not suffered enough yet" and his contemplation of a "massive attack, bigger than ever before" signal a potential shift from military-denial targeting to civilian-punishment targeting — bridges and electrical power infrastructure whose principal purpose is not military denial but mass suffering [62]. The analysis distinguishes two target types: destroying transformers and transmission substations can produce widespread blackouts but is often repaired within days or weeks, while destroying the generating units themselves — the massive turbine-generator assemblies inside power stations — produces prolonged outages lasting months or years, since these are custom-engineered, weigh hundreds of tons, and are not kept as spare parts. Either mission is well within current American capabilities; power plants are fixed, above-ground, and impossible to conceal [62]. For Tehran — a city of roughly ten million experiencing July temperatures near 100°F — the consequences of a prolonged blackout would be catastrophic. Water pumps serving 800 million gallons daily would fail within hours. Roughly one-quarter of perishable food would be lost when cold chains collapse; destroying major bridges would magnify food shortages by paralyzing distribution, potentially dropping inbound delivery capacity by 50–90%. The 1995 Chicago heat wave killed 739 people in five days with 2.7 million residents; a one-to-three-month blackout in Tehran would produce casualties on a far greater scale. Hospitals with 25,000 beds and 5,000 dialysis patients would begin rationing lifesaving care as generator fuel, water, and medicines deplete [62]. The historical record is unambiguous: coercive bombing of civilian infrastructure rarely produces rapid political capitulation when governments believe their survival is at stake. The United States Strategic Bombing Survey, after interviewing thousands of German civilians and examining captured Gestapo reports, found that bombing civilian infrastructure lowered morale but did not produce political capitulation — the strongest emotional response was not surrender but anger, and demands that Germany strike back rather than compromise. Thomas Griffith's study of electrical-system targeting across WWII, Korea, Vietnam, and Desert Storm confirmed that such attacks provide limited military utility and rarely achieve intended strategic goals [62]. Iranian Foreign Minister Araghchi stated: "Our defense doctrine is clear: eye for an eye. Any aggression against Iran, including our infrastructure, will compel a powerful and decisive response." Once punishment replaces denial as the primary military strategy, both sides become convinced that more escalation — not less — is necessary [62]. The Escalation Trap logic is self-reinforcing. Every family forced to watch an elderly parent die from heat, every child without clean water, every patient denied dialysis becomes a source of motivation for retaliation. Iran would respond not by matching American power directly but through its asymmetric advantages — commercial shipping, regional proxy forces, cyber operations, and attacks on US military facilities.

The nuclear dimension introduced a new strategic complication. [Israeli]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html) intelligence reported that Iran had moved thousands of uranium-enrichment centrifuges into deeply fortified tunnels beneath Pickaxe Mountain near Natanz, buried 300–450 feet under solid rock — beyond the reach of existing conventional bunker-busting munitions. Satellite imagery showed continued construction, reinforced tunnel entrances, and heavy vehicle activity. Iran denied IAEA access for verification. Trump identified the facility as a potential military target, but it remained untouched [59]. The core analytical insight is that airpower can destroy targets but rarely compels determined regimes to abandon core political objectives. US and Israeli bombing achieved tactical successes throughout the war, but Iran responded by moving enrichment capability deeper underground. Airpower did not solve the nuclear problem — it transformed it. A facility 300–450 feet beneath solid rock cannot be reliably eliminated by conventional bunker-busting alone. Pape frames this as "the Ground Power Dilemma": if Washington concludes Pickaxe Mountain has become Iran's protected nuclear sanctuary, the choice narrows to tolerating a hardened nuclear program or accepting far greater risks of operations beyond an air campaign [59]. The fear-driven escalation logic compounds the trap. Drawing on the 2003 Iraq WMD precedent, Pape argues that leaders escalate based on what they believe adversaries are capable of, not what later proves true. If Washington believes Pickaxe Mountain has made Iran's nuclear program invulnerable, that belief alone creates pressure for broader military action regardless of what inspectors might discover. Uncertainty favors escalation. The political clock linkage intensifies the pressure: if the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) cannot reopen Hormuz before the November midterms, pressure for broader escalation — including attacks on deeply buried nuclear facilities — will grow [59]. See [Escalation Theory Critique]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/escalation-theory-critique.html) [59].

The US military is already pre-positioning for Stage 3 ground operations. Two carrier strike groups moved from the Indian Ocean into the Gulf of Oman — placing 5,000–6,000 personnel within Iranian missile and drone range. Coastal suppression bombing concentrates on surveillance radars, command-and-control networks, anti-ship missile batteries, and drone launch sites along Iran's southeastern coast — the classic preparatory targets for littoral operations. A Marine Expeditionary Unit has been repositioned closer to the Gulf of Oman. Pape identifies two operational plans: **Plan A** — carriers provide air cover for the "Southern Passage" near Oman, protecting ships from Iranian drones and missiles; **Plan B** — if air cover is insufficient, take territory on the coast, specifically Chabahar. "This would be the worst losses we've seen since Vietnam." Any ground war will likely last at least six months [33].

Pape's analysis (July 20, 2026) argues that America is unlikely to reopen the Strait of Hormuz before the November midterm elections. He draws a critical distinction between tactical and strategic victory: tactical success measures military performance — launchers destroyed, attacks declined — while strategic success requires the political objective of restoring normal commercial shipping. The judge of strategic victory is the market itself: shipping companies, insurers, and commodity traders must conclude that transiting Hormuz is routine again. Only 50–100 drones or missiles are needed to swarm ships, and Iran possesses tens of thousands; a single successful strike can reverse months of restored confidence [52]. The suppression problem is structural. Iran spans 1.65 million square kilometers — three times the size of Iraq, four times California — with a population of 92 million and roughly one million personnel. Mobile launchers, anti-ship missiles ranging 80–600+ miles, and Shahed-136 drones with 600–1,500 mile range are dispersed deep into Iran's interior, requiring suppression across roughly 500 miles of waterway. Destroying 80–90% of these assets is insufficient — all must be suppressed continuously. A Shahed-136 drone is approximately the size of an F-150 truck; Iran has an estimated 2.5–3 million comparable vehicles, making visual identification of launch platforms effectively impossible [52][61]. Pape demolishes the prospect of a limited landing: Iran's coastline rises into rugged mountains with concealed firing positions — there is no accessible beachhead. A Marine Expeditionary Unit of roughly 2,200–2,500 Marines controls only a one-by-two-mile area; once a first landing succeeds, the campaign revolves around protecting the growing force ashore rather than reopening Hormuz. Iran does not need to defeat a landing — it needs to make remaining there dangerous enough that the mission expands beyond its original scope [52]. The political clock is Iran's strategic advantage. Iran fights for regime survival; the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) fights for a narrow objective with a domestic deadline. Iran needs only to prevent the reopening of Hormuz before November. If Hormuz remains contested through the election, Congress may become the decisive constraint: by 1974, Congress refused further military assistance to South Vietnam. If Republicans retain Congress, [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) has greater latitude; if Democrats capture either chamber, investigations and funding battles narrow his options. Iran does not need to win militarily — it needs to keep the contest going until America's political clock overtakes its military clock [52].

Pape's framework is stark: there is no third choice between surrender and escalation. "Everybody wants there to be a third choice. That doesn't make a third choice." Either Trump capitulates and accepts Iran as the rising regional hegemon, or he escalates toward Stage 3 ground operations. The war is likely to continue "at least through January" 2027 [33]. He also addresses suggestions of using nuclear weapons: radiation clouds cannot be controlled and would kill large numbers of American troops over time. Lindsey Graham's call for nuclear strikes is "clickbait" — a nuclear detonation in Gaza or the West Bank would kill tens of thousands of Jews, echoing the bombing of Auschwitz dilemma [33].

Lebanon represents a critical pressure point for horizontal escalation. A parallel US-Israel deal in Lebanon undermines the MoU with Iran — the two documents became contradictory. Kent calls US intervention in Lebanon "absurd" and warns that Houthi attacks in the Red Sea targeting Saudi oil would be a major game-changer. The apparent division between VP Vance pushing for the MoU and Secretary Rubio undermining it is a genuine internal conflict, not a "good cop, bad cop" strategy. Kent advises Trump to militarily restrain Israel by cutting significant chunks of aid — providing maximum space for the MoU to work [26]. Pape identifies commercial shipping as the key escalation pathway: Hormuz remains open not because the US forced it open, but because Iran permits it — and Iran can withdraw that permission at any time. Iran's three conditions for sustained passage are: release of $12 billion in frozen assets, an end to Israeli operations in Lebanon, and a reduction of US military presence in the region [5][20]. The Houthi situation in the Red Sea is concurrently escalating — Saudi Arabia has been trying to move 4 million barrels/day through the Red Sea, and the Houthis have shut that down before [33].

Tchakarova warns of a "very bumpy second half of 2026": US-Israel-Iran escalations will continue with no end in sight; Yemen-Saudi Arabia tensions are coming; Türkiye and Syria might also get involved. Russia will face a strong recession scenario and boost its summer offensive in Ukraine. Two needed ceasefires in Ukraine and Iran are the mutually strengthening signal toward preventing the [Global System Rupture]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-rupture.html) scenario [37].

## The Ceasefire Collapse and Renewed Conflict

The Versailles Memorandum was a dead letter almost from the moment it was signed. [Phillips O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) documents the day-by-day military operations that reignited the war in mid-July, framing them as the inevitable product of a "consequence-free world" the US chose to inhabit. The [Versailles Memorandum]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/iran-ceasefire-mou.html) was a sham: Iran gave up nothing, Trump lacked any foundation to declare victory, and when the truth dawned he faced the binary choice between admitting the greatest strategic defeat in US history or restarting the bombing. He chose the latter. Trump declared the ceasefire formally over on July 8 at the [Ankara Summit]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/nato-ankara-summit-2026.html); what that meant became clear over five consecutive days of US strikes [50]:

- **July 11**: CENTCOM struck approximately 140 targets and set terms to end hostilities (open the strait, halt shipping attacks, surrender enriched uranium). The IRGC struck the container ship *GFS Galaxy* in Hormuz and declared the strait closed; a claimed strike on Jordan's Prince Hassan Air Base was disputed.
- **July 12**: US strikes on Qeshm Island, Bandar Abbas, and Hajiabad; first use of one-way attack drones this campaign. Iran fired its widest missile/drone volley yet at UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain, Oman, and Jordan.
- **July 13**: Five-hour US strike wave on Bushehr, Chah Bahar, Jask, Konarak, Abu Musa, Bandar Abbas. IRGC missiles struck two UAE-flagged tankers (*al-Bahiya*, *Mombasa*) in Omani waters — 1 Indian crew killed, several injured; oil price spiked almost 10%.
- **July 14**: Third consecutive night of coastal strikes; Trump reinstated the naval blockade and threatened infrastructure (bridges, power plants) next.
- **July 15**: Seven-hour fourth-night strike wave (command centers, air defense, Bandar Abbas) plus a separate round on Greater Tunb Island; an empty tanker was disabled with Hellfire missiles for ignoring the blockade. Kuwait intercepted Iranian missiles/drones.
- **July 16**: Fifth consecutive day of strikes (Bandar Abbas, Ahvaz, Chabahar); Trump reportedly weighing ground operations and bombing an underground site linked to Iran's nuclear program.

[Heather Cox Richardson]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/heather-cox-richardson.html) documents the next phase of the escalation arc. On July 15, Trump reversed his reimposed blockade within 24 hours — claiming that Gulf allies told him they would rather invest directly in the U.S. than pay tolls — only to convene a Situation Room meeting with his top national security team to discuss "new plans for devastating strikes" against Iran. Those in the room included Vice President Vance, Secretary of State Rubio, Defense Secretary Hegseth, Chairman of the Joint Chiefs General Caine, CIA Director Ratcliffe, and Middle East envoy Witkoff. Before the meeting, Trump told Fox News that after the "hard" strikes that week, "next week, it gets really bad for them because next week comes the power plants. Next week comes the bridges. We're gonna knock out all their power plants. We're gonna knock out all their bridges unless they get to the table and negotiate." Attacks on civilian infrastructure are usually illegal under international law. A former member of Trump's negotiating team told Laura Rozen of *Diplomatic* that the escalation was probably a ploy to kick-start further negotiations — "a very risky and low probability gamble, but nothing else makes sense" [44]. The next day, Reuters reported that Iranian leaders had asked the Houthis they back in Yemen to close the [Bab el-Mandeb]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/red-sea.html) strait — the second of the two main routes for Middle East oil exports, alongside Hormuz — in response to the threatened strikes on Iran's power network. Closure of the second strait would exacerbate energy shortages even as the U.S. [SPR]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/spr-depletion-crisis.html) dropped to its lowest level since 1983. Iran thus activated its geographic second front: rather than bearing the entire escalation cost through Hormuz, Tehran widened the pressure by operationalizing its Red Sea proxy [45]. By July 23, Trump formalized the infrastructure threat on social media: any Iranian attack on shipping would trigger US bombing of "ONE BRIDGE OR POWER PLANT, including those located next to, or in, the Capital City of Tehran." Secretary Rubio escalated the framing: "The president's policy is a head for an eye." Richardson notes that attacking civilian infrastructure for political purposes is a war crime under international law [63].

O'Brien's likelihood table crystallizes the strategic assessment of the renewed campaign: 99% that Iran has made no meaningful concession; 98.5% that [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) and [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) are benefitting significantly (the US blowing through depleted munitions means no new Patriots for Ukraine and a weaker Taiwan deterrent; rising oil prices boost Russian revenue); 90% that oil markets still believe Trump will "TACO" (Trump Always Chickens Out); 80% that ground-troop threats are hot air; 66% that Trump still needs a deal by the midterms; and 99.9% that the US is haemorrhaging allies and partners. The campaign is an extraordinary gamble: restarting the bombing in a weaker strategic position than when operations ended in April, with the midterm elections approaching and 60–66% of Americans opposing the war [50]. The "consequence-free world" framing extends to the assassination-policy hypocrisy: Trump's July 11 tweet threatening to "completely decimate and destroy all areas of Iran" should Iran attempt to assassinate him is juxtaposed by O'Brien against Trump's own celebration of the assassination of Khamenei at the start of the war. The US pursued and legitimized a policy of leader assassination; the same policy now makes Trump a legitimate target for Iran. Undersecretary of State Colby's parallel tweet thread — insisting that a "collective middle powers strategy" is a distraction and that "access to the American DIB is a privilege, not a right" — is read as panic: the administration is realizing that its own Europe-withdrawal policies have produced the strategic autonomy it now pleads with Europeans not to pursue. The Aqaba pipeline crisis illustrates how the renewed conflict widened beyond Hormuz. Pape identifies a new escalation vector in his Breaking Analysis #6 (July 19, 2026): Iran's threat to Aqaba, [Jordan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/jordan.html)'s only coastal city on the northern tip of the Red Sea. The US Embassy in Amman issued an emergency alert advising Americans to avoid Aqaba's airport and seaport due to a specific credible threat, and air raid sirens went off in Jordan warning of incoming Iranian missiles. Aqaba functions as both a tourist destination and a deep-water commercial port — a gateway to desert attractions and, crucially, a potential terminus for the long-delayed Basra-Aqaba pipeline [51]. The threat follows a Washington meeting between [Iraq]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iraq.html)'s prime minister and Jordan's foreign minister to accelerate the Basra-Aqaba pipeline — a 1,600-kilometer, $18 billion project designed to carry 2.25 million barrels per day from southern Iraq to the Red Sea. The Iran war had cut southern Iraqi exports by approximately 70%, exposing Baghdad's dependence on the Strait of Hormuz and making the bypass pipeline a strategic imperative. Jordan gains transit fees and 150,000 barrels per day for its Zarqa refinery. For Iraq, the calculus is about security rather than profit: Aqaba costs roughly $6 per barrel versus 60 cents through the Gulf, but the premium buys insulation from Iranian coercion [51]. Under the Escalation Trap framework, Pape argues that Iran has powerful incentives to strike any infrastructure that could permanently reduce its coercive leverage. A functioning Basra-Aqaba pipeline would undercut Iran's "security resistance belt" — a chain of control stretching from the Mediterranean through the Red Sea to the Gulf — by creating a permanent alternative outlet for Iraqi oil. Projects like Aqaba are increasingly attractive military targets — not for their immediate economic value but because they challenge Iran's strategy of preserving leverage through control of regional energy chokepoints. Striking bypass infrastructure before it becomes operational is cheaper than competing with it after it exists [51].

[Andrew Davidson]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/geopolitical-futures.html) (GPF) frames the renewed fighting as an unequal but ultimately inconclusive contest. Destroying Iranian capabilities is not the same as compelling Tehran to accept US terms. Washington can weaken [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html)'s ability to threaten shipping and prevent it from exercising control over the Strait of Hormuz, but cannot cheaply guarantee normal commercial passage or force Iran to surrender its remaining leverage without accepting a prolonged campaign. If Iran cannot withstand the campaign indefinitely, it can preserve enough uncertainty to deny the US a clean political victory [43]. The two sides are pursuing different forms of exhaustion: Iran is trying to outlast Washington politically even as military losses and economic pressure weaken its position; the US is trying to force Tehran to pause before the costs of regional defense exceed the political value of the campaign. The military balance strongly favors the United States — American forces can identify and destroy Iranian maritime and coastal systems faster than Tehran can replace or employ them — yet Iran's remaining systems are dispersed across a large territory, and commercial confidence depends not on how many Iranian assets are destroyed but on whether another vessel might still be hit. Washington must provide security continuously; Iran needs only intermittent success. A single attack can restore uncertainty after numerous safe transits and keep commercial traffic below normal levels [43].

Iran's margin for victory is shrinking as US strikes degrade the military systems needed to sustain disruption and the [reinstated blockade]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/us-naval-blockade-iran.html) constrains seaborne oil exports that provide Iran with critical foreign currency and state revenue. Overland routes through the North-South corridor can only partially offset these losses. As the margin narrows, limited attacks become less effective because they impose lower costs on Washington than Tehran is absorbing in return. Iran's clearest means of changing the calculation would be to attack Gulf energy infrastructure or expand maritime disruption to the Bab el-Mandeb strait — but either option could unite its adversaries: ending the Gulf states' role as mediators, deepening their military cooperation with Washington, bringing [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html) back into the campaign, and justifying broader strikes on Iranian territory. Iran's strongest remaining escalation option is also the one most likely to unite its adversaries [43]. The pressure is not confined to the waterways and coastlines: the US strike on the Aq Taqeh Khan railway bridge in Aqqala County — on a corridor linking Iran to [Central Asia]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/central-asia.html) and onward to [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) and [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) — demonstrated that pressure would not remain confined to the waterways. These losses are accumulating amid uncertainty over Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei, whose prolonged absence from public view has weakened the regime's ability to project clear authority [43].

Yet time is not entirely on Washington's side either. The US can absorb the costs of the campaign far longer than Iran, but a prolonged operation places growing demands on forces and munitions already committed across several regions. Further Iranian escalation would require the US to defend not only personnel and bases but also ports, shipping routes, and energy infrastructure throughout the Gulf — consuming air and missile defense interceptors, surveillance capacity, and naval assets. If commercial traffic remains disrupted, the economic costs compound the military burden. Because the campaign depends on continued Gulf access and cooperation, prolonged resource expenditure without restored passage or Iranian concessions becomes increasingly difficult to justify. The approaching US midterm elections sharpen the pressure, but the larger constraint is the cost of sustaining regional protection without a clear political result [43].

The likely outcome is a truce rather than a settlement. If neither side escalates, Iran is more likely to seek a ceasefire before the US abandons its core objectives — even if Tehran briefly intensifies attacks to improve its bargaining position. Iran's release of a US citizen amid the renewed exchanges suggests Tehran already has de-escalation in mind. Washington has greater room to adjust the practical terms — easing blockade enforcement, allowing limited Iranian exports, or modifying transit and escort procedures — without abandoning its core demand that Iran stop threatening commercial passage. A pause would likely involve Iran scaling back attacks enough for commercial traffic to recover while preserving its formal claims over Hormuz and presenting the outcome as proof that resistance forced negotiations. Washington would likewise portray any reduction in attacks as evidence that military pressure worked. Neither side would have resolved the underlying dispute, and both would retain incentives to test the balance sometime in the future. Washington can keep winning the strikes, and Iran can keep denying Washington a clean victory, but neither can sustain that contradiction indefinitely [43].

O'Brien documents the continuation of US strikes through eleven consecutive nights as of July 22, 2026 — a campaign with no achievable military objective. Trump is hoping Iran will offer peace terms he can sell: a nuclear concession plus the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz without tolls. No military operation short of full-scale invasion and regime change can produce this outcome. General McCaffrey estimates a full invasion would require 600,000 troops and one year — a commitment that is not happening. Every day of strikes thus depletes US munitions while strengthening Iran's political position, with no theory of how military action produces the desired political result [58]. Iran's "sideways escalation" strategy compounds the dynamic: rather than attacking only US forces, Iran is systematically striking the infrastructure of US-allied Gulf states, making the war politically costly for host governments and demonstrating that the US cannot protect its allies. The timeline from July 12–21 documents this widening pattern: July 12 strikes on Qatar, UAE, Bahrain, Kuwait, Jordan, and Oman (including a Patriot battery and ammo depot in Kuwait and Duqm port in Oman); July 13–14 cluster munition warhead against Bahrain; July 14–15 strikes on Bahrain, Kuwait, and Jordan; July 16–17 strikes on Kuwaiti desalination infrastructure; July 17–18 strike on Jordan's Muwaffaq Salti Air Base (2 US KIA) and a ballistic missile on a US base in Saudi Arabia (first in 3+ months); July 19 strikes on Jordan, Kuwait, Bahrain, a second strike on Kuwaiti desalination, and ballistic missiles on Aqaba near Eilat; July 20 strikes on two oil tankers in Hormuz, US assets in Jordan/Kuwait/Bahrain, and an IRGC-claimed hit on an Amazon data center in Bahrain; July 21 claimed destruction of US radar and air defense in Muharraq and Riffa, Bahrain [58]. ## Regional and Horizontal Escalation

The sideways escalation extended to critical digital infrastructure. Iran struck data centers in the [UAE]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/uae.html) providing Amazon Web Services, causing them to go down for several months at minimum — fires spread through the facilities and the lead time for replacement parts ensured a prolonged outage. The strike demonstrated that Iran's targeting of Gulf infrastructure extends beyond energy and military assets to the cloud computing backbone that sustains both commercial operations and military command-and-control systems [69].

The war's escalation was not confined to the US-Iran bilateral. Within 48 hours of July 16, Yemen's Iran-backed Houthis launched a coordinated barrage of ballistic missiles and drones targeting [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html)'s Abha International Airport in direct retaliation for airstrikes on Sanaa. Pape frames the assault as a classic case of *horizontal escalation* that widens the war by opening a second maritime front at the [Bab el-Mandeb]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/red-sea.html) gateway. Until then, the war had centered on the Strait of Hormuz; opening a second front transforms the crisis from a regional confrontation into a broader contest over the security of global energy flows. The move expands the political stakes for every major actor while shrinking their room to compromise: leaving two strategic chokepoints under threat would appear politically unacceptable, forcing Washington, Riyadh, and their allies toward military rather than diplomatic responses. [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) gains additional leverage without directly committing its own forces — operationalizing its Red Sea proxy to impose costs Tehran itself need not bear. As energy markets tighten and oil inventories continue falling, every new disruption raises both the economic costs of restraint and the political incentives for further escalation. Every Houthi attack on Saudi Arabia — and every Saudi retaliation — creates new pressure to escalate further, including for ground operations to "reopen" Hormuz, not just to respond to the Houthis [47].

Kamran Bokhari ([GPF]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/geopolitical-futures.html)) reframes the Houthi entry against the prevailing assumption that the group acts as Iran's proxy. Despite being widely described as the most active member of Iran's regional network, the Houthis had avoided escalation throughout the worst crisis the Iranian regime has faced since its inception — a restraint that revealed their independent calculus. Their central objective is consolidating power as Yemen's dominant political and military force; every move serves that goal. Their belated entry into the Iran war was an opportunistic exploitation of regional instability, not an ideological commitment to Tehran's conflict [64]. Iran supplies resources that bolster Houthi capabilities, but it does not exercise the command and control associated with a traditional proxy relationship. The Houthis retain considerable freedom to operate as they see fit. Their actions are best read as instruments of political coercion: limited, carefully calibrated escalation that exploits Saudi Arabia's vulnerability without necessarily triggering a full return to war. They struck Saudi Arabia's Abha airport on July 13–14 after accusing the Saudis of attacking Sanaa International Airport while a Houthi delegation returning from Tehran attempted to land, followed by a July 21 announcement imposing a maritime blockade on Saudi Arabia [64]. The autonomy framing has strategic implications: as Iran's position deteriorates, Houthi participation will likely remain measured — the movement will exploit opportunities created by wider confrontation so long as benefits exceed costs, but will pull back once sustained escalation threatens its domestic standing or invites unacceptable retaliation. The Houthis remember Operation Rough Rider, the 52-day US campaign in 2025 that consisted of more than 1,000 strikes against them. They are also watching the erosion of Iranian patronage: as Iran loses influence, the Houthis lose standing relative to the internationally recognized government in Aden. This dynamic limits Iran's ability to offset its conventional military losses through its partners — the regional balance of power is shifting against Iran despite sporadic "successes" in the Red Sea and the Gulf [64].

By late July, horizontal escalation had crossed into [NATO]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/organizations/nato.html) territory. [Velina Tchakarova]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/velina-tchakarova.html)'s Day 150 diagnostic confirms that Iran has threatened RAF Fairford in the [United Kingdom]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-kingdom.html) over its use for B-1 bomber missions, and NATO air defences engaged Iranian drones and missiles near Incirlik Air Base in [Turkey]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/turkey.html). Iranian drone activity has also been reported near Erbil in Iraqi Kurdistan, and strikes have hit Sheikh Isa Air Base in Bahrain and Azraq Air Base in Jordan in the same 48-hour window. The geography of the war has now touched a NATO member state directly — a different order of escalation risk than the Gulf-and-Yemen theatre tracked since February [65]. The [Red Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/red-sea.html) blockade has produced its first confirmed kinetic strikes on tankers rather than diversions alone: the Houthis claimed missile and drone strikes on two Saudi oil tankers, setting one ablaze — the transition from blockade declaration to enforcement, now completed with confirmed attack. This is the S5 generative linkage identified in the Day 144 diagnostic reaching its kinetic expression [65]. See [Global System Rupture]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-rupture.html).

## The Two-Front Maritime Trap and the August Buffer Collapse

[Robert Pape]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/robert-pape.html)'s July 28 analysis reframes the war as a two-front maritime contest across "strategically linked theaters" — Hormuz plus the [Red Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/red-sea.html) — produced by Iran's activation of Houthi attacks on Saudi oil tankers. The single-chokepoint problem has become a structural trap: closing one corridor no longer solves the bypass problem because the bypass itself can be attacked [70].

The **three-buffer framework** decomposes the ~16 mbpd that offset the ~20 mbpd Hormuz baseline into identifiable components. Roughly 3–4 mbpd came from SPR releases — the US and 31 partner countries releasing ~400 million barrels total, including ~172 million barrels from the US SPR. Roughly 4–5 mbpd came from Chinese demand destruction following the Trump–Xi summit, pulling world oil consumption from ~107 mbpd before the war to ~103 mbpd. The largest buffer — roughly 7 mbpd — came from physical bypass infrastructure: the Saudi East-West Pipeline moving ~5 mbpd west to [Yanbu]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html) on the Red Sea, and the Abu Dhabi pipeline carrying ~2 mbpd to Fujairah on the Gulf of Oman. Each buffer has a distinct vulnerability profile. SPR releases are finite and approaching the congressional mandated minimum. Chinese demand destruction reflects economic weakness rather than policy choice and is now reversing as Chinese refiners accelerate Russian crude purchases. Pipeline bypass depends on the security of Red Sea and Gulf of Oman shipping lanes — and the Red Sea bypass is the largest buffer of the three [70].

The Red Sea front now threatens the largest buffer directly. Over 40% of Saudi oil bypassing Hormuz via the Red Sea is not reaching the global market after Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia's Red Sea terminals. Each buffer falls along a different trajectory, but the composite — ~16 mbpd now offset by SPR releases, softer Chinese demand, and bypass pipelines — is set to fall by more than half by end of August. The buffers that absorbed the Hormuz shock cannot absorb a simultaneous Red Sea shock, because the bypass buffer that anchors the stack is now the most exposed. The forecast: by end of August, "shortages, not just disruption, are about to begin in earnest" [70]. See [SPR Depletion Crisis]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/spr-depletion-crisis.html).

**Operation Rough Rider** (March 15 – May 6, 2025) is the closest historical test and sets the minimum planning assumption. The seven-week campaign with 1,000+ strikes, anchored by the USS Gerald R. Ford and including B-2 bombers from Diego Garcia, tactically dominated but only fought the Houthis to a tie. Shipping through the Red Sea remains down 60% a year later. Airpower destroyed launchers, warehouses, and commanders but could not reliably suppress a dispersed, underground missile-and-drone network quickly enough to restore commercial confidence across an entire maritime theater. If reopening one corridor required months of sustained combat and still failed to restore commercial traffic, analysts should begin by assuming that reopening both corridors simultaneously will take at least as long [70].

The carrier arithmetic compounds the constraint. Of America's eleven carriers, only four are deployed worldwide, two are already committed to Iran combat operations, and at most two more could be shifted into the Middle East — producing a maximum of four carriers available to split across both theaters. With no hidden reserve, splitting four carriers between Hormuz and the Red Sea offers no overwhelming concentration of force, only a prolonged campaign against dispersed missile and drone networks across two connected theaters. The United States did not plan for a long war against Iran and does not have the deployable forces for two long naval wars — leaving strategic overstretch, defined as the gap between military commitments and usable military capacity, structurally entrenched [70]. See [Energy and Resources]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/energy-and-resources.html).

**Pipeline bypass logistics** complete the picture. [Javier Blas]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/javier-blas.html) frames the bypass problem in pipeline-capacity arithmetic. The Sumed pipeline — a 50-year-old, 320-kilometer pair of pipes connecting Ain Sukhna on the Red Sea to Sidi Kerir near Alexandria on the Mediterranean — has capacity of ~2.5 mbpd, roughly half of current Yanbu flow. The Eilat-to-Ashkelon pipeline adds another ~1.2 mbpd, narrowing but not closing the ~1.3 mbpd gap. Saudi Arabia's preferred workaround for the Bab el-Mandeb closure is to push oil north through the Suez Canal rather than south toward the chokepoint, but VLCCs — the workhorse carriers, each capable of carrying two million barrels — cannot transit Suez fully laden because the waterway is too shallow. The required workaround is lightering: supertankers offload part of their cargo into Sumed, transit with reduced draft, and reload on the Mediterranean side. Oil tankers heading to Asia after emerging through Sumed's Mediterranean terminus are routed through the eastern rather than the Indian Ocean side, adding ~25 days to the trip from Saudi Arabia to Japan. The longer route ties up a much larger share of the tanker fleet at any given moment, intensifying the charter-demand surge already underway at Hormuz. Combined pipelines, lightering, and longer routing keep oil flowing, but at materially higher freight, charter, and refueling costs and with operational complexity that compounds the existing insurance and war-risk distortions [71].

Two conditions could invalidate the planning assumption: a political settlement requiring full US withdrawal of all forces and bases from the region (implausible as of July 2026), or a massive new naval force generation not currently in evidence. Absent either, the assumption survives the stress test: reopening both maritime corridors will take months, not weeks. The US may ultimately reopen both corridors, but the current balance of available forces offers little reason to expect this can be done quickly enough to avoid months of continued disruption to global energy markets [70]. See [Red Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/red-sea.html) and [Insurance and Trade Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/insurance-and-trade-warfare.html).

## Spiral Phase: Dispersal Architecture as Target

The next escalation vector opened by [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) sits inside rather than alongside the dispersal logic — the same architecture that lets the US sustain military operations has itself become the target class. [Robert Pape]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/robert-pape.html)'s Breaking Analysis #8 (July 29, 2026) frames the strike that hit [Jordan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/jordan.html)'s Muwaffaq Salti Air Base that day as confirming a June 15 "Period of Maximum Leverage" forecast and the entry of the war into the spiral phase [72].

The strike itself was the rare kind that publicly identifies both actor and target. The IRGC explicitly stated that its Aerospace Force targeted a US military CENTCOM center inside Jordan; all incoming projectiles were successfully intercepted by air defenses; no US casualties were reported; global oil prices rose 5%. The attack occurred shortly after Trump's meeting with Netanyahu — a timing that frames the strike as operating on Iran's own decision cycle rather than as a function of US escalation [72].

What changed structurally is the target class. Behind the Jordan strike: the Pentagon has dispersed assets away from vulnerable Gulf bases (Al Udeid in Qatar, facilities in Bahrain) because Iran's arsenal of precision ballistic missiles, maneuvering warheads, and mass-produced drones has made concentration near Iran structurally unsafe. The Pentagon's response has been to disperse to Jordan — Muwaffaq Salti now hosts roughly 4,000 US personnel, advanced fighters, drones, ISR, and air-defense systems. Jordan is therefore not "another American base" but a pillar of the geographic architecture that lets the US sustain military operations at all [72].

The same dispersal that gave Jordan its value made it a target. Iran's objective has shifted from destroying American military power to making that power progressively more expensive, politically difficult, and operationally distant. Every additional mile imposed on US aircraft increases sortie times, refueling requirements, logistics costs, and operational tempo penalties, while repeated attacks against facilities supporting US operations increase pressure on regional governments that host American forces. The strike demonstrates that Iran is now contesting the **entire regional system through which the United States projects military power**, not merely American forces — and the dispersal architecture is the part of that system least defended. The conclusion Pape draws from the spiral phase: understanding the shift is essential to understanding where this war is likely headed next, and why the costs of sustaining America's regional military presence are likely to continue rising even if no decisive battlefield breakthrough occurs [72]. See [Iran Strike on Jordan]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/iran-jordan-strike-2026-07-29.html).

## Mediterranean Theater Extension: The Damietta Strike

The spiral-phase dynamics also appeared on the maritime side on July 29, 2026, but at a separate geography. Two ships caught fire after an explosion at Damietta Port on Egypt's Mediterranean coast, north of the Suez Canal — the first recorded drone attack on commercial shipping at a Mediterranean Egyptian port and the first expansion of the [US-Iran War's]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) kinetic footprint past the Persian Gulf and Red Sea chokepoints into the Mediterranean approach corridor [73].

Three maritime tracking firms (Kpler, Ambrey, and Windward) corroborated different parts of the event. Kpler said both vessels had been moved away from the port. Ambrey's chief commercial officer Joshua Hutchinson stated in an interview that the explosion was believed caused by a drone strike, hitting at least one of the ships. Egypt's petroleum ministry confirmed there were no confirmed injuries or deaths but did not officially confirm drone causation or attribute the attack. Two anonymous Iranian sources framed the strike as an escalation signal regarding global shipping and energy disruption, without identifying the launching actor [73].

The asset profile is its own political signal. The vessel that caught fire was the **Energos Winter**, a US-owned LNG storage tanker owned by Energos Infrastructure (Stamford, CT) via Apollo Global Management-managed funds and leased to Egyptian Natural Gas Holding Company (EGAS), Egypt's government-owned gas company. The second ship, **GasLog Salem**, is an LNG carrier operated by Greek firm GasLog, struck by at least one drone according to Ambrey. President Trump told reporters he had been briefed on the explosion and implied Iran was responsible, completing the response with a pledge to retaliate — "it's our turn to hit them" [73].

The Damietta strike extends the rerouting pressure into a corridor that had until now absorbed redirected traffic. Windward reports that the number of vessels passing through Bab al-Mandeb has decreased by one-fifth since July 20, and traffic through the Strait of Hormuz remains at less than one-tenth of prewar levels. A Mediterranean strike pushes shipping companies and Egyptian port authorities into evaluating a third approach corridor alongside the Red Sea and Gulf risks already priced into war-risk premiums, insurance regimes (see [Insurance and Trade Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/insurance-and-trade-warfare.html)), and charter decisions. The geopolitical pressure on Cairo also widens: Egypt sits on both the Sumed pipeline terminus (a 50-year-old, 320-kilometer pair of pipes from Ain Sukhna on the Red Sea to Sidi Kerir near Alexandria, with capacity ~2.5 mbpd) and a Mediterranean LNG import terminal leased to a US company — making disruption at either end a direct concern for Egypt's energy position regardless of where the kinetic event occurs [73]. See [Damietta Port Drone Strike]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/damietta-port-drone-strike-2026-07-29.html) and [Egypt]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/egypt.html).

[Robert Pape]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/robert-pape.html)'s Breaking Analysis #9 (July 31, 2026) frames the Damietta strike as the most important strategic-map event of the war so far: until July 29, every major energy disruption lay south of Suez. The strike extends the war's geography into a third front, demonstrating for the first time that [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) or its proxies can threaten both ends of the Middle East's export system. Damietta sits adjacent to the Suez Canal's northern gateway and the SUMED pipeline — two routes that gained strategic importance precisely because the Red Sea and Hormuz had become unreliable. The IRGC's operational doctrine envisions a [security resistance belt]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/iran-security-resistance-belt.html) connecting the Persian Gulf, Red Sea, and Mediterranean into a single strategic theater; every new front forces Washington to defend another piece of the global energy system without reducing pressure anywhere else [79]. The strike's target profile — a US-owned civilian LNG vessel — signals that American commercial assets are no longer insulated from the conflict, and that the war is evolving from a contest over military access to the Gulf into a broader contest over who controls the economic arteries of globalization [79]. In June, the dilemma was to concede Hormuz or escalate; by August, the dilemma has expanded to conceding all three avenues of access to Persian Gulf energy or escalating — a trap that makes Trump progressively more locked into escalation rather than less [79].

## Spiral Phase Oscillation: The Illusion of Control

Pape's Breaking Analysis #10 (August 3, 2026) reframes [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html)'s abrupt cancellation of planned strikes on Iran — announced amid claims that Middle Eastern allies had reached peace-agreement parameters — as not de-escalation but **oscillation**. Iranian officials denied any negotiations were underway, and the structural indicators continued to deteriorate: commercial tanker traffic through [Hormuz]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html) had slowed to an estimated 80% drop in volume, with only one inbound large crude carrier arriving in the past week. The [Bab el-Mandeb]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/red-sea.html) remained under severe pressure as unofficial Houthi blockades forced multiple commercial vessels to turn back. State Department security alerts warned American citizens throughout the Middle East to prepare for "unexpected escalation" and consider departing the region [78].

In the Spiral Phase of the Escalation Trap, tactical pauses are not off-ramps. They are moments when political leaders search for alternatives because the available military options have become costlier while the underlying strategic problem remains unresolved. Airpower has not restored secure commercial transit through Hormuz, has not broken Iran's regional leverage, and has not produced a political settlement. The failure on all three measures creates an increasingly difficult **ground power dilemma** for Washington: the military instrument that has been applied cannot achieve the political objective, yet the political objective cannot be abandoned [78].

The American political calendar is shifting incentives in ways that favor Iran, which treats **time itself as a weapon**. A pause without a political settlement allows Tehran to keep consolidating its emerging security resistance belt stretching from the Mediterranean through the Red Sea to the Persian Gulf. If that process continues while shipping remains constrained, Washington's options become progressively more difficult rather than easier. The indicators to watch are not presidential rhetoric but Hormuz and Red Sea commercial traffic, Chinese purchases of discounted Iranian oil, and tightening global oil and refined-product inventories [78]. See [Iran's Security Resistance Belt]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/iran-security-resistance-belt.html) and [Escalation Theory Critique]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/escalation-theory-critique.html).

## Domestic Politics and the War's Legitimacy

The human and institutional costs of the war mounted in parallel with its strategic incoherence. [Heather Cox Richardson]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/heather-cox-richardson.html) documents that as of July 20, 2026, the US death toll reached 17 KIA since Trump launched strikes on February 28, with 427 service members wounded. An Iranian attack on a US base in Jordan killed two soldiers and left one missing; another died in northern Iraq. The Jordan strike was the fourth in five days, wounding dozens and damaging helicopters — demonstrating that Iranian forces still retain substantial missile inventories and are becoming more skilled at evading US air defense systems. The Pentagon under Defense Secretary Hegseth did not disclose earlier strikes, casualties, or damage, citing operational security; no major briefing has been held since early May. CBS News reported that Hegseth's claim that "almost 90%" of injuries were minor is misleading — the Army classifies soldiers as "seriously injured" only if at risk of dying within 72 hours. Pentagon spokesperson Sean Parnell called concealment accusations "outright lies." Neither the White House nor the Pentagon has outlined a military strategy since the [ceasefire MoU]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/iran-ceasefire-mou.html) was declared no longer in force. In nine days, both sides increased strikes. The administration did not consult Congress before launching the war and has exceeded the 60-day time limit for military action without congressional approval [55].

The financial costs are equally opaque. Richardson reports that the Pentagon is seeking a $67 billion supplemental appropriation, with Defense Secretary Hegseth and Chairman of the Joint Chiefs Gen. Caine testifying before the Senate Appropriations Committee. Hegseth claimed the Iran war has cost $37.5 billion, though independent analysts assess the true figure is higher. The Navy and Air Force will exhaust their operational funds by the end of July 2026; the Pentagon is already diverting equipment and maintenance money and canceling exercises to sustain the campaign. The Army is cutting legal resources for disability evaluations. The funding crisis underscores the gap between the war's open-ended commitments and the resources available to sustain them — a gap that the $1.5 trillion defense budget (see [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html)) was supposed to close but manifestly did not [56]. The same hearing produced a striking exchange between Sen. Ossoff and Hegseth. Ossoff pressed the secretary on his earlier claims that Iran's missile threat had been "destroyed" and rendered "combat ineffective" four months ago — claims contradicted by the ongoing missile strikes that have killed US soldiers and damaged bases throughout July. Hegseth deflected: "No one ever stated that every single missile was gone." Ossoff persisted: "You will not answer whether your statement... was a truthful statement." The exchange crystallizes the central gap between the administration's declared victory narrative and the operational reality of a war that continues to impose costs on American forces and taxpayers alike. See [Donald Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) [56].

The domestic political backlash intensified through July. Richardson documents that the administration's war funding has become deliberately obfuscated: Rep. Joe Morelle, ranking Democrat on the House Appropriations defense subcommittee, reports that the administration has not given Congress an accounting of where the money is going. Three separate funding buckets — standard appropriations, reconciliation measures that bypass appropriations committees, and supplemental requests — are being used without "rhyme or reason." Hegseth claimed the war has cost $37.5 billion; NBC News reported the internal Pentagon estimate at about $100 billion. Moody's economists estimated the cost to U.S. households at approximately $1,100 each when higher gas prices are included [63]. Sen. Murkowski (R-AK) raised the possibility that the administration was using the supplemental funding request as a backdoor way to circumvent the War Powers Resolution, referring to a 1999 OLC opinion that a supplemental appropriation passed without authorizing language could substitute for explicit congressional authorization. Hegseth deferred to "our legal department." The constitutional crisis is compounded by the fiscal picture: federal debt has surpassed 100% of GDP, projected to exceed its WWII record by 2030. Domestic austerity continues alongside war spending — Medicaid, SNAP, foreign aid, scientific research, and CDC funding are being slashed while the Pentagon seeks more [63]. Just over a third of MAGA voters think the Iran war has been worth the economic cost, down from about half in May. A person close to the White House told *Politico* that officials are "at a maximum level of frustration" and that Trump "is now fully aware that the only way to the victory that he wants is completely politically impossible." On July 23, the House passed a concurrent resolution to end the war in Iran by a vote of 214 to 208, with four Republicans joining Democrats. The Senate declined to advance S.J. Res. 180 by a vote of 47 to 49, with four Republican senators not voting. Rep. Jason Crow (D-CO), a former Army Ranger, warned that the impulse to resort to war led to "twenty-plus years" of war in Iraq and Afghanistan, "trillions of dollars, and thousands of American lives lost for wars that ended poorly" [63]. The economic signals hardened in parallel: oil surged 7% to more than $100 a barrel for Brent crude after Houthi attacks on two Saudi Arabian oil tankers in the Red Sea. The average price of gasoline in the United States rose to $4.09 a gallon. The U.S. has surged additional troops, weaponry, and medics to the Middle East, adding to tens of thousands already there; the Navy maintains 17 ships including two carrier strike groups. Barak Ravid of *Axios* reported that Trump is "close to making a decision" about a "massive attack" on Iran, saying the Iranians "haven't received enough pain yet" [63].

## Technology, Cost, and Systemic Effects

The war exposed a structural asymmetry in military technology that favors cheap mass over expensive small-batch systems. O'Brien identifies the core problem: the United States relied on pricey Patriot and THAAD interceptor systems to defend against cheap Iranian drones — an exchange ratio that is mathematically unsustainable over any prolonged engagement. The US did not possess a cheap mass attack weapon, and its munitions stocks were greatly run down by the time the MoU was signed. Rebuilding these inventories will take years. The broader conclusion O'Brien draws is that the US war machine is built on wrong principles — expensive, technologically advanced systems in small numbers, optimized for a paradigm of warfare that [drone warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/drone-warfare.html) has rendered obsolete [2]. Urbanski (RUSI) argues that the Iran war and the Ukraine war carry opposite signs — in Iran, an authoritarian regime struck by a superpower; in Ukraine, a young democracy attacked by an authoritarian superpower — yet for NATO's budget planners they yield one coherent set of lessons. Neither Iran nor Ukraine found a way to defeat a superpower decisively on the battlefield. Both found ways to make a superpower's victory expensive — and potentially unaffordable — by shifting the contest into domains where the Western alliance has under-invested: the economics of munitions, the protection of infrastructure far behind the front line, commercial risk, and the public's confidence in everyday security. The US expended roughly half its Patriot inventory, more than 850 Tomahawks, and over 1,000 air-launched stand-off weapons. Rebuilding those stockpiles will take four to five years [38].

RUSI (Carneiro Júnior) documents the operational role of Palantir's Maven Smart System during Operation Epic Fury. The platform — descended from Project Maven, which provoked internal revolt at Google before Palantir took it over — fused satellite reconnaissance, persistent drone surveillance, signals intelligence, and population pattern-of-life analysis to identify, prioritise, and recommend strike packages. By April 9, US Central Command reported more than 13,000 strikes against Iranian targets, roughly 1,000 on the opening day alone. Palantir's CTO told Bloomberg Television the conflict would be remembered as the first major war in which AI played a central role. The juxtaposition is analytically significant: a war fought substantially over the survival of Iran's nuclear programme is difficult evidence for Palantir's manifesto claim that "the atomic age is ending, and a new era of deterrence built on AI is set to begin." See [AI Military Race]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/ai-military-race.html) [41].

CSIS (Cancian and Park) analyze the Trump administration's $87.6 billion supplemental request to Congress (June 24, 2026). Approximately one-third (~$32.7 billion) is driven by Iran war costs. Key DOD line items include $21 billion for munitions (only ~$7.6B estimated for war-use replacement), $17.3 billion for operational costs, and $12.1 billion for classified programs. A major omission is base repair — 228 destroyed structures identified by satellite imagery. Estimates reaching hundreds of billions did not account for the decline in daily costs after the first days [40]. CSIS (Shah and Sherman) also assess that Russia-Iran cyber collaboration during the conflict has been overstated. An unverified Ukrainian intelligence assessment suggested interactions between Russian hacktivist groups and Iranian Handala Hack via Telegram, but the evidence base is thin. Historical distrust (Russian Turla's commandeering of Iranian cyber infrastructure for false-flag operations) limits capability sharing. Drone technology and satellite imagery sharing — where Russia provided Iran with targeting intelligence for US assets — have demonstrated far greater strategic impact [40].

The war's systemic effects extend far beyond the battlefield. Post-war Iran has escalated external operations in Europe through the IRGC's "gig economy" model — recruiting disposable agents via gaming platforms for assassination plots and surveillance. These operations are funded through an oil-backed shadow banking system that circumvents Western financial architecture. The EU's designation of the IRGC has not materially degraded this network. A post-war Iran governed through an IRGC lens will likely escalate operations abroad, complicating any diplomatic stabilization framework. See [Organised Crime and Statecraft]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/organised-crime-and-statecraft.html) [24]. The war exposed structural vulnerabilities in the defense industry that extend beyond munitions depletion: in US Army deployments, more than 70 percent of logistics tonnage is fuel, and some 55 percent is consumed by the logistics systems themselves. The war demonstrated that fuel supply chains can be disrupted more quickly and effectively than front-line forces can be destroyed. NATO's Locked Shields 2026 exercise simulated large-scale attacks on power grids, telecom networks, and government systems, revealing the increased vulnerability of digital infrastructure. The defense industry is shifting toward energy resilience (microreactors, microgrids, storage) and cyber-physical integration. See [Defense Industrial Resilience]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/defense-industrial-resilience.html) [27].

Colibasanu (GPF) identifies the energy-to-food cascade as a defining second-order effect. The Gulf was the world's largest regional exporter of urea and ammonia and the second-largest exporter of diammonium phosphate (DAP) and monoammonium phosphate (MAP). Around one-third of global seaborne fertilizer trade passes through Hormuz. Food shocks will be strongest in regions that rely heavily on imported fertilizer, subsidized agricultural systems, and nitrogen-intensive crops. See [Food and Agriculture]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/food-and-agriculture.html) [27]. Colibasanu frames the broader lesson as the replacement of efficiency with resilience as the organizing principle of the global economy. The key pattern across all regions is not collapse but persistent cost inflation and volatility — resilience is replacing efficiency, and the cost of resilience is permanent [27]. The US focus on Iran also had a direct impact on sanctions against Russia: RUSI documents how the US issued general licences allowing Russian oil sales during the Hormuz crisis, kneecapping G7 price cap efforts just as they were biting (Urals fell below $50/barrel by December 2025). Nearly 30% of Russian crude in April 2026 was transported on G7 tankers. The price cap is exhausted — RUSI recommends replacing it with a full maritime services ban [39].

Tchakarova's domain diagnostics place the US-Iran War domain (S1) at RUPTURE — the most severe status level in her seven-domain framework. Her Day 144 diagnostic identifies kinetic enforcement of a routing regime — the US attempting to dictate which vessels may transit and on what terms — as the defining operational pattern. The ninth consecutive strike night is underway, with a dual blockade active (US naval blockade on Iranian ports and Iranian chokepoint control over Hormuz). The campaign had struck 95 locations across 12 cities, representing a scale of bombardment that continues to escalate. The diagnostic also notes the first credible de-escalation channel since the [MoU collapse]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/iran-ceasefire-mou.html), suggesting that even at RUPTURE the system generates its own counter-dynamics. The S1 domain feeds the S5 (Global Supply Chain Fracture) domain generatively: strikes on Iranian power infrastructure produced the [Red Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/red-sea.html) blockade through Houthi third-party action [60]. Her Day 150 diagnostic confirms that the tactical binary carried since Day 144 — ceasefire versus joint campaign — is now practically abandoned. The United States has conducted airstrikes against Iran on a nightly basis without interruption, reaching a thirteenth consecutive night on 24 July. Trump has said the campaign needs "more of the same" and is considering, without a final decision, a "massive attack." Attrition is no longer the residual branch; it is the operating mode. The S1 domain holds at RUPTURE, but its internal composition has changed: the war is no longer bilateral-with-retaliation but multilateral-with-NATO-adjacency. The S7 domain approaches RUPTURE on the basis of the NATO-territory escalation [65]. See [Global System Rupture]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-rupture.html).

## The Triumvirate of Happiness: Great-Power Restraint on the Iran File

[Velina Tchakarova]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/velina-tchakarova.html) frames the great-power coordination around the Iran file as the "Triumvirate of Happiness" — a quiet crisis ceiling preventing the war from cascading into a [Global System Rupture]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-rupture.html). The proof point is Trump's July 24 declaration that both Xi and Putin "have now confirmed to me that they will not sell any weapons to Iran. When Xi said that, he made sure to include Chinese companies as well. I took them both at their word. I am doing Xi a very BIG favor, and it would be VERY BAD for either of them to help arm Tehran." Only days earlier, on the sidelines of the ASEAN ministerial in Manila, Secretary Rubio had held separate discussions with Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi and Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov about the Persian Gulf, along with the South China Sea and Ukraine. Rubio told reporters that "great differences" are likely to remain in place but the priority was managing differences so Xi's announced September trip to the US could proceed. In his meeting with Lavrov, Russia told the Secretary that continuing to supply Kyiv with arms is not acceptable while asserting willingness to work toward a diplomatic solution in Ukraine, with both pleased by the return of discussions between Roscosmos and NASA. None of the three wants a complete break in oil flows out of the Gulf, an energy-price spike globally, and the energy, fertilizer, and food consequences of a prolonged shutdown of the Strait of Hormuz and Bab al-Mandab [74].

The Triumvirate is a de-escalation mechanism: the US is publicly relying on Chinese and Russian weapon-supply abstention to keep Iran's capacity bounded. Cooperation is "cooperation without binding commitment" — sustainable precisely because no party acknowledges it, fragile because it rests on private understandings rather than binding agreement. Both Beijing and Moscow have offered nothing to back up Trump's version of events, and Russia has plenty of reason to keep vague its intentions in Tehran. Trump publicizing the deal is itself an insurance policy against defection — he is raising the reputational costs of breaking the deal — but the same move reveals its weakness. The peace can hold, just barely, and only as long as all three accept that the systemic costs of rupture outweigh the costs of staying put [74].

## The Patriot Bottleneck and the Two-War Continuum

The Iran war and the [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html) share a binding constraint: finite US Patriot interceptor missiles, each costing $3–5M, with little room to increase production. Ukrainian cities need Patriots to shoot down Russian ballistic missiles. The US is using the same Patriots to defend its bases in the Middle East against Iranian missiles. The Patriot is one of the few weapons that can intercept ballistic missiles, and there is little industrial slack to expand output. There are two theaters, but there is only one bottleneck [74].

The Patriot limits manifest in Washington's deal-making posture. [Netanyahu]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html)'s deferral to "Trump's decision and timing" on strikes against Iran — "we know the situation, if we're attacked we will respond, but we will also rely on President Trump" — confirms the escalatory ladder is in Washington, not Jerusalem. Several Israeli commentators bluntly concluded that the US was running the war and Israel was sitting in the passenger seat. [Zelensky]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/zelensky.html)'s parallel lobbying for Patriot production licenses (promised at the Ankara NATO Summit) and Lockheed Martin joint-production talks — Ukraine needs twenty-five Patriot systems — both secures and acknowledges Washington controls the industrial base. The similar Trump-NATO walk through both wars during the [Sen. Lindsey Graham]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) funeral week produced the same result: both proxy presidents returned home weaker than when they arrived [74].

## China's Escalating Military Support and the MANPADS Threshold

[Phillips O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) documents a qualitative escalation in Chinese support for [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html). Through the first six months of the war, Chinese assistance consisted of missile propellant chemicals (sodium perchlorate shipped via IRISL vessels from Zhuhai, enough for ~785 additional missiles), satellite intelligence (the TEE-01B spy satellite monitoring US bases across the Gulf), AI-enabled satellite analysis marketed by Chinese firms, BeiDou navigation data for targeting, and dual-use chipmaking equipment supplied by SMIC. An F-15E downed in southwestern Iran in April was likely hit by a Chinese-made shoulder-launched missile [75].

In late July 2026, Reuters reported that China was preparing to deliver 400 advanced MANPADS launchers — FN-16 and QW-12 systems comparable to the Stinger — with the first shipment expected within weeks. O'Brien frames this as a categorical shift from dual-use components to finished advanced weapons, a "game changer" that would deter US low-level airpower. MANPADS are ideally suited to thwart escalation because US ground operations (supply, evacuation, patrols) require low-altitude flights near Kharg Island or inside Iran. The weapons are small, easy to operate, and could deploy by September — creating a binary choice for Trump: escalate before they arrive (risky) or admit he cannot escalate at all [75].

Even as Cabinet members admitted to Congress that China and Russia were enabling Iran's war effort, Trump posted on Truth Social that Xi and Putin had told him they would not "partake" and that he trusted them. O'Brien compares the moment to 1861, when the US was weak enough for external powers to directly threaten it — except unlike then, the US is not on an upward trajectory [75]. See [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) and [Great Power Competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html).

## The Thirteen-Day Campaign and Iran's Strategic Framework

The US conducted thirteen consecutive nights of strikes through July 23, achieving no strategic gains while further burning through critical munitions. The White House leaked the stockpile depletion problem even as Ambassador Mike Waltz publicly contradicted himself on Meet the Press — simultaneously blaming Biden, denying a shortfall existed, admitting a shortfall existed, and calling for leakers to be prosecuted. Trump's threats of large reinforcements were, in O'Brien's assessment, always a bluff: the US has already burned through the advanced systems that can be fired from a distance, and escalation now means soldiers face much heavier casualties [76].

After the US halted strikes on July 23, Iran continued attacking — demonstrating that no ceasefire existed. O'Brien catalogues the sequence: July 24 saw Arash drones strike Isa Air Base (Bahrain) and Al-Azraq/Muwaffaq Salti Air Base (Jordan); July 25 brought Houthi strikes on Aramco facilities at Jizan and Yanbu (confirmed by NASA FIRMS thermal data); July 27 opened a new proxy front as Iran-backed Iraqi groups attacked Saudi Eastern Province petroleum facilities; July 28–29 saw US reprisal strikes in Iraq and parallel Iranian strikes on Jordan. O'Brien's framing: "Iran is toying with the USA" — Trump wants out, but Iran will not let him [76].

Iran's strategic framework, as O'Brien reconstructs it, operates on an Aims/Ways/Means logic. **Aims**: control over the Strait of Hormuz, weakened US military presence, ability to restart the nuclear program, and dissolution of the US-led coalition. **Ways**: keep fighting, deplete US munitions stocks, demonstrate US weakness to allies, sustain high oil prices, delay talks unless acceptable terms emerge. **Means**: existing cruise and ballistic missiles supplemented by cheap drones and Chinese/Russian deliveries — a mix sustainable with present resources for enough time to make the US hurt. Iran calculates that time is on its side: the longer the war drags, the closer to November midterms, the more desperate Trump becomes, the stronger Iran's leverage. The war is now "the least popular war in US history" [76].

The likely shape of peace, in O'Brien's assessment, would involve Iran issuing a non-concession statement of no intent to develop nuclear weapons (with few actual checks), tacit Iranian control of Hormuz acknowledged through "administrative fees" rather than "tolls," unfrozen Iranian assets (large percentage returned immediately), sanctions wound down, and a partial US withdrawal from the immediate vicinity of Iran — all packaged by Trump as a "massive victory." O'Brien's judgment: "It will actually represent the quickest and arguably the most decisive defeat in US history" [76].

## Caspian Theater Expansion

The war's geographic footprint is expanding beyond the Gulf and the Black Sea into the [Caspian Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/caspian.html), where two geographically distant conflicts — the US-vs-Iran war and the Russia-Ukraine war — are converging in a single basin. Kamran Bokhari ([GPF]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/geopolitical-futures.html)) traces the pattern through a sequence of escalatory events [80].

The first Caspian strike occurred on March 18–19, 2026, when [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html) struck [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html)'s Bandar Anzali port on the Caspian coast — damaging naval vessels, headquarters facilities, and a shipyard. The strike aimed at disrupting the Iran-Russia arms-smuggling route that flows through the port: Iran supplies [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) with Shahed drones, drone-production expertise, artillery shells, and short-range ballistic missiles, receiving in return advanced drones and tactical intelligence. [Zelensky]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/zelensky.html) has framed Iran as a Russian co-belligerent, and the Bandar Anzali strike demonstrated that Israel and Ukraine share an interest in degrading the Caspian logistics corridor sustaining both adversaries. See [Israeli Strike on Bandar Anzali]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/bandar-anzali-strike-2026-03-19.html) [80].

On July 26, 2026, [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html) struck an Iranian cargo vessel in the Caspian Sea, killing a civilian sailor — the first time the Ukraine war reached the Caspian directly. Iran considered retaliating with a missile strike on a Ukrainian Black Sea port, but diplomacy de-escalated the situation. The strike linked the two wars in a new theater, demonstrating that the Caspian is no longer a sanctuary from either conflict. See [Ukraine Strikes Iranian Vessel in Caspian Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/ukraine-caspian-vessel-strike-2026-07-26.html) [80].

The convergence creates a structural risk: if Tehran continues expanding the conflict while pressure mounts on Washington, the US could shift to attacking Iranian Caspian assets directly. The six trans-Caspian states promoting the Middle Corridor have a shared incentive to keep the Caspian a commercial waterway, but each strike erodes the norm that the Caspian is off-limits. [Azerbaijan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/azerbaijan.html)'s position as a Caspian littoral state with close Israeli ties and a long Iranian border makes it a key node in this expanding theater — and the [South Caucasus]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/south-caucasus.html) corridor connecting the Caspian to Turkey and Europe via the Trump Route adds further strategic weight to the basin [80].

Bokhari also documents the broader diplomatic cross-currents in the Caspian: Kazakhstan has joined the Abraham Accords, and Kazakhstan, Azerbaijan, and Armenia participate in the Gaza Board of Peace — signaling that trans-Caspian states are engaging with Middle East diplomatic frameworks even as the military dimension of the Caspian grows. Israel's opening of its first permanent embassy in Ashgabat (Turkmenistan) in April 2023, approximately 20km from the Iranian border, extended the diplomatic ring around Iran's Caspian flank [80].

## The Offloading Doctrine and Post-War Architecture

[Kamran Bokhari]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/kamran-bokhari.html) reads the United States as executing a deliberate structural transition from Middle East hegemon to "offshore actor with its finger on the scales" — a shift that predates the war. The Trump administration's "offloading" posture was articulated well before February 2026, holding that US forces would stand down as capable Arab and Muslim partners stood up, replacing American dominance with shared responsibility. Washington had already decided to reduce its assets within striking range of Tehran before launching operations, and the strategic calculation is that even after the conflict the US will not return to its previous force posture. There is no returning to an era in which Washington is the region's principal security guarantor [81].

[S. Clinton Hinote]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/s-clinton-hinote.html) corroborates the motivation from the operational side — the US wants to be "done with the Middle East" to execute its pivot to Asia and shed expensive basing infrastructure. The theory of success, as Hinote articulates it, is to put the Iranian regime into "some sort of state where we don't have to go back anytime soon" — "a steady state better than what was in existence on February 28th." But he diverges on whether the execution matches the theory. US forces are accomplishing their military objectives: the Iranian Navy, Air Force, air defenses, and missile and drone production capabilities are being destroyed. Yet the conversion of tactical success into strategic outcomes is, in Hinote's judgment, "the weakness in this entire operation." Stepping back to February and assessing where things stand today, "I don't know that this would be in our theory of success. It would probably be a branch plan that would not be going so well" [82]. The tension between Bokhari's structural reading and Hinote's operational assessment is the core question — whether offloading is coherent doctrine or drift dressed as strategy — taken up in the Contested block below. See [Endless Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/endless-warfare.html) and [Escalation Theory Critique]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/escalation-theory-critique.html).

Hinote addresses the demand side — why the US wants to shed its Middle East role — but not the supply side: whether regional partners can cohere. The architecture being built to replace US hegemony is a regional core of [Turkey]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/turkey.html), [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html), [Pakistan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/pakistan.html), and [Egypt]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/egypt.html), supported by [Qatar]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/qatar.html), the [UAE]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/uae.html), [Jordan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/jordan.html), and [Azerbaijan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/azerbaijan.html). Bokhari identifies the defining challenge as twofold: aligning these states — each with its own interests and priorities — and integrating them into a workable relationship with [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html). The three most consequential actors either lack formal diplomatic ties with Israel or, in Ankara's case, have seen relations deteriorate over competition for Syria. Unlike Europe, which built its post-war order on the institutional frameworks of NATO and the EU, the Middle East is a "blank canvas": regional integration mechanisms must be constructed from scratch to manage Iran as it hurtles toward uncertainty and Israel as it undergoes its own evolution [81]. A "fundamental psychological shift" has nonetheless occurred — Iran attacking Arab states is "the new normal," and many Arab governments view Iran as a more fundamental strategic threat than Israeli policies toward the Palestinian territories, Lebanon, and Syria. Iran's demographic, geographic, and military advantages, and its exploitation of chronic Arab state-system weakness, reinforce this perception and will shape regional calculations for years to come [81].

## Proxy Network Collapse and Regime Resilience

Iran's regional position collapsed even as the regime itself survived — a paradox that defines the post-war landscape. Bokhari traces the degradation of Iran's proxy network as the strategic opening that enabled the emerging regional order: [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html)'s 2024 campaigns degraded Hamas and Hezbollah as military forces, and the resulting vacuum allowed Turkey — aided by its Hayat Tahrir al-Sham allies — to shape a new Sunni- and Kurdish-aligned political order in Syria by year's end. That outcome, combined with Turkey's expanding influence in Iraq and Washington's support for Ankara's regional role, has constrained Tehran's leverage on multiple fronts. Iran's once-rock-solid position in [Iraq]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iraq.html) is increasingly vulnerable as Baghdad and Washington curb pro-Iranian Shiite militias and strengthen state control over armed groups [81].

Yet the regime that presided over this collapse endured the most intensive decapitation campaign in modern history. Hinote calls the US-Israel operation "maybe the most successful decapitation operation in history" — yet there was no strategic paralysis and no internal uprising, despite [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html)'s public call for the Iranian people to "take over your government" and the IRGC to "lay down your weapons." One would expect a high degree of inaction at the highest levels after such a campaign; the expected paralysis never materialized, even as there is "nobody left in Iran who really can make that decision and enforce it up and down what is left of the government and military apparatus" [82]. Bokhari assesses near-term survival as near-certain so long as the US declines ground intervention and no viable domestic insurgent alternative exists — but over time, the war, the death of senior leadership, and the devolution within the IRGC will force Tehran to rethink its position as these losses reshape the internal balance of power [81].

The tension between these two facts — regional position collapsing while the regime itself survives — is not a contradiction but the war's central strategic paradox. Decapitation worked tactically, yet the conversion to strategic success is, in Hinote's assessment, "the weakness in this entire operation" [82]; proxy degradation opened space for rivals yet did not produce regime change.

## The Geographic and Doctrinal Intractability

The war's intractability has three interlocking facets — geography that offers no great options for reopening Hormuz, a last-2% problem that no military solution can close, and US-Israeli theories of victory that may work against each other. Each alone would constrain US options; together they produce a conundrum the US has not solved and is unlikely to solve.

Geography is the foundational constraint. [James Slife]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/james-slife.html), a former Air Force commander, frames the Strait of Hormuz as a problem military planners have struggled with for years: Iran controls three sides of a narrow, shallow, crowded waterway with very limited room to maneuver. "If there was a great option for how air power could unblock the Strait of Hormuz, it would probably be unblocked at this point" — but the air and naval components are limited to targeting the means Iran uses to exercise control, and "geography just doesn't favor us in this regard" [82]. Hinote identifies the structural dilemma this produces for the insurance industry: the US joint force can prevent the majority of attacks on shipping — even into the 98% range — but "that last 2%" is what the insurance industry cannot absorb, because Iran's mountainous shores provide protective cover for intermittent attacks. The problem is not military failure — "I call 98% pretty militarily successful" — but that 2% is sufficient to deny commercial confidence. So long as Iran has the will to try, it retains some level of capability, creating "a conundrum that we just haven't solved and are unlikely to solve" [82]. See [Insurance and Trade Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/insurance-and-trade-warfare.html).

The doctrinal facet compounds the geographic one. Slife notes that the US and Israel pursued "very different target sets which may tell us something about what our respective national aims are." Hinote articulates the Israeli theory with precision: Israel believes the Iranian regime will always be hostile and must go back repeatedly to re-strike regime targets — a strategy of "mowing the grass," where the grass will grow back and they will return to mow it again for years. The more leaders killed, the better, because replacements take time to become dangerous. The US theory, by contrast, is a coercion campaign demanding the strait open and Iran give up its nuclear aspirations — but "there's nobody left in Iran who really can make that decision and enforce it up and down what is left of the government and military apparatus." The two aims "not only were they not necessarily totally aligned, they may have in certain circumstances worked against each other." The result, in Hinote's words, is "a really difficult place to be" — the intractability stems from the disparity between what the US is trying to achieve militarily and what those military means can actually produce politically [82].

## Service-Reliability Drag and the F-15E Rescue

Heavy deployments have exceeded what the Air Force and Navy can routinely sustain, producing a delayed cumulative effect on readiness that Slife flags as underappreciated. The pace of operations over the last year "has probably exceeded the Air Force and the Navy's ability to maintain levels of readiness going forward" — an impact Slife says we will need to "pay close attention to" over the next three to five years, because "there is always a delayed impact to these kinds of heavy operations" [82].

Hinote highlights the F-15E back-seater rescue as a critical but underreported success that may stand as "one of the best we've ever seen" — comparable in significance to Operation Eagle Claw (1980), which was "so bad that it led to reforms for the next decade." The operation required "tremendous integration across multiple types of forces in a very difficult time frame," with scores of aircraft and helicopters sent into harm's way to rescue a single weapon-system officer. Had he been captured and paraded on Iranian television, "any political objectives that we were trying to achieve would be all the more difficult to achieve" — the rescue preserved not just a pilot but the political conditions for the campaign [82].

## The Operational Pause and Its Hidden Costs

The current state is not a ceasefire but what [Norman T. Roule]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/norman-roule.html) — former National Intelligence Manager for Iran at ODNI — terms an "operational pause": a "period of coercive restraint" in which Iran continues attacks on ships while the US restrains its response. The US is not approaching a significant core agreement. The diplomacy exhibits a consistent pattern: the US announces negotiations; Iran denies them; indirect negotiations follow through regional parties — but the parties are not at the table on nuclear, missile, or militia issues as originally discussed [83].

The Iran-Oman mechanism being discussed is likely overstated. If tolls of any sort are charged, a cascade of unresolved questions follows: what happens to a shipper who refuses to pay? How does it interact with US sanctions? Are American ships allowed to pay Iran? Through what channels, and will the US sanction other countries for using them? Iran's parliament has declared no enemy countries may transit — are Israeli or Israeli-related ships then banned from the Gulf? These are "hugely consequential questions that are being glossed over" [83]. The sanctions interaction is the most strategically dangerous hidden cost: if Iran acquires formal control over the strait, money paid by shippers would "inevitably go to its missile program and proxies," giving Tehran billions in revenue and "a permanent foot on the throat of the international community" — including the ability to dictate which food shippers can reach [Kuwait]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/kuwait.html). The idea that the US can "build on trust" with an IRGC government "has no evidence in reality" [83]. See [Hormuz Toll System]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/hormuz-toll-system.html).

Iranian aggression has continued in the cyber domain throughout the pause. Iran's cyber activities have continued "unabated" since the conflict began, including probable attacks on US water-infrastructure SCADA systems in Minnesota. While defended against by national cyber architecture and Gulf partner systems, these attacks fit the established pattern of Iranian cyber activity against SCADA systems. If left unanswered, Tehran will conclude "there's no reason we shouldn't continue to do this." Roule argues the appropriate response is "a congruent asymmetric response" against Iran's cyber architecture — proportional but "just done differently," consistent with multiple administrations' stated approaches [83].

## Gulf State Defense and Moderation

Roule assesses Gulf States' defense as "very, very successful" — the product of years of engagement with the US and the American private sector. Their air defense has protected their own nationals and infrastructure, hundreds of thousands of Americans in the region, and the international economy by maintaining stable energy prices. [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html) joined US strikes on Iraq — a first — demonstrating the quality of Saudi Air Forces and the closeness of military partnership. The Gulf States talked [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) out of imminent escalation; if they face further strikes, they will demand a voice in any major attack on Iran. "It is their neighborhood," Roule observes. "They know their region better than we do. We should respect that" [83].

## Saudi Nuclear Deal: Iran War Context and Proliferation Risk

[Guido Steinberg]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/guido-steinberg.html) contextualizes the US-Saudi civilian nuclear agreement within the ongoing Persian Gulf fighting. The agreement was concluded during the war, with Iran responding to US strikes by firing rockets and drones at Saudi Arabia and other Gulf states. This context motivated the Saudi nuclear track: the war, the Hormuz closure, and Iranian bombardment have underscored Saudi vulnerability. Although dissatisfaction with the US over the poorly planned war is high in Riyadh, MBS and his circle see no alternative to the alliance with Washington [84].

Two details of the agreement are "wegweisend" (path-breaking): Saudi Arabia is to be allowed to enrich uranium domestically, and inspections are to be less stringent than in the neighboring UAE's 2009 agreement (which included a renunciation of domestic enrichment and NPT Additional Protocol adherence). This weakens the "gold standard" set by the UAE deal. Abu Dhabi will likely seek the same conditions retroactively; Turkey may respond with its own nuclear program given its rivalry with Riyadh; and Iran will point to Saudi conditions in future negotiations to justify its own enrichment rights. An "Atommacht Saudi-Arabien" (nuclear-power Saudi Arabia) has become a possibility [84]. See [Nuclear Proliferation and Arms Control]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/nuclear-proliferation-and-arms-control.html).

Steinberg assesses that Iran's new, heavily militarized leadership will continue the weapons program pursued since 1987. The Strait of Hormuz as a weapon does not displace the nuclear program — Iran's leadership will do everything to more effectively deter the US and Israel in the future. The combination of Iran's continued nuclear pursuit and the Saudi nuclear agreement makes a Middle East with three, four, or five nuclear powers "very much more probable than previously" [84].

## Contested: Is US Iran strategy a deliberate offloading doctrine or adrift improvisation?

[Kamran Bokhari]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/kamran-bokhari.html) reads US strategy as a coherent, deliberate, and irreversible transition from hegemon to offshore balancer — the Trump administration's geostrategic doctrine of offloading posture predated the Iran war, and US assets will not return to prior posture. [81]

[S. Clinton Hinote]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/s-clinton-hinote.html) reads the same phenomena as effectively adrift — US forces are accomplishing military objectives but the conversion to strategic success is the weakness of the entire operation. The outcome "would not have been in the US theory of success" and reflects a branch plan "not going so well." [82]

The split is methodological, not evidential — Bokhari reads structural doctrine (grand strategy as structural inevitability); Hinote reads operational outcomes (tactical success without strategic conversion). Neither disputes the other's facts.
