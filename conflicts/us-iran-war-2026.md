# Background

The US-Iran War of 2026 pitted the world's dominant conventional military against a geographically entrenched adversary whose theory of victory was narrower — survival rather than conquest — and therefore more resilient [28][67]. For the IRGC and many Iranians, the conflict concerned regime survival and national sovereignty; for the United States, save for the nuclear program, it did not [67]. This asymmetry meant Iran could absorb far higher costs and maintain political will long after domestic support for the war had eroded in the US — a pattern consistent with Vietnam, Iraq, and Afghanistan [67].

Iran controls three sides of the Strait of Hormuz — a narrow, shallow, crowded waterway through which roughly 20 million barrels per day of petroleum liquids flow, representing approximately 20% of global supply [28][38][66][82]. Iran never needed to achieve sea control; it needed only to make transit uninsurable and unprofitable [38]. The Strait's geography offers no accessible beachhead: Iran's coastline rises into rugged mountains with concealed firing positions, and the country spans roughly 1.65 million square kilometers with a population of about 92 million and roughly one million personnel under arms [52][61]. A Shahed-136 drone is approximately the size of an F-150 truck; Iran has an estimated 2.5–3 million comparable vehicles, making visual identification of launch platforms effectively impossible [52][61]. Destroying 80–90% of Iranian anti-shipping assets is insufficient — all must be suppressed continuously [52]. Even into the 98% range of attack prevention, the last 2% is what the insurance industry cannot absorb [82].

Iran's doctrine — mosaic defense, formalized in 2005 by the IRGC's Center for Strategy under Mohammad Ali Jafari and the ideologue Hassan Abbasi — is a decentralized command structure designed to survive decapitation strikes [28]. Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi stated: "We've had two decades to study defeats of the U.S. military to our immediate east and west. We've incorporated lessons accordingly" [28]. Iran had studied the force structure it faced and designed its counter-campaign accordingly — targeting not strike aircraft but the tankers, radar networks, communications nodes, and command platforms that sustained them [28]. The cost exchange told its own story: Iran's drones and missiles cost thousands of dollars apiece; the interceptors fired to stop them ran into millions [28].

Over five years before the war, Iran built an economy to evade sanctions — its tanker fleet grew from roughly 70 vessels in 2020 to nearly 550 by 2025, sustained by Chinese refineries that alone accounted for close to 90 percent of Iran's foreign oil sales [28]. This shadow infrastructure allowed Iran to ship roughly 1.5 million barrels of crude per day even during the Hormuz blockade [8].

The United States entered the war relying on expensive, technologically advanced systems in small numbers — Patriots and THAAD air defense costing millions per shot to engage cheap Iranian drones — a force structure optimized for a paradigm of warfare that drone technology had rendered obsolete [2][38]. CSIS estimated the US expended roughly half its Patriot inventory, more than 850 Tomahawk cruise missiles, and over 1,000 air-launched stand-off weapons; rebuilding those stockpiles will take four to five years [38]. The Patriot interceptor is the sole missile that can intercept ballistic missiles, and finite production capacity binds the Iran war and the Russia-Ukraine War into a single resource problem: "There are two theaters, but there is only one bottleneck" [74][89].

The "escort" paradigm for Hormuz epitomizes Western doctrinal obsolescence: the concept derives from World War-era anti-submarine warfare, but today's threat is drone and missile swarms that cannot be defended against at scale — escorts provide more targets, not protection [54]. The purpose of attack is not necessarily to sink ships but to discourage sailing — a political and economic effect, not a kinetic one [54].

The energy disruption cascaded through chemicals and fertilizer into food prices: roughly one-third of global seaborne fertilizer trade passes through Hormuz, and standard trade models underestimated the impact because they missed the bottleneck mechanism [27][91]. The refining bottleneck compounded the crude shortage: around 10% of world refining capacity was offline, primarily from Ukraine's drone campaign against Russian refineries, meaning a Hormuz reopening would add crude but not usable fuel [30][42][87]. The crack spread — the margin between buying crude and selling gasoline and diesel — reached the highest levels ever recorded [87][93].

Israel's long-term goal for Iran is complete regime change toward a secular, business-friendly government; in the short term, Israel views a chaotic Iran — potentially fracturing into ethnic states — as beneficial despite the disaster it would cause for the US [26]. The nuclear issue was a pretext used by Israel to draw the US into a war it could not win alone [26]. This produced a structural US-Israel strategic divergence: Washington seeks to use military gains to create a diplomatic framework constraining Iran, whereas Israel remains skeptical that an ideologically driven regime can be durably moderated through negotiation [23]. The two aims not only diverged but in certain circumstances worked against each other [82].

Iran and its axis partners — Russia and China — maintained a structured coordination architecture predating the war, including trilateral deputy foreign minister meetings in Beijing in March 2025 and expert-level sessions in Moscow in April 2025 [6]. The axis consultation on the ceasefire was "almost certainly a green light, not an obstacle" [6].

The legal contest over the Strait predates the war: Iran maintains that only innocent passage — not transit passage — applies, a reading that would grant it wider regulatory authority; Iran is not a party to UNCLOS, having signed but not ratified, while Oman is a party — leaving the two littoral states under divergent legal regimes [97]. Iran's proposed toll system lacks any basis in international law: under UNCLOS Article 26, states may not charge fees for the mere passage of foreign ships through their territorial sea [97].

Iran has pursued a military nuclear program since 1987 [84]. The US-Saudi civilian nuclear agreement, concluded during the war, permits Saudi domestic uranium enrichment with less stringent inspections than the UAE's 2009 agreement — eroding non-proliferation precedent and making a Middle East with three, four, or five nuclear powers markedly more probable [84].

## Pre-War Context

US leaders had threatened Iran for a decade, the methods of an air campaign were predictable, and Israel's participation was expected — the outbreak itself was not surprising [21]. Iran was not surprised either: it had expected the attack and developed capabilities to pursue its objective of survival [21]. The war was a personal decision by Donald Trump, made without political pressure and not in the national interest — a finding that directly challenges the assumptions of realism [2]. The United States started the war without informing its European allies, Japan, or South Korea, after more than a year of insults, threats, and tariff wars [2]. Aurelien classifies the war as a classic case of "surprise-at-failure": the surprise came because the overall strategic situation was read wrongly, with backwards logic from desired outcome to necessary false assumptions about Iranian state fragility, targetability, and popular uprising [21].

# Current Situation

As of August 2026, the conflict has entered what Norman Roule terms an "operational pause" — not a ceasefire but a "period of coercive restraint" in which Iran continues attacks on shipping while the US restrains its response [83]. The Versailles Memorandum (MoU) of June 17 collapsed around July 13; Trump declared it formally over on July 8 [31][36][50]. Thirteen consecutive nights of US strikes (July 11–23) achieved no strategic gains while further burning through critical munitions [76]. After the US halted strikes on July 23, Iran continued attacking — directly or through proxies — demonstrating that no ceasefire existed [76]. Trump appeared willing to extend the blockade through the November midterms, preferring continued war over accepting visible defeat [95].

Iran's parliament is reviewing legislation to bar vessels linked to the US, Israel, and other "hostile nations" from the Strait of Hormuz and impose transit fees of 5–7% of cargo value — moving from military coercion to institutionalized maritime authority [86]. Iran has established the Persian Gulf Strait Authority (PGSA) to regulate shipping and collect tolls [97]. The Supreme National Security Council has issued six demands before Hormuz reopens: approximately $300 billion for damages, over $100 billion in frozen assets, and ending Israel's occupation of Palestine — terms Pape reads as victor's justice, not bargaining [87]. The period of maximum leverage runs through January 2027: Iran's objective is to "drive a stake through the heart of the Trump presidency" by wrecking the US economy [87]. Tehran is explicitly waiting out the Trump presidency, reorganizing its military leadership for prolonged confrontation [96].

Tanker traffic through Hormuz remains at roughly an 80% drop in volume, with only one inbound large crude carrier arriving in the past week [78]. No LNG tanker has crossed since July 16; Qatari LNG vessels accumulated in the Gulf, unable to reach export markets [53]. War-risk premiums for vessels transiting the strait rose to 3–10% of hull value — as much as 40 times the prewar rate [53]. VLCC daily earnings hit approximately $470,000, a record [53]. The Houthis separately announced a blockade of Saudi Arabia's Red Sea ports [53]. The rumored 7% cargo-value fee under negotiation mirrors the approximately 7% that marine insurers currently charge for Persian Gulf voyages — institutionalizing the wartime-risk premium rather than eliminating it [85].

The war's kinetic footprint has expanded across three maritime fronts: the Persian Gulf, the Red Sea (Bab el-Mandeb), and the Mediterranean (Damietta Port drone strike, July 29) [73][79]. NATO territory has been directly touched: Iran threatened RAF Fairford over its use for B-1 bomber missions, and NATO air defenses engaged Iranian drones near Incirlik Air Base in Turkey [65]. The Caspian basin has become a convergence zone where the US-Iran war and the Russia-Ukraine war overlap — Ukraine struck an Iranian cargo vessel in the Caspian on July 26 [80].

China is preparing to send Iran finished advanced munitions — QW-12 and FN-16 MANPADS, up to 400 shoulder-fired air defense missile launchers — a categorical shift from dual-use components to finished weapons that could arrive and deploy by September [75]. The Triumvirate of Happiness — clandestine great-power coordination between Washington, Beijing, and Moscow designed to keep the war from escalating into Global System Rupture — is a crisis ceiling only as strong as each member's willingness to stay put [74].

Pape diagnoses Stage Four of the Escalation Trap: strategic paralysis. If the US remains in paralysis while Iran consolidates, Iran becomes the rising regional hegemon in the Persian Gulf [87]. The Saudi-Turkey-Pakistan "Mecca pact" defense agreement fills a power vacuum left by US decline [87]. Pape's exit criterion is testable: tactical success must be converted into political success — a stable settlement requiring Iran to accept constraints because it believes the alternative is worse — before January 2029 [88]. The war is likely to continue "at least through January" 2027 [33].

US domestic support has collapsed. As of July 20, the death toll reached 17 killed in action with 427 wounded [55]. The House passed a concurrent resolution to end the war 214–208 on July 23 [63]. The Pentagon's internal cost estimate is approximately $100 billion, against the official $37.5 billion [63]. Just over a third of MAGA voters think the war has been worth the economic cost, down from about half in May [63]. A White House source reported the president is "now fully aware that the only way to the victory that he wants is completely politically impossible" [63]. The state of the US military is, in O'Brien's assessment, arguably the worst since 1945 [95].

The US is withdrawing forces from the Gulf: precipitous pullouts from Kuwait because American forces cannot be protected against even cheap Iranian strike capabilities, with forces partially redeployed to Jordan [94]. The Carter Doctrine — the 1980 commitment to use military force to keep the Persian Gulf open — has been demolished by the paradox that US military force itself caused another power to close the Gulf [94]. Under discussion is an Iran-Oman mechanism: inbound ships would route through Iranian territorial waters, outbound vessels near Oman's coast, with "service fees" split equally — the US further marginalized from the governance structure [94]. Qatar distributed a draft plan for restarting talks on August 4, with Iran holding authority over inbound traffic and Iran and Oman jointly over outbound shipping [90]. Iranian officials dismiss US assertions of bilateral negotiations as "repeated lies" and "desperate cries," maintaining that Tehran engages only via regional mediators [85].

The 60-day [Iran Ceasefire MoU]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/iran-ceasefire-mou.html) has expired without producing peace, effectively opening the next phase of the war [101]. By mid-August, Hormuz throughput had collapsed to just five commodity vessels on one Saturday and none the following Sunday, against a pre-war baseline exceeding 130 vessels per day [101]. Oil has traded above $90 per barrel as expectations of a renewed agreement faded [101]. Pre-war, the strait transited approximately 20.9 million barrels per day of petroleum liquids — roughly one-fifth of global consumption — and over one-fifth of global LNG trade; [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html) and UAE bypass pipelines can reroute only about 4.7 million barrels per day [101]. Roughly half of any crude-oil price change passes through to US retail gasoline within two weeks, and about 80% within four weeks [101]. Treasury Secretary Bessent has pledged economic measures against [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) of unprecedented scope [101].

Legislative pressure has intensified on both sides of Congress. The House voted 214–208 to require congressional approval for further military action against Iran, with four Republicans joining Democrats; the Senate rejected its parallel measure 49–47 [101]. In June, the Senate had voted 50–48 for a resolution to halt unauthorized US military action against Iran — the first Senate approval of such a measure during the conflict [101]. Public opposition has deepened across multiple polls: AP-NORC found 64% of Americans believe the war was not worth fighting, 72% consider preventing oil and gas price rises an important foreign-policy goal, and 40% name gasoline prices as a major stress source, up from 27% before the war [101]. A late-July Quinnipiac survey recorded 60% opposed to US military action — the highest opposition since the war began — with 59% lacking confidence in [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html)'s Iran deal and 45% saying the US is weaker as a consequence [101]. Reuters/Ipsos pegged Trump's approval at 33%, the lowest of his presidency, with only 20% supporting the war and 80% expecting it to last a long time [101].

Since the June 17 MoU, [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) used the diplomatic lull to prepare for a larger confrontation: the IRGC consolidated control over the regular army, appointed veterans to key posts, expanded counterintelligence, and ramped up missile and drone production [102]. The IRGC coordinated with allied militias in Iraq, Yemen, and Lebanon during the pause [102]. Iran expanded the maritime theater to the [Red Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/red-sea.html) to counter Saudi bypass routes [102]. In early July, roughly three weeks after the MoU, Iran began shooting at ships the US was guiding through the strait, catching the US by surprise [102]. Iran is rebuilding infrastructure and restoring missile base access faster than expected [102]. The IRGC threatened Gulf states with destruction of energy facilities if the US strikes similar sites in Iran, sending detailed target lists [102].

The [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) has redeployed the USS George Washington from the Western Pacific to replace the Abraham Lincoln in the [Middle East]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html), leaving zero US carriers in the Western Pacific for the first time since 1943–44 [103]. The carrier maintenance backlog is now backed up for years; the Lincoln alone will require at least two years of refit [103].

# Analysis

## Strategic Diagnosis: Why the US Lost and How Iran Won

The diagnosis runs from what the United States got wrong—institutionally, doctrinally, politically—to how Iran had spent twenty years preparing the ground for exactly that failure.

Three weeks before the war, on February 5, [Kamran Bokhari]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/kamran-bokhari.html) of [Geopolitical Futures]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/geopolitical-futures.html) judged that "there is no way the regime can avoid a hard landing" [1]. A decade of institutional decay and hyperfactionalism within the conservative establishment had created space for "a loose coalition of pragmatists to gain a tenuous foothold" [1]. The IRGC's four-decade-old regional strategy had "effectively collapsed" after the October 2023 regional war and the twelve-day Israel-Iran exchange the previous June [1]. Collapse of the strategy was not collapse of the institution: the IRGC "remains a formidable power center" through its hold on the energy sector, internal security, telecommunications, and the defense industry [1]. The pragmatists had become the regime's most important brokers, working to prevent further systemic destabilization [1]. Washington's objective in pursuing military options, Bokhari assessed, "would be to induce a domestic political realignment in [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html), using targeted strikes to weaken hardliners and create space for pragmatists to accept concessions" [1]. Strikes on IRGC command centers and offensive assets "could potentially achieve the intended effect" [1], though both formulations were hedged in the source—a rationale under consideration, not a prediction of success [1]. The war began on February 28, before the internal dynamics Bokhari was tracking had time to resolve [3].

[Phillips O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) delivered the bluntest verdict: "The US militarily lost this war almost from the moment it started—which is a historic failure" [2]. The United States bombed girls' schools, failed to protect its own facilities, and achieved no meaningful strategic objectives [2]. The sinking of the Iranian Navy is "a derisory achievement and if anything might help the Iranian Navy going forward" [2]. O'Brien catalogued 17 advanced aircraft lost or damaged (fighters, AWACS, special forces helicopters), bases hit extensively with damage costing billions, and approximately six weeks of fighting burning through years—in some cases decades—of military production, a depletion confirmed by a [CSIS]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/csis.html) report documenting what was used and how long it takes to rebuild each system [3]. There is evidence, O'Brien wrote, "that the profound corruption and intellectual and organizational poverty of the Trump administration has percolated down to the military. It would certainly be surprising if it had not" [3]. Iranian drones penetrated American defenses, killing at least six soldiers in [Kuwait]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/kuwait.html); three F-15E aircraft were shot down in a single friendly-fire incident over Kuwait; in Bahrain, a lone Iranian drone destroyed a Fifth Fleet radar unit, Iranian equipment that cost perhaps $30,000 devastating a piece of U.S. military hardware estimated to be worth tens of millions of dollars [4]. [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html)'s campaign had deliberately spurned traditional partners, and O'Brien compared the erosion to Roman imperial decline, where "the Roman army still looked formidable for years afterward" but "fought as hard as ever, but less effectively" [4]. Trump declared the strikes were conducted "in honor of" three servicemen killed in action—a formulation that substitutes faux-testimonial sentiment for strategic logic [57]. The "honor" framing is symptomatic of an institutional culture that celebrates the qualities that do not win wars (see [Revolution in Military Affairs]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/revolution-in-military-affairs.html)) [57].

[Aurelien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/aurelien.html) classified the war as "a classic example of surprise-at-failure, which is one of the different types of surprise" [21]. The outbreak itself was scarcely surprising: US leaders had threatened Iran for at least a decade, the methods were predictable, and [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html)'s participation was expected [21]. The surprise came because "the overall strategic situation was read wrongly by those who made the decisions" [21]. The dynamic Aurelien identifies is backwards logic from desired outcome to necessary false assumptions: since a ground invasion was infeasible, all faith had to be placed in an air campaign, but the campaign could only succeed if the Iranian state was very fragile, if main state targets could be reliably hit, and if the Iranian people would rise up once the state was weakened—"thus, the wished-for political outcome generated a series of consecutive necessary false assumptions about the likely effectiveness of bombing and the fragility of the Iranian state which had to be true, otherwise the objective would need to be abandoned. Therefore the assumptions were true. Except of course they weren't" [21]. The capability of the Iranian military was not properly understood, and the objectives of the Iranian state were wrongly evaluated [21]. Unlike the Americans, the Iranians were not surprised: "This was what they had expected and, since the objective was survival and then retaliation, they had developed a capability to pursue it" [21]. Aurelien places this pattern alongside Operation Barbarossa (1941), the Iraq invasion (2003), and the Falklands (1982) as cases where the wished-for political outcome generated a chain of necessary false assumptions about military effectiveness [21].

The West cannot understand the Iran conflict, Aurelien argues, because its strategic concepts are outdated and its ruling class lacks military literacy [54]. The "escort" paradigm for Hormuz epitomizes this doctrinal obsolescence: the concept derives from World War-era anti-submarine warfare, when grouping merchant vessels with escorting warships reduced losses from U-boats, but today's threat is swarms of drones and missiles that cannot be defended against at scale—escorts provide more targets, not protection (see [Hormuz Toll System]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/hormuz-toll-system.html)) [54]. The purpose of attack is not necessarily to sink ships but to discourage sailing—a political and economic effect, not a kinetic one [54]. [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) holds the initiative and the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) cannot win [54]. Western failure resulted from arrogance, inherited military folklore, and a simple disinclination to study Iranian military capability [54]. The most likely outcome is prolonged low-level tension that suits Iran and strains Western cohesion—and it will not be the West that decides when it stops [54].

O'Brien's diagnosis extends to the war's origins: Trump's decision to attack Iran was not in the national interest and was made without political pressure to do so, a finding that directly challenges the assumptions of realism [2]. In O'Brien's formulation, "wars are not the choice of state actors doing what is in the national interest; they are almost always wars of 'choice' that are decided due to the particular views of individual leaders who can have very different views" [2]. By May 2026, Trump was "desperate to get out of this war" but unable to, with the emerging Iran deal worse than the JCPOA [19]. Trump called on Arab states to join the Abraham Accords—in O'Brien's guess, to appease Netanyahu and GOP hardliners expressing dismay at the prospect of a deal with Iran [19]. China was backing Iran, sending missile components and supporting Iran's ceasefire positions diplomatically [19]. Trump spoke about the Taiwan arms sale as a "bargaining chip"—"It's a very good negotiating chip for us, frankly"—leaving democratic [Taiwan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/taiwan.html) twisting in the wind while [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) supported its friend Iran (see [Taiwan Standoff]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/taiwan-standoff.html)) [19]. There was no rally-round-the-flag effect: US society remains locked in a partisan death match, one side doing whatever Trump wants while the other opposed the war from the start—"old patriotic ideas are gone" [2]. The gap between stated objectives and observable outcomes has introduced uncertainty about the credibility and predictability of US policy, shaping how both partners and adversaries will assess future American commitments [28]. The fracturing of Gulf cohesion and the economic volatility generated by the strait's disruption are consequences that will define the strategic environment long after any agreement is reached [28].

Pape assesses that states gaining leverage rarely hurry to end wars; Tehran believes time increasingly favors Iran, and Iran is no longer negotiating to stop losing but to win more—having gained Hormuz as a source of leverage that no paper deal can diminish, since it is built on new military realities of the power of mass drones [5]. Iran's network of proxies and surrogates, built over decades, allows Tehran to project power and coerce neighbors at relatively low cost [29]. By his own words, Trump admitted the United States is in "full-blown reaction-mode," granting Iran control over the tempo of both military operations and peace negotiations [68]. The current dynamic from the US side is emotionally driven and self-defeating [68].

The outcome of the war aligns with Iran's strategy all along—not a failure of execution by the US and Israel but a successful implementation of a twenty-year campaign plan [28]. That plan absorbed the opening strikes and converted survival into leverage [28]. Iran fought a different war than the one it was offered: rather than contesting air superiority symmetrically, it moved the contest onto terrain its adversaries were not equipped to contest—maritime coercion, economic disruption, information dominance, and political will [28]. The doctrine behind this has a name: mosaic defense, formalized in 2005 by the IRGC's Center for Strategy under Mohammad Ali Jafari and the ideologue Hassan Abbasi—a decentralized command structure designed to survive exactly the kind of decapitation strikes that opened this war [28]. Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi stated explicitly: "We've had two decades to study defeats of the U.S. military to our immediate east and west. We've incorporated lessons accordingly" [28]. Iran's theory of victory was simple: survive long enough to impose costs by striking the Gulf States and playing the Hormuz card [28]. Iran had studied the force structure it faced and designed its counter-campaign accordingly, targeting not strike aircraft but the tankers, radar networks, communications nodes, and command platforms that sustained them [28]. The cost exchange told its own story: Iran's drones and missiles cost thousands of dollars apiece; the interceptors fired to stop them ran into millions [28]. Tehran spent five years building an economy to evade sanctions—its tanker fleet grew from roughly 70 vessels in 2020 to nearly 550 by 2025, sustained by Chinese refineries that alone accounted for close to 90 percent of Iran's foreign oil sales [28].

Iran's kinetic targeting of Gulf Cooperation Council countries served a strategic purpose: demonstrating that supporting the US campaign has a price [28]. When Iran struck the UAE's oil terminal, Kuwait and [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html) closed their airspace to US military aircraft [28]. [Project Freedom Collapse]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/project-freedom-collapse.html)—Trump's tanker escort plan—collapsed when Saudi Arabia refused basing, citing lack of confidence in US strategy coherence [28]. Pape assesses that Iran may have discovered that controlled instability is more profitable than total closure: "limited attacks, periodic disruptions, shipping uncertainty, drone incidents, insurance volatility, and the constant possibility of escalation may already be enough to sustain a large geopolitical risk premium in global energy markets" [16]. Iran no longer needs to close Hormuz completely to achieve leverage; intermittent instability may prove sufficient [16]. If oil remains above $90, Iran's fiscal position strengthens while Western economies absorb the inflationary cost [16].

Two theories of victory clashed: the US and Israel wanted surrender and Iran's collapse; Iran wanted survival [28]. Tehran's narrower objective gave it the capacity to keep imposing costs and a negotiating position strong enough to turn resilience into political leverage [28]. The asymmetry extended to the negotiating sequence: before Iran has to resolve a single hard issue—the nuclear stockpile, the missile program, or its regional proxies—the United States lifts the naval blockade, withdraws forces, issues sanctions waivers, and makes frozen assets available [28]. "Pay first, Verify later is not a strategy. It is leverage leaving the room" [28]. Washington has stopped bombing, but Iran has not been forced to surrender its leverage over Hormuz; Iranian resistance has constrained the further use of American force—deterrence working in a direction Washington did not anticipate [92]. The restraint that lowers the risk of an imminent spectacular Iranian attack simultaneously allows a regional balance of power to shift without US participation in the balancing process [92]. The driving question is not merely that Iran is gaining leverage, but who is gaining relative power while the United States is not acting [92].


## The Energy War: Operations and Market Dynamics

The [Middle East]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html) energy front traced a temporal cascade — from the initial Hormuz shutdown through borrowed stabilization to structural bottlenecks in refining and insurance, and ultimately to a food-price transmission. For the IRGC and possibly for many Iranians, this was an existential war — regime survival and national sovereignty at stake — while for the United States, save for the nuclear program, it was not [67]. This asymmetry meant [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) could absorb far higher costs than the Americans and maintain political will long after domestic support for the war had eroded in the US [67]. In Vietnam, Iraq, and Afghanistan, none of the wars were existential for the United States, yet each was treated as existential by the adversary [67]. Friedman's theory is that Iran is prepared for two negotiating options: an agreement with Washington on terms more favorable to Iran, predicated on domestic political considerations at home, or a prolonged war, with Washington ultimately ending it as it did in Vietnam, Afghanistan and Iraq [67]. The Strait of Hormuz itself would not "return to normal" without either a deal that ends Iranian control or a US-led military operation that forces the strait open to prevent Iranian control [66]. Iran could maintain its stranglehold for as long as it was willing to attack, harass, and threaten shipping there; protraction served Iran's interests by normalizing its de facto control of transit through the strait [66]. The Iranians are likely aware of that fact, which is one of the reasons they are stalling and delaying the negotiations process [66]. Iran never achieved sea control in the Strait of Hormuz; it did not need to — it needed only to make transit uninsurable and unprofitable [38]. Within days, crude oil exports fell 95% and LNG 99% — the largest supply disruption in the history of the oil market, in the International Energy Agency's judgement — achieved with technologically unsophisticated means: sea mines, short-range missiles, swarming fast boats, and enough demonstrated resolve to convince insurers, shipowners, and energy markets that transit was not worth the risk [38].

### The Week-by-Week Cascade

Tanker traffic through the strait crashed from 40 vessels per day transiting in January to a single tanker making the trip on March 3, according to Vortexa data [7]. Iranian drone strikes forced [Qatar]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/qatar.html)'s QatarEnergy to halt production on March 2, taking one-fifth of global LNG export capacity offline in an instant [7]. [Egypt]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/egypt.html) faced a potentially existential crisis in a country wholly dependent on Israeli and Gulf Arab energy imports [7]. [Europe]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/europe.html)'s gas storage was already at a 5-year low, with [France]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/france.html) and [Germany]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/germany.html) hitting record lows [7]. Diesel and heating oil prices jumped almost 42% in just a few days, far outpacing increases in crude oil prices — the diesel and jet fuel crisis had begun [7]. Gulf countries affected by the blockade had exported 14 million barrels of crude and 6 million barrels of refined products through the strait; the world market was now in a 10 million barrel shortfall in crude and a full 6 million in refined fuels — 16% of all liquid fuels produced and consumed worldwide [8]. Pipeline diversion to Yanbu on [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html)'s [Red Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/red-sea.html) coast increased to around 2.5 million barrels per day from 786,000 in February, but was nowhere near enough [8]. Meanwhile Iran apparently was still able to ship its oil: loading about 1.5 million barrels of crude a day, with [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) receiving about 1.25 million barrels daily, Kpler data show [8]. The Bab-el-Mandeb chokepoint remained under Houthi control, who could resume the blockade at any time [8]. Operation Prosperity Guardian, launched in December 2023 to unblock the waterway, was confirmed a failure — "a colossal blunder" in the words of the National Security Institute at George Mason University [8]. Insurance companies were unlikely to offer policies and shipping companies unlikely to risk their crews and vessels [8].

### The Borrowed Calm: Three Buffers and the Price-Physical Divergence

Tchakarova identified three buffers absorbing the Hormuz shock simultaneously [14]. The American buffer: record crude exports averaging 5.35 million barrels per day in May, plus a 66-million-barrel SPR drawdown, stocks at 349 million approaching 1983 lows [14]. The Chinese buffer: [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html)'s 7.8 million barrels per day against its 2025 average of 11.6 represented a 33 percent demand gap filled by inventory draws, not absent consumption [14]. The Russian buffer: Moscow forced to cut exports as Ukrainian strikes crippled its refining system [14]. The calm was real — and it was engineered, temporary, and depleting; each buffer was finite: the SPR approached 1983 lows, Chinese inventories drew down daily, Russia's export decline removed supply structurally [14]. The world was not balanced; it was borrowed [14].

By early July, Brent had fallen from a wartime peak above $180 to around $72, with forward curves moving into contango [22]. Persian Gulf production remained roughly 45% below its pre-war level, regional exports sat around 25% below February levels, and shipping companies remained reluctant to send vessels back through Hormuz [22]. The price decline was driven by four distinct offsets working simultaneously: physical rerouting; coordinated reserve releases of more than 300 million barrels — the largest emergency drawdown in history; China demand destruction, with crude imports falling roughly 40%, close to five million barrels a day; and broader demand erosion, with global oil demand contracting an estimated 5.5 million barrels a day in Q2 2026 [22]. Full restoration of export capacity was expected to take many months, with Qatar's damaged LNG facilities potentially requiring years [22]. Shipping companies remained wary of sending vessels back into the strait given unresolved insurance costs, lingering concerns about sea mines, and uncertainty over the durability of the ceasefire framework itself [22]. Scenario modeling assigned roughly 50% probability to a fragile equilibrium (oil $70–85 while the memorandum holds despite recurring friction); 30% to a renewed rupture (a serious incident sends prices back toward $100+); and 20% to durable stabilization (a lasting settlement allows prices to settle near pre-war levels) [22]. The price collapse was best read as evidence that global demand management and reserve coordination could now absorb shocks that would have been unmanageable a decade ago — but Iran's retained leverage over Hormuz meant the underlying rupture had not closed, only been priced as if it had [22].

### The Refining Bottleneck

Krugman argued that, to oversimplify, a true reopening of the Strait would have made more crude oil available to the global economy, but that wouldn't have done the global economy much good in the short run, because the world didn't have the capacity to turn that crude into usable products [30]. Some of this shortage reflected the loss of refined products formerly exported from the Persian Gulf, but a big factor was the ongoing [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html): Blas suggests that around 10% of world refining capacity is now out of operation [30] — a figure corroborated by the IEA, which reported nearly 3 million bpd of Middle East refining capacity shut down, and by Kpler's Matt Smith, who put the global refinery cutback at 9 million bpd, more than 10% of global capacity [42]. The effective consumer price of oil rose approximately $75/barrel during the war, while crude rose only $45 — high consumer prices kept demand depressed even when crude prices nearly normalized, because a Hormuz reopening would add crude but not usable fuel [30]. China's refinery runs crashed to pandemic lows as crude imports collapsed by more than 40% in June year-on-year [42]. Ultra-low sulfur diesel reached $168 per barrel against ~$80 crude [42]. Russian diesel exports collapsed from 800,000 bpd to zero under the Ukrainian drone campaign [42]. The [SPR depletion crisis]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/spr-depletion-crisis.html) saw the drawdown rate fall from ~1.3 million bpd in mid-June to 0.43 million by July 10, trending toward zero by early August [42]. Basing economic or military decisions on crude prices alone — in a world stripped of refining capacity — had become an outright civilizational risk [42].

## The Escalation Trap: From Ceasefire Collapse to Exit Criterion

### The Ceasefire Collapses and the Trap Becomes Self-Reinforcing

The sort-of ceasefire with Iran was called off — [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) has reinstated the blockade, and the Iranians are back to hitting things with their drones and missiles [31][36] ([Hormuz Ceasefire Collapse 2026]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/hormuz-ceasefire-collapse-2026.html)). Pape argues that the ceasefire collapse is not simply another round of strikes but evidence that the conflict has entered a fundamentally new phase — the Escalation Trap is becoming self-reinforcing [32][33]. Both governments are becoming politically invested in objectives that cannot both be achieved at the same time [32][33]. Pape describes Trump wanting to "take control of the Strait of Hormuz and charge a 20% fee," with carrier redeployment per CENTCOM, as a shift from punishment to reversing the defeat [33]. On the Iranian side, the Supreme Leader gave a statement pledging to avenge the blood of the leaders killed and all the martyrs — "It cannot be manipulated. You have the Supreme Leader legitimating it" [33]. Pape identifies "two separate engines of escalation" — even if one of these engines slows, the other can drive escalation for both [33]. This is not temporary rhetoric but a structural feature of the conflict [33].

Three pressures converge on Trump's decision calculus. The first is the Hoover risk: Trump signed the MoU to avoid an economic crisis, explicitly saying he did not want to be "Herbert Hoover," but the MoU failed to stop the oil inventory drawdown — the world gained only about 14 days of supply from a couple hundred million barrels released [33]. The SPR salt caverns face a structural risk: at low levels, water eats away the cavern walls causing collapse — "We're getting into new territory" [33]. The second is MAGA turnout: according to the Reagan Institute's Summer Survey, 51% of self-identified MAGA Republicans favor replacing Iran's government; only 25% prefer a negotiated settlement [35]. If Trump fails to open Hormuz, a significant portion of MAGA will not show up at the polls [33][35]. The third is the revenge spiral itself: Pape notes that a previous Supreme Leader's call to action led to attacks and deaths for decades, and draws on 25 years of suicide terrorism research — 95% of suicide attacks are done to coerce democracies to withdraw military forces from territory [33]. Every US bombing campaign kills more Iranians who are then portrayed as martyrs, strengthening the legitimacy of the revenge call; if even one limited revenge attack succeeds, public pressure for decisive retaliation would intensify, giving Trump even stronger incentives to authorize larger operations including ground options [33]. Iran's political objectives have expanded through three phases: Iran is moving aggressively from survival to ambition to now revenge [33].

### The Civilian-Infrastructure Threshold

Iran crossed another threshold: instead of concentrating on military bases, Iranian missile and drone strikes hit a vital power and water desalination plant in [Kuwait]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/kuwait.html) — part of the system that produces roughly 90% of the country's drinking water; unlike past fragments that had inadvertently hit desalination plants, this plant was directly targeted [49]. Pape identifies the strike as a categorical escalation: civilian survival infrastructure is becoming a direct battlefield target [49]. Drawing on his Bombing to Win research, Pape's central finding is that when civilians suddenly lose access to life's basic necessities, survival rapidly overwhelms every other concern — instead of rallying around the flag, people become consumed by the immediate struggle to keep themselves and their families alive: "anger comes after survival" [49]. The strike proves the concept for a vulnerability pattern that extends across the Gulf: [Qatar]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/qatar.html) and Bahrain each rely on only three to five desalination plants, while [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html) and the [UAE]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/uae.html) rely on 30 and 70 respectively [49] ([Geoeconomics of Water]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/geoeconomics-of-water.html)). Fowler argues that escalating military strikes on energy facilities and civilian infrastructure would serve only to alienate the Iranian population and distract from the Iranian leadership's appalling incompetence, strengthening the regime — the opposite of the intended political effect [68]. The current MoU must be abandoned and recast in a more evenhanded manner, leading to permanent peace; adhering to it as currently written will only ensure we will be forced to return in the future [68].

Trump's declaration that Iranians "have not suffered enough yet" signals a potential shift from military-denial targeting to civilian-punishment targeting — bridges and electrical power infrastructure whose principal purpose is not military denial but mass suffering [62]. The technical mechanics are stark: destroying transformers and transmission substations can produce widespread blackouts but is often repaired within days or weeks, while destroying the generating units themselves produces prolonged outages lasting months or years, since these are custom-engineered, weigh hundreds of tons, and are not kept as spare parts [62]. For Tehran — a city of roughly ten million experiencing July temperatures near 100°F — the consequences of a prolonged blackout would be catastrophic: water pumps serving 800 million gallons daily would fail within hours, and roughly one-quarter of perishable food would be lost when cold chains collapse [62]. The 1995 Chicago heat wave killed 739 people in five days with 2.7 million residents; a one-to-three-month blackout in Tehran would produce casualties on a far greater scale [62]. History strongly suggests that inflicting massive civilian suffering through electric power targeting rarely produces rapid political capitulation when governments believe their survival is at stake [62].

### The Nuclear Dimension: Pickaxe Mountain and the Ground Power Dilemma

[Israeli]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html) intelligence reported that Iran had moved thousands of uranium-enrichment centrifuges into deeply fortified tunnels beneath Pickaxe Mountain near Natanz, buried 300–450 feet under solid rock — beyond the reach of existing conventional bunker-busting munitions [59] ([United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html)). Airpower did not solve the strategic problem — it transformed it: if Israeli intelligence is correct, Iran responded not by surrendering its nuclear program but by moving its surviving enrichment capability deeper underground [59]. Pape frames this as "the Ground Power Dilemma": if Washington concludes Pickaxe Mountain has become Iran's protected nuclear sanctuary, the choice narrows to tolerating a hardened nuclear program or accepting far greater risks of operations beyond an air campaign [59]. Drawing on the 2003 Iraq WMD precedent, Pape argues that leaders escalate based on what they believe adversaries are capable of, not what later proves true — uncertainty favors escalation [59].

### Spiral Phase Oscillation: The Illusion of Control

Pape's Breaking Analysis #10 (August 3, 2026) reframes [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html)'s abrupt cancellation of planned strikes on Iran as not de-escalation but oscillation [78] ([Middle East]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html)). Iranian officials denied any negotiations were underway, and the structural indicators continued to deteriorate: commercial tanker traffic through Hormuz had slowed to an estimated 80% drop in volume, with only one inbound large crude carrier arriving in the past week [78] ([Red Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/red-sea.html)). The Bab el-Mandeb remained under severe pressure as unofficial Houthi blockades forced multiple commercial vessels to turn back [78]. State Department security alerts warned American citizens throughout the Middle East to prepare for "unexpected escalation" and consider departing the region [78]. In the Spiral Phase of the Escalation Trap, tactical pauses are not off-ramps — they are moments when political leaders search for alternatives because the available military options have become costlier while the underlying strategic problem remains unresolved [78]. Airpower has not restored secure commercial transit through Hormuz, has not broken Iran's regional leverage, and has not produced a political settlement; the failure on all three measures creates an increasingly difficult ground power dilemma for Washington [78]. The American political calendar is shifting incentives in ways that favor Iran, which treats time itself as a weapon: a pause without a political settlement allows Tehran to keep consolidating its emerging security resistance belt stretching from the Mediterranean through the Red Sea to the Persian Gulf [78] ([Iran Security Resistance Belt]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/iran-security-resistance-belt.html), [Escalation Theory Critique]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/escalation-theory-critique.html)). The indicators to watch are not presidential rhetoric but Hormuz and Red Sea commercial traffic, Chinese purchases of discounted Iranian oil, and tightening global oil and refined-product inventories [78].

### The Risk-Pricing Reframe and Legislative Escalation

Pape's August 5 analysis reframes the negotiating surface from shipping to risk: the 7% cargo-value fee under discussion is materially identical to the approximately 7% of cargo value that marine insurers currently charge for Persian Gulf voyages — meaning the rumored deal institutionalizes the wartime-risk premium rather than eliminating it [85]. A typical VLCC carries roughly 2 million barrels of crude worth about $160 million at $80 per barrel; even if only a fraction of prewar Gulf exports resumed, a 7% levy applied across Hormuz traffic could generate well over $100 billion annually — on the order of $140 billion under prewar shipping assumptions — what Pape terms "one of the largest geopolitical wealth transfers in modern history" [85]. It is difficult to imagine any American president accepting a permanent arrangement that effectively institutionalizes Iranian geopolitical leverage over the world's most important energy chokepoint [85]. Pape's base case is that either no agreement is reached, or any deal that leaves the chokepoint-fee issue unresolved is fragile from the outset [85]. A genuine breakthrough requires bilateral public confirmation; as of writing, Iranian officials dismiss US assertions of bilateral negotiations as "repeated lies" and "desperate cries," maintaining that Tehran engages only via regional mediators [85].

On August 7, Iran's parliament began reviewing the "Strategic Action for the Security and Sustainable Development of the Strait of Hormuz and the Persian Gulf" bill, barring vessels and cargo linked to the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html), [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html), and other "hostile nations" and imposing transit fees of 5–7% of cargo value [86]. If enacted, Iran would no longer be threatening to control the strait — it would be legislating that control as the normal operating rule for international commerce [86]. Pape frames this as a profound strategic shift from military coercion to institutionalized maritime authority: air power alone rarely compels a determined adversary to abandon what it regards as a core national interest — it can destroy military capabilities, raise economic costs, and delay strategic programs, but it seldom changes the opponent's underlying political objective [86]. The legislation connects to what Pape calls the "Hail Mary" ground war threshold: as costs to Americans rise from Iran's sustained Hormuz control, a president with little to lose may gamble on a limited ground operation for symbolic victory before the midterms [86]. The 600-mile Iranian coastline cannot be reliably controlled from the air; territory ultimately requires forces on the ground [86].

### Stage Four: Strategic Paralysis and Iran's Victor's-Justice Demands

Pape's August 9 briefing introduces Stage Four of the Escalation Trap: strategic paralysis — if the US remains in paralysis, doing nothing while Iran consolidates, Iran becomes the rising regional hegemon in the Persian Gulf, the fourth center of world power [87]. Stage Four would see Iran escalate to toppling [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html)'s MBS, organizing assassination attempts against MBS and [UAE]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/uae.html) leaders, and projecting power through the security resistance belt from the Mediterranean through the Red Sea to the Gulf [87][78] ([Iran Security Resistance Belt]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/iran-security-resistance-belt.html)). The period of maximum leverage runs through January 2027: Iran's objective is to "drive a stake through the heart of the Trump presidency" by wrecking the US economy, not merely wounding it temporarily [87]. Iran's Supreme National Security Council has issued six demands before Hormuz reopens — terms Pape reads as victor's justice, not bargaining: approximately $300 billion for damages, over $100 billion in frozen assets, and ending Israel's occupation of Palestine (not negotiating — ending) [87]. Iran is "playing balance-of-power politics like a black belt" while the US behaves "like green belts" — a persistent analytical error of underestimating Iran [87].

The [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html)-[Turkey]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/turkey.html)-[Pakistan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/pakistan.html) defense agreement — the "Mecca pact" — fills a power vacuum left by US decline; it is not "a new NATO": it lacks an integrated command structure, Turkey has 18 million Kurds as internal vulnerability, and the pact is defensive in framing [87]. Its emergence confirms the Escalation Trap's prediction: when the dominant power becomes less willing or able to guarantee order, regional states hedge, build coalitions, and diversify security relationships [87]. Beneath the surface of paralysis, military signals accumulate: Trump told 800 generals and admirals at the Pentagon on September 30, 2025 that the US military would treat liberal cities as training grounds and that they could "get on board or get out" [87]. The CENTCOM top air commander's retirement mid-war is an unusual signal; B-1 and B-52 bomber wings have been brought to bases with weekends canceled — Pape warns that what appears to have been paralysis may start to look more like action [87].

### The Exit Criterion and the 2029 Regional-Balance Test

Pape's August 10 essay formalizes the Escalation Trap into a testable theory by defining the escape criterion: tactical success converted into political success [88] ([Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html)). A stable settlement requires Iran to accept constraints because it believes the alternative is worse, while the US and its regional partners accept a balance of power they can sustain; until that conversion happens, any pause in bombing or temporary agreement is just a pause inside the trap [88]. A reduction in violence does not falsify the trap; neither does another temporary agreement [88]. The standard Pape sets for judging US policy is not whether [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) can defeat Iran, but whether by January 2029 he has left behind a regional system — one that includes [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html), the [UAE]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/uae.html), [Turkey]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/turkey.html), and [Pakistan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/pakistan.html) — more stable than the one the US entered in 2026 [88]. The goal is a regional balance in which Iran has enough power and incentive to accept balance rather than continue trying to expand its leverage, and in which Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Turkey, Pakistan, and other regional powers have enough capability to constrain Iran without requiring permanent American military dominance [88]. The choice is not between "victory" and "withdrawal" — it is between continuing to escalate without a credible theory of political victory and using remaining American leverage to construct something that can actually last [88]. Falsification conditions are explicit: Pape would change his assessment if sustained Iranian military degradation produced genuine political concessions, if Tehran accepted a durable settlement addressing the central disputes, and if Washington could reduce military pressure without triggering another cycle of Iranian escalation [88]. The war is Trump's "Hurricane Katrina": the political sequence — war → energy shock → inflation and shortages → economic pain → political blame → pressure to escalate → deeper strategic failure — is one Pape has been warning about [88].


## The Ceasefire Collapse and the Thirteen-Day Campaign

The thirteen days from July 11 to July 23, 2026 traced an arc from a dead ceasefire through escalating mutual strikes to a projected endgame in which neither side could claim victory — documented day by day by [O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) and framed by a widening circle of analysts watching the campaign outrun Washington's ability to control it.

Donald Trump's fantasy MOU/ceasefire was revealed to be a sham: the [MOU]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/iran-ceasefire-mou.html) he constructed with Iran was never a basis for an agreement, because Iran gave up nothing in it [50]. As the truth dawned that Iran had no intention of making concessions on its nuclear program or its control of the Strait of Hormuz, Trump faced a binary choice between admitting the greatest strategic defeat in US history or reigniting military operations in a desperate attempt to see if something, anything might make Iran more malleable. He chose the latter [50]. [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) declared the ceasefire formally over on July 8 at the [Ankara Summit]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/nato-ankara-summit-2026.html) [50].

It was, in O'Brien's assessment, an extraordinary gamble: restarting the bombing in a weaker strategic position than when operations ended in April, with the midterm elections approaching and 60–66% of Americans opposing the war [50]. On July 11, Trump posted on Truth Social that the US military was "ready, willing, and able, for a one year period of time, subject to extension, to completely decimate and destroy all areas of Iran" [50]. O'Brien juxtaposes this against Trump's own celebration of the assassination of Khamenei at the start of the war — the same policy of leader assassination now makes Trump himself a legitimate target for Iran [50].

### Five Consecutive Days (July 11–16)

O'Brien compiled a day-by-day list of military operations launched by both sides over the first five days [50]:

- **July 11:** CENTCOM claimed approximately 140 targets and set terms to end hostilities. The IRGC struck the container ship GFS Galaxy in Hormuz and declared the strait closed.
- **July 12:** US strikes on Qeshm Island, Bandar Abbas, and Hajiabad; first use of one-way attack drones this campaign. Iran fired its widest missile/drone volley yet at UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain, Oman, and Jordan.
- **July 13:** Five-hour US strike wave on Bushehr, Chah Bahar, Jask, Konarak, Abu Musa, Bandar Abbas. IRGC missiles struck two UAE-flagged tankers in Omani waters — one Indian crew member killed, several injured; the oil price spiked almost 10%.
- **July 14:** Third consecutive night of coastal strikes; Trump reinstated the naval blockade and threatened infrastructure next.
- **July 15:** Seven-hour fourth-night strike wave plus a separate round on Greater Tunb Island; an empty tanker was disabled with Hellfire missiles for ignoring the blockade.
- **July 16:** Fifth consecutive day of strikes; Trump reportedly weighing — not yet ordered — ground operations and bombing an underground site linked to Iran's nuclear program.

### The Escalation Arc

[Heather Cox Richardson]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/heather-cox-richardson.html) relayed the decision-making behind the strikes: on Monday, Trump announced he was reimposing a blockade on Iran and that the U.S. would become "THE GUARDIAN OF THE HORMUZ STRAIT"; the next day he reversed course, claiming that Gulf allies told him they would rather invest directly in the U.S. than pay tolls [44]. Barak Ravid of Axios, who often has inside information from the White House, reported that Trump had convened a Situation Room meeting with his top national security team to discuss "new plans for devastating strikes" against Iran [44]. Trump told the Fox News Channel: "next week comes the power plants. Next week comes the bridges. We're gonna knock out all their power plants. We're gonna knock out all their bridges unless they get to the table and negotiate" [44]. Nate Swanson, a former member of Trump's negotiating team, told foreign policy specialist Laura Rozen of *Diplomatic* that the escalation was probably a ploy to kick-start further negotiations — "a very risky and low probability gamble, but nothing else makes sense" [44].

The campaign opened a second-strait dimension. Reuters reported that Iranian leaders have asked the Houthis they back in Yemen to close the [Bab el-Mandeb]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/red-sea.html) strait [45]. The Strait of Hormuz and the Bab el-Mandeb are the two main routes for oil exports from the Middle East; the closure of the second strait would exacerbate energy shortages even as the [U.S. oil reserve]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/spr-depletion-crisis.html) drops to its lowest level since 1983 [45].

O'Brien published a "highly speculative" likelihood assessment: 99% that Iran has made no meaningful concession whatsoever; 98.5% that [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) and [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) are both benefitting significantly from the week's war; 90% that oil markets continue to believe Trump will "TACO"; 80% that Trump's threats to use US ground troops are hot air; 66% that Trump still needs a deal by the midterms; 99.9% that the [US]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) is haemorrhaging allies and partners [50]. Trump's "desperate attempt" to restart bombing after his "fantasy MOU" was revealed a sham had reignited [his war]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/hormuz-ceasefire-collapse-2026.html) [50].

### The Aqaba Pipeline Crisis

[Robert Pape]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/robert-pape.html) identifies a new escalation vector: Iran's threat to Aqaba, [Jordan's]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/jordan.html) only coastal city on the northern tip of the Red Sea [51]. The US Embassy in Amman issued an emergency alert advising Americans to avoid Aqaba's airport and seaport due to a specific and credible threat, and air raid sirens went off in Jordan warning of incoming Iranian missiles [51]. The threat follows a Washington meeting between Iraq's prime minister and Jordan's foreign minister to accelerate the Basra-Aqaba pipeline — a 1,600-kilometer, $18 billion project designed to carry 2.25 million barrels per day from southern [Iraq]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iraq.html) to the Red Sea [51]. The Iran war had cut southern Iraqi exports by approximately 70%, exposing Baghdad's dependence on the Strait of Hormuz and making the bypass pipeline a strategic imperative [51]. Under the Escalation Trap framework, Pape argues that Iran has powerful incentives to strike any infrastructure that could permanently reduce its coercive leverage: a functioning Basra-Aqaba pipeline would undercut Iran's [security resistance belt]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/iran-security-resistance-belt.html) by creating a permanent alternative outlet for Iraqi oil [51].

### The Inconclusive Contest

Davidson (GPF) frames the renewed fighting as an unequal but ultimately inconclusive contest. Washington can weaken Iran's ability to threaten shipping but cannot cheaply guarantee normal commercial passage or force Iran to surrender its remaining leverage without accepting a prolonged campaign [43]. If Iran cannot withstand the campaign indefinitely, it can preserve enough uncertainty to deny the US a clean political victory. Washington must provide security continuously; Iran needs only intermittent success [43]. Iran's margin for victory is shrinking as US strikes degrade the military systems needed to sustain disruption and the reinstated [naval blockade]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/us-naval-blockade-iran.html) constrains seaborne oil exports [43]. Iran's strongest remaining escalation option is also the one most likely to unite its adversaries: attacking Gulf energy infrastructure or expanding maritime disruption to the Bab el-Mandeb strait — either of which could end the Gulf states' role as mediators, deepen their military cooperation with Washington, and bring [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html) back into the campaign [43]. The US strike on the Aq Taqeh Khan railway bridge in Aqqala County — on a corridor linking Iran to [Central Asia]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/central-asia.html) and onward to [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) and [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) — demonstrated that pressure would not remain confined to the waterways [43]. The likely outcome is a truce rather than a settlement: Washington can keep winning the strikes, and Iran can keep denying Washington a clean victory, but neither can sustain that contradiction indefinitely [43].

### Eleven Nights and Sideways Escalation

By July 22, O'Brien documents the continuation of US strikes through eleven consecutive nights [58]. The US has no actual military operation to achieve what it wants, beyond a full invasion and deposition of the Iranian regime [58]. General Barry McCaffrey estimates a full invasion would require 600,000 troops and one year — a commitment that, as O'Brien notes, is not happening [58]. [Iran's]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) response has been what O'Brien calls a form of "sideways" escalation: rather than attacking only US forces, many of Iran's attacks over the previous ten days have focussed on the resources of US allies in the region [58]. What they need to do is make the war so politically costly for the US that Iran emerges from the war in a stronger position [58]. The timeline from July 12–21 documents this widening pattern of strikes across Qatar, UAE, Bahrain, [Kuwait]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/kuwait.html), [Jordan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/jordan.html), [Oman]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/oman.html), and [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html) — including desalination infrastructure, air bases, and oil tankers [58]. The IRGC also claimed to have hit an Amazon data center in Bahrain; this report is still awaiting verification [58]. Iran hit Jordan's Muwaffaq Salti Air Base in Azraq, killing at least two US service members, and a ballistic missile struck a US base in Saudi Arabia — the first Iranian attack on Saudi Arabia in over three months [58].

### The Thirteen-Day Campaign Endgame

On July 23, the US launched its last day of direct attacks in this phase — thirteen consecutive nights of strikes that achieved no strategic gains while further burning through critical munitions [76]. The White House leaked the stockpile depletion problem even as Ambassador Mike Waltz publicly contradicted himself on *Meet the Press*: first blaming the Biden administration for the munitions shortfall, then denying it existed and saying the military had "everything that it needs," then pivoting again to admit the shortfall by saying those leaking the information should go to jail [76]. Trump's threats of large reinforcements were, in O'Brien's assessment, always a bluff: the US has already burned through the advanced systems that can be fired from a distance, and escalating with ground forces would blow through what remains of its air defense stockpiles in a very short time [76]. After the US halted strikes on July 23, [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) continued attacking — either directly or through its proxies — demonstrating that no ceasefire existed [76]. O'Brien's framing is blunt: "Iran is toying with the USA." Trump wants out, desperately, and Iran will not let him [76].

### Iran's Strategic Framework

O'Brien offers a speculative framework for Iran's strategy, prefacing it: "I have no real idea of what their strategy is, and will describe what it might be simply from observing what is happening" [76]. The framework's **Aims**: control over the Strait of Hormuz, a weakened US military presence in the region, the ability to restart the nuclear program, and the dissolution of the US-led coalition [76]. **Ways**: keep fighting, deplete US munitions, demonstrate US weakness to allies, sustain high oil prices, and delay talks [76]. **Means**: existing cruise and ballistic missiles supplemented by cheap drones and Chinese/Russian deliveries [76]. Iran has calculated that time is on their side: the longer they can draw this out, the closer to November midterms, the more desperate Trump becomes, the stronger Iran's leverage. They can see how unpopular the war is in the USA — arguably now the least popular war in US history [76].

### The Likely Shape of Peace

O'Brien sketches the probable endgame: Iran would issue a non-concession statement of no intent to develop nuclear weapons; Iranian control over the Strait of Hormuz would be tacitly acknowledged, with Iran charging "administrative fees" rather than "tolls"; Iranian assets would be unfrozen, sanctions wound down, and the US would begin a partial withdrawal from the immediate vicinity of Iran; Trump would package it all as a "massive victory" [76]. In O'Brien's judgment: "It will actually represent the quickest and arguably the most decisive defeat in US history" [76].


## Horizontal Escalation: Theater Widening from Gulf to Caspian

Each step outward forced Washington to defend a new piece of the regional system without reducing pressure on any prior front [79].

### The Gulf's Digital Frontier

Even within the Gulf itself, Iran's targeting expanded beyond the energy and military assets that had defined the war's opening phase. Iran struck data centers in the [UAE]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/uae.html) providing Amazon Web Services, causing them to go down for several months at minimum [69]. AI infrastructure is particularly at risk to such a looming threat, with data centers going down for several months at a minimum [69].

### The Red Sea Front

The first step outward came through the Bab el-Mandeb gateway. Within 48 hours of July 16, Yemen's Iran-backed Houthis launched a coordinated barrage of ballistic missiles and drones targeting [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html)'s Abha International Airport in direct retaliation for airstrikes on Sanaa [47]. Pape assessed that this assault heavily risks horizontal escalation that would widen the Iran war by opening a second maritime front at the strategic Bab el-Mandeb gateway [47]. [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) gains additional leverage without directly committing its own forces [47].

[Geopolitical Futures]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/geopolitical-futures.html)' Bokhari reframes the Houthi entry against the prevailing assumption that the group acts as Iran's proxy. The Houthis had avoided escalation throughout the worst crisis the [Iranian]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) regime has faced since its inception — a restraint that revealed their independent calculus [64]. Their central objective is consolidating power as Yemen's dominant political and military force; every move serves that goal, and their belated entry into the Iran war was an opportunistic exploitation of regional instability, not an ideological commitment to Tehran's conflict [64]. Iran supplies resources that bolster Houthi capabilities, but it does not exercise the command and control associated with a traditional proxy relationship; the Houthis retain considerable freedom to operate as they see fit [64]. Houthi participation will likely remain measured — the movement will exploit opportunities created by wider geopolitical confrontation so long as the benefits exceed the military, political and economic costs, and once sustained escalation threatens its domestic standing in Yemen or invites unacceptable retaliation, it can be expected to pull back regardless of Tehran's preferences [64]. The Houthis remember all too well Operation Rough Rider, the 52-day US campaign in 2025 that consisted of more than 1,000 strikes against them [64].

### Crossing into NATO Territory

By late July, the war's geography had crossed another threshold. Tchakarova reports that horizontal escalation had crossed into [NATO]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/organizations/nato.html) territory: Iran has threatened RAF Fairford in the [United Kingdom]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-kingdom.html) over its use for B-1 bomber missions, and NATO air defences engaged Iranian drones and missiles near Incirlik Air Base in [Turkey]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/turkey.html) [65]. The geography of the war has now touched a NATO member state directly — a different order of escalation risk than the Gulf-and-Yemen theatre tracked since February [65]. The [Red Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/red-sea.html) blockade had also produced its first confirmed kinetic strikes on tankers rather than diversions alone: the Houthis claimed missile and drone strikes on two Saudi oil tankers, setting one ablaze [65] — a development tracked in the [Global System Rupture]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-rupture.html) framework as the transition from blockade declaration to enforcement.

### The Two-Front Maritime Trap

[Robert Pape]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/robert-pape.html)'s July 28 analysis reframes the war as a two-front maritime contest. By opening an active [Red Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/red-sea.html) front through its regional partners while keeping Hormuz closed, Iran is creating a two-front war problem in strategically linked theaters; the escape route itself is coming under sustained attack [70].

The three-buffer framework decomposes the ~16 mbpd that offset the ~20 mbpd Hormuz baseline: roughly 3–4 mbpd from SPR releases, roughly 4–5 mbpd from Chinese demand destruction, and roughly 7 mbpd from physical bypass infrastructure — [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html)'s East-West Pipeline ~5 mbpd to Yanbu, Abu Dhabi pipeline ~2 mbpd to Fujairah [70]. Each buffer has a distinct vulnerability profile. Emergency inventories have fallen steadily since early June — from roughly 340 million barrels to just above 310 million by mid-July — while weekly releases have slowed markedly; the reserve remains available, but its ability to offset prolonged supply losses is becoming increasingly constrained as operational limits approach, and world reserve drawdowns will likely drop to half their current rate or less [70]. Earlier in the war, weaker Chinese demand absorbed a significant share of lost Gulf exports, but that cushion is now eroding as [Chinese]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) refiners have accelerated purchases of August and September Russian crude cargoes while domestic demand has strengthened, reducing one of the largest sources of slack in global oil markets; China's reduced demand could fall by a third or more from current levels [70]. Pipeline bypass depends on Red Sea bypass capacity remaining under military pressure [70]. Over 40% of Saudi oil bypassing Hormuz via the Red Sea is not reaching the global market after Houthi attacks, and by the end of August those offsets may have fallen by more than half [70] — a milestone tracked in the [SPR Depletion Crisis]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/spr-depletion-crisis.html). Pape's forecast: "shortages, not just disruption, are about to begin in earnest" [70].

The precedent is sobering. Operation Rough Rider (March 15 – May 6, 2025) — seven weeks, 1,000+ strikes — tactically dominated but only fought the Houthis to a tie; shipping through the Red Sea remains down 60% a year later [70]. If reopening one corridor required months of sustained combat and still failed to restore commercial traffic, analysts should begin by assuming that reopening two corridors simultaneously will take at least as long — and probably longer [70]. The carrier arithmetic underscores the constraint: of America's eleven carriers, only four are deployed worldwide, two are already committed to Iran combat operations, and at most two more could be shifted — producing a maximum of four carriers available to split across both theaters; the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) does not have the deployable forces for two long naval wars [70] — a constraint with direct implications for [Energy and Resources]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/energy-and-resources.html).

[Javier Blas]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/javier-blas.html) frames the bypass problem in pipeline-capacity arithmetic. The Sumed pipeline has a capacity of about 2.5 million barrels a day, roughly half of the current flow from Yanbu; adding the Israeli pipeline would bring an extra 1.2 million barrels a day of transport capacity, narrowing — but not closing — the gap [71]. VLCCs cannot transit Suez fully laden; the required workaround is lightering — supertankers offload part of their cargo into Sumed, transit with reduced draft, and reload on the Mediterranean side [71]. Oil tankers heading to Asia after emerging through Sumed's Mediterranean terminus are routed through the eastern Mediterranean rather than near the Indian Ocean, adding ~25 days to the trip from [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html) to Japan [71].

Two conditions could invalidate the planning assumption: a political settlement — Washington accepts terms that persuade Tehran and its partners to stop threatening shipping, likely full military withdrawal of all US forces and bases from the region (implausible as of today) — or a massive new naval force generation. Absent either, the battles to reopen both the Strait of Hormuz and the [Red Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/red-sea.html) are likely to last for months, not weeks [70] — a timeline tracked in the [Insurance and Trade Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/insurance-and-trade-warfare.html) framework.

### The Dispersal Architecture: Jordan as Target

The next step outward struck not a chokepoint but the architecture that sustains US power projection itself. Pape's Breaking Analysis #8 (July 29, 2026) frames the strike that hit [Jordan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/jordan.html)'s Muwaffaq Salti Air Base as confirming a June 15 "Period of Maximum Leverage" forecast and the entry of [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html)'s war into the spiral phase [72] — an event tracked as the [Iran-Jordan Strike]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/iran-jordan-strike-2026-07-29.html). The IRGC explicitly stated that its Aerospace Force targeted a US military CENTCOM center inside Jordan; all incoming projectiles were successfully intercepted by air defenses, and global oil prices rose 5% [72]. The next ring carried the war past Suez. The outermost ring carried the war into a basin where two geographically distant conflicts converge. Israel's opening of its first permanent embassy in Ashgabat, Turkmenistan, in April 2023 — approximately 20 km from the Iranian border — extended the diplomatic ring around Iran's Caspian flank [80].

## Technology, Cost, and Systemic Effects

[Phillips O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) identifies the core problem at the tactical level: the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) went into the war completely lacking cheaper systems to engage [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) air power, relying on the very pricey Patriots or THAAD air defense systems that cost millions per shot to engage not only cheaper Iranian ballistic missiles but even super cheap Iranian drones — a costly mistake that meant the US could not fight for that long [2]. The broader conclusion O'Brien draws is that the US war machine has been constructed on almost all the wrong principles to wage a modern conflict of any length: not based on industrial strength and cost-benefit analysis, but on highly expensive and technologically advanced systems in small numbers which were thought capable of overwhelming an enemy in early engagements — a paradigm that [drone warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/drone-warfare.html) has rendered obsolete [2].

Urbanski ([RUSI]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/rusi.html)) argues that the Iran war and the Ukraine war yield one coherent set of lessons for [NATO]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/organizations/nato.html)'s budget planners: neither Iran nor [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html) found a way to defeat a superpower decisively on the battlefield, but both found ways to make a superpower's victory expensive — and potentially unaffordable — by shifting the contest into domains where the Western alliance has under-invested: the economics of munitions, the protection of infrastructure far behind the front line, commercial risk, and the public's confidence in everyday security [38]. The scale of that munitions depletion is quantified in [CSIS]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/csis.html)'s estimate: the US expended roughly half its Patriot inventory, more than 850 Tomahawk cruise missiles, and over 1,000 air-launched stand-off weapons; rebuilding those stockpiles will take four to five years [38].

The war also marked the emergence of AI as a central operational tool: Carneiro Júnior (RUSI) documents the role of Palantir's Maven Smart System during Operation Epic Fury — the platform, descended from Project Maven, which provoked internal revolt at Google before Palantir took it over, fused satellite reconnaissance, persistent drone surveillance, signals intelligence, and population pattern-of-life analysis to identify, prioritise, and recommend strike packages [41]. By April 9, US Central Command reported more than 13,000 strikes against Iranian targets, roughly 1,000 on the opening day alone [41]. Palantir's CTO told Bloomberg Television the conflict would be remembered as the first major war in which AI played a central role — whether or not that claim survives historical scrutiny, as Carneiro Júnior notes, it is the claim a company at the heart of that targeting architecture chose to make about its own product [41]. The juxtaposition deserves more attention than it has received: a war fought substantially over the survival of Iran's nuclear programme is difficult evidence for the proposition that nuclear weapons no longer organise great-power behaviour. Point 12 of Palantir's manifesto states the shift with unusual directness: "One age of deterrence, the atomic age, is ending, and a new era of deterrence built on AI is set to begin." See [AI Military Race]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/ai-military-race.html) [41].

The defense industrial base bore the cost: CSIS (Cancian and Park) analyze the [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) administration's $87.6 billion supplemental request to Congress of June 24, 2026 — about one-third of the request appears to be driven by the costs of the Iran war [40]. A major omission is the repair and rebuilding of damaged bases: The Washington Post identified 228 destroyed structures, though the DOD has stated it may not rebuild all of them [40].

Beyond the battlefield, the war's systemic effects propagated through covert operations, energy, food, and sanctions architecture. Levitt documents how Iran has really picked up on what he calls "kind of a gig economy" — going onto gaming platforms and recruiting to carry out surveillance or even operations for small amounts of money [24]. Iran is sitting on a tremendous amount of sanctioned oil; it uses front companies to sell the oil, and the proceeds go to fund the IRGC and its Qods Force through a system of shadow banking — financing their proxies without spending an actual penny. See [Organised Crime and Statecraft]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/organised-crime-and-statecraft.html) [24]. Davidson (GPF) identifies a structural vulnerability in the defense industry that extends beyond munitions depletion: in U.S. Army deployments, more than 70 percent of logistics tonnage is fuel; some 55 percent is consumed by the logistics systems themselves; disruptions caused by the war in Iran — particularly the closure of the Strait of Hormuz but also mining and attacks on nearby vessels — showed how quickly energy supply chains can be disrupted. See [Defense Industrial Resilience]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/defense-industrial-resilience.html) [27]. Colibasanu (GPF) identifies the energy-to-food cascade as a defining second-order effect: from 2023 to 2025, the Gulf was the world's largest regional exporter of urea and ammonia and the second-largest exporter of DAP and MAP, with around one-third of global seaborne fertilizer trade passing through Hormuz. See [Food and Agriculture]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/food-and-agriculture.html) [27]. Colibasanu frames the broader lesson: the key pattern across all regions is not collapse but persistent cost inflation and volatility — resilience is replacing efficiency, and the cost of resilience is permanent [27].

The US focus on Iran also eroded the sanctions architecture against [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html): as Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz and energy markets tightened, the US issued a general licence to authorise the delivery and sale of Russian crude oil and petroleum products already loaded on vessels — kneecapping G7 price cap efforts just as pressure was proving to bite; nearly 30% of Russian crude in April 2026 was transported on G7 tankers, particularly Greek-owned [39]. ## Great-Power Restraint and Cross-Theater Coupling

### The Triumvirate of Happiness: Great-Power Restraint on the Iran File

[Tchakarova]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/velina-tchakarova.html) frames the great-power coordination around the Iran file as the "Triumvirate of Happiness" — the moniker she assigned to clandestine discussions between Washington, Beijing, and Moscow designed to keep the Iran war from escalating and pulling the world down into a [Global System Rupture]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-rupture.html) [74]. "It isn't an alliance, and it isn't a peace. It is a crisis ceiling," she writes — cooperation without binding commitment, a floor only as strong as the willingness of all three to accept that the systemic costs of rupturing it outweigh the costs of staying put [74]. The shared interest holding the Triumvirate together is negative: none of the three wants a complete break in oil flows out of the Gulf, a spike in energy prices globally, and the subsequent energy, fertilizer, and food consequences of a prolonged shutdown of the Strait of Hormuz and Bab-al-Mandab [74]. It is sustainable precisely because no member has to recognize it, allowing each state to cooperate with the others on Iran while avoiding being tied down in any other arena. If and when one or more members decides to stop abiding by the ceiling, it collapses, releasing the very tensions the three have been keeping at bay [74].

On July 24, [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) released a declaration stating that "both Xi and [Putin]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/putin.html) have now confirmed to me that they will not sell any weapons to Iran," including Chinese companies. "I took them both at their word," he said [74]. This came just days after Secretary Rubio held separate discussions with Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi and Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov about the Persian Gulf — along with the South China Sea and Ukraine — on the sidelines of the [ASEAN]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/organizations/asean.html) ministerial in Manila [74]. Weeks later, even as members of his Cabinet had to admit to Congress that China and Russia were indeed enabling the Iranian war effort, Trump tweeted out a denial that they were doing anything at all — or in his words not "participating" in helping Iran. "I will tell you that President Xi said he will not partake, and President Putin said the same thing. I think you know I trust them… I don't think they'd want to have me disappointed," he said in the Oval Office when asked about the report [75].

Tchakarova reads Trump's publicizing of Xi's and Putin's promises as an insurance policy against the ceiling's fragility: he is trying to turn informal off-the-record agreements into public ones that are costly to break in terms of reputation. But the move also reveals the deal's weakness — neither Beijing nor Moscow has offered anything to back up Trump's version of events, and Russia has plenty of reason to keep vague its intentions in supporting Tehran [74].

### The Patriot Bottleneck and the Two-War Continuum

The Iran war and the [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html) share a binding constraint: finite US Patriot interceptor missiles, each costing $3–5M, with little room to increase production [74]. The Patriot is one of the only weapons available that can intercept ballistic missiles; Ukraine wants Patriots to shoot down Russian missiles, while the US is using them to defend its bases in the [Middle East]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html) against Iranian missiles [74]. There are two theaters, but there is only one bottleneck [74][89].

The bottleneck places the escalatory ladder in Washington, not in the capitals of its partners [74]. Netanyahu's point was that [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html) would obey Washington in its war with Iran: "We know the situation, if we're attacked we will respond, but we will also rely on President Trump's decision and timing," he told Trump, according to one Israeli official. Several Israeli commentators pointed out bluntly that the US was now running the war and Israel was sitting in the passenger seat [74]. Israel deferred to the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) on the timing and scope of strikes [74].

[Zelensky]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/zelensky.html) had a clear goal when he met with Trump: to secure fulfillment of a promise made during a [NATO]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/organizations/nato.html) summit meeting at the White House to issue the necessary licenses for Ukraine to produce Patriot interceptor missiles domestically. Zelensky later told Fox News that Trump had agreed: "The President said, I accept that I will be granting the licenses." Ukraine needs twenty-five Patriot systems to help defend its cities from Russian drones and ballistic missiles. Zelensky also met with executives from Lockheed Martin and spoke of "joint production" discussions [74]. The proxy fights and pays the price; Washington controls the escalatory ladder and the industrial base needed to fight the war [74]. Both proxy presidents returned home weaker than when they arrived. Netanyahu returned to Israel a week after the bombing campaign started, frustrated that he could not continue his attacks on Iran as Trump negotiated a deal. Zelensky returned to a country still being bombarded, relying on an American supply chain that can only be established domestically over several years [74].

The leverage that Patriot scarcity gives Washington is not wielded only on Ukraine's behalf: [O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) documents a structural dynamic in which the United States uses its residual military leverage over [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html) to benefit [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) — by continuing to dribble small numbers of Patriot interceptors through European intermediaries, the Trump administration maintains decisive leverage over Ukrainian operational decisions, a control mechanism more valuable to Moscow than a complete cutoff would be, since ending supplies entirely would remove the restraint that currently binds Ukrainian strategy [100]. In O'Brien's words, the USA keeping Ukraine dependent on Patriots means that even a small number gives the US leverage, and that leverage can be used to do a massive favor for Vladimir Putin — a favor that the Ukrainians were sadly forced to start giving right away. The US has achieved major leverage over Ukraine through a brutal rationing of access to Patriot interceptors; if they cut that off entirely, it would actually have been worse for Russia [100].

The mechanism was demonstrated on July 31, when Vice President Vance called Zelensky to demand Ukraine halt its campaign against Russian oil exports through the Black Sea port of Novorossiysk — specifically targeting the CPC terminal through which Kazakh oil flows. Ukraine complied, ceasing strikes on CPC infrastructure and non-Russian-flagged vessels [100]. The "non-Russian" designation is effectively meaningless: Russia's shadow tanker fleet deliberately uses non-Russian flags to evade sanctions, so the US was instructing Ukraine to stop a campaign that had been materially degrading Russian oil revenue [100]. The oil-price justification is unconvincing — if the US truly cared about commodity inflation, it might equally urge Russia to halt its growing campaign against Ukrainian grain shipments from Odesa, which threatens global food supply chains [100].

### Iran-Ukraine Infrastructure Strike Coupling

Tchakarova identifies a parallel in targeting logic between the two theaters: since March 2, Iran has pursued a retaliatory approach of targeting energy and water desalination facilities in the Gulf region [89]. Ukraine's Energy Minister Denys Shmyhal, in an interview with POLITICO, noted that this winter it will be even harder for Ukraine to withstand the onslaught of Russian attacks because, in his view, the Russian Armed Forces will now also target water supply facilities. "Russia's goal now is to destroy our water supply," he said. "Last winter, they targeted the electricity and heating sectors, but it was possible to survive as long as gas and water remained available during power outage" [89].

Tchakarova identifies the binding constraint not as the targeting parallel but as the shared Patriot interceptor stockpile: "The Iran war has drained United States stockpiles of Patriot interceptors, the same PAC-3 munitions that provide Ukraine its only reliable defense against Russian ballistic missiles. This is the clearest and most consequential Simultaneity coupling in the entire system, and it converts two geographically distant wars into a single coupled problem governed by one finite resource" [89].

### China's Escalating Military Support and the MANPADS Threshold

[O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) documents the progression of Chinese military support to [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) through the first six months of the war [75]. [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html)'s assistance consisted of missile propellant chemicals — sodium perchlorate shipped via IRISL-owned vessels from the Gaolan Port chemical terminal in Zhuhai, with at least five shipments tracked and analysts estimating the combined cargo could support roughly 785 additional missiles; satellite intelligence, including the TEE-01B spy satellite used to monitor US bases across [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html), [Jordan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/jordan.html), Bahrain, [Kuwait]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/kuwait.html), Djibouti, [Oman]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/oman.html), the UAE, and [Iraq]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iraq.html); AI-enabled analysis of satellite, flight, and shipping data on US assets in the region; BeiDou navigation data that Iran may be using for targeting; and dual-use chipmaking equipment supplied by SMIC to Iran's military-industrial sector [75]. NBC reported on 29 May that the F-15 downed over southwestern Iran in April was probably hit by a Chinese-made shoulder-launched missile [75].

In late July 2026, Reuters reported that China was preparing to send Iran finished, advanced munitions — QW-12 and FN-16 MANPADS, comparable to the Stinger. Iran is expected to receive within weeks a first shipment out of up to 400 Chinese-made shoulder-fired air defense missile launchers, according to three sources familiar with the deal [75]. O'Brien frames this as a categorical shift from dual-use components to finished advanced weapons. "If they are about to transfer fully built, advanced weapons, that could be a game changer (and the start of a process which sees much more go to Iran)" [75]. These MANPADS seem almost perfectly suited to thwart any Trumpist escalation: if Trump decided to insert US ground troops anywhere in Iran — be it around the Strait, to try and find Iranian nuclear materials, or to seize Kharg Island — the US would have to maintain a regular flow of low-level flights [75]. The weapons could arrive and deploy by September, creating a binary outcome: Trump might either be forced into an immediate escalation before they arrive, or have to admit that he cannot afford to escalate at all [75].

O'Brien compares the moment to 1861, the first year of the US Civil War, when outside powers were willing to directly threaten the US in ways that China is doing now — and the US had to take it. The difference: then, the US recovered and was still on an upward trajectory. "It definitely is not on an upward trajectory now" [75]. For O'Brien, this marks the US's most vulnerable moment against external powers since the Civil War, situated within the broader frame of [great-power competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html) [75].


## The Post-War Strategic Landscape

### The Offloading Doctrine and Post-War Architecture

[Kamran Bokhari]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/kamran-bokhari.html) reads the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) as executing a deliberate structural transition from [Middle East]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html) hegemon to "offshore actor with its finger on the scales" — a shift that predates the war [81]. He frames this as a geostrategic doctrine the Trump administration undertook well before the first strikes, and one whose endpoint is categorical: there is no returning to an era in which Washington is the region's principal security guarantor [81]. Hinote corroborates the motivation — the US wants to be "done with the Middle East" to execute its pivot to Asia and shed expensive basing infrastructure, with a theory of success that puts the Iranian regime into "some sort of state where we don't have to go back anytime soon" [82]. But Hinote diverges on whether the execution matches the theory: US forces are accomplishing their military objectives — the Iranian Navy, Air Force, air defenses, and missile and drone production capabilities are being destroyed — yet the conversion of tactical success into strategic outcomes is, in his judgment, "the weakness in this entire operation" [82] ([endless warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/endless-warfare.html), [escalation-theory critique]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/escalation-theory-critique.html)).

The architecture being built to replace US hegemony is a regional core of [Turkey]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/turkey.html), [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html), [Pakistan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/pakistan.html), and [Egypt]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/egypt.html), supported by [Qatar]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/qatar.html), the [UAE]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/uae.html), [Jordan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/jordan.html), and [Azerbaijan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/azerbaijan.html), taking a leadership role so that there can be a self-sustaining balance of power [81]. The defining challenge is aligning these states and integrating them into a workable relationship with [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html); unlike Europe, the Middle East is a "blank canvas" where regional integration mechanisms must be constructed from scratch [81]. A "fundamental psychological shift" has accompanied this architectural challenge — [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) attacking Arab states is "the new normal," and many Arab governments view Iran as a more fundamental strategic threat than Israeli policies toward the Palestinian territories, [Lebanon]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/lebanon.html), and [Syria]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/syria.html) [81].

[O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) extends this posture to its irreversible verdict: the USA has lost the war; Iran will emerge, in the short term at least, in a stronger position; that entire paradigm is gone, with many US bases in the Gulf supposedly empty — what is being negotiated is "haggling over the terms of US defeat" [94]. The Carter Doctrine — the 1980 commitment to use military force to keep the Persian Gulf open — has been demolished by the paradox that US military force itself caused another power to close the Gulf [94]. Advanced missile interceptor stockpiles have been depleted by approximately 80%, with ATACMS and PrSM long-range ground-launched missiles "virtually all" expended; rebuilding these stockpiles will take years, possibly more than a decade [94][95]. The US is already withdrawing forces from the Gulf: the Wall Street Journal reported precipitous pullouts from [Kuwait]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/kuwait.html) — the country the US liberated in 1991 and used as a major regional base — because American forces cannot be protected against even cheap Iranian strike capabilities [94]. Forces withdrawn from the Gulf have been partially redeployed to [Jordan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/jordan.html), which has in turn drawn regular Iranian attacks, making Jordanians question the US role [94]. [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html) is openly "hedging" — losing confidence in the US as ultimate security guarantor and recognizing that Washington cannot protect it from Iran or even help fight the Houthis [94].

Under discussion is an Iran-[Oman]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/oman.html) mechanism: inbound ships from the Indian Ocean would route through Iranian territorial waters, while outbound vessels would transit near Oman's coast, with "service fees" for environmental response, security, and labor costs split equally between Iran and Oman — the US further marginalized from the governance structure [94]. If the US concedes on Hormuz, the nuclear question becomes academic: with military stocks so low, Washington will have little or no leverage remaining [94]. By mid-August, the war had lost all strategic purpose for the US [95]. [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) appeared willing to extend the blockade through the November midterms — a shift from earlier assessments — because Iran was giving him nothing with which to claim victory, and he preferred continued war over accepting visible defeat [95]. The White House narrative pivoted to minimizing the disruption: Treasury Secretary Bessent claimed 8-9 million barrels per day were getting through, while the actual data showed fewer than 10 tanker transits per day compared to the pre-war 130-140 [95]. Oil markets had internalized months of promises that a deal was imminent, keeping prices below what the physical disruption warranted — but if markets concluded Trump would truly maintain the blockade through the election, prices would adjust sharply [95].

The state of the US military is, in O'Brien's assessment, arguably the worst since 1945 [95]. The USS Abraham Lincoln has been at sea for over 265 days on a deployment planned to end in May, with reports of a mental health crisis including sailors attempting to go overboard, and Navy representatives compelled to meet with distressed crew families [95]. In a training exercise in Germany, Ukrainian drone operators easily spotted and defeated US Army troops from Fort Hood, suffering what the Wall Street Journal termed a "drubbing" [95]. The US has spent approximately $9 trillion on defense since 2016 and produced a deeply flawed, deteriorating force [95].

### Proxy Network Collapse and Regime Resilience

Iran's regional proxy network collapsed — Hamas and Hezbollah degraded in 2024, Turkey shaped a new order in Syria, Iraq's position increasingly vulnerable — while the regime "will almost certainly survive in the near term" [81]. The [Israeli]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html) military campaign that degraded Hamas and Hezbollah in 2024 collapsed Iran's regional proxy network, creating a strategic opening for Turkey, which was able, with the help of its allies in the Hayat Tahrir al-Sham movement, to help shape the emergence of a new political order in Syria [81]. Iran's position in [Iraq]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iraq.html) — once considered rock solid — is increasingly vulnerable as Baghdad and Washington curb the influence of pro-Iranian Shiite militias and strengthen state control over armed groups [81]. Hinote calls the US-Israel operation "maybe the most successful decapitation operation in history" — yet there was no strategic paralysis and no internal uprising, despite [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html)'s public call for the Iranian people to "take over your government" and the IRGC to "lay down your weapons" [82]. Bokhari assesses near-term survival as almost certain so long as the US declines ground intervention and no viable domestic insurgent alternative exists — but over time, the war, the death of senior leadership, and the devolution within the IRGC will force Tehran to rethink its position [81].

[Pape]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/robert-pape.html) extends this paradox to Iran's forward-looking endgame: Tehran is explicitly waiting out the Trump presidency. Majid Shakeri, an adviser to Iran's Parliament Speaker, stated that Tehran would "accompany [Trump] until his term ends" [96]. Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi has reinforced the immediate part of that position, saying Tehran is not engaged in direct negotiations with Washington and is limiting communications to an indirect "exchange of messages" [96]. Iran's parliament is considering legislation that would exclude vessels linked to the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html), [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html), and other designated hostile states and impose transit fees of 5% to 7% of cargo value [96]. Tehran is simultaneously reorganizing its military leadership for prolonged confrontation [96]. Iran's objective is not battlefield victory but hegemonic recognition: creating conditions that no future American president can reverse — a new president who fully accepts Iran as the regional hegemon [96]. Pape projects a three-year trajectory if Iran maintains this position through January 2029: persistent risk premiums in global oil prices with repeated spikes and widespread recession fears; front-line Gulf states delaying or abandoning bypass pipeline plans under Iranian pressure, with demands to dismantle US military bases on their territory; and expansion of non-dollar energy and financial settlement with [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) and [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html), making US financial stability increasingly dependent on decisions in Tehran, Moscow, and Beijing [96]. The cumulative result would make Iran the "4th center of world power" [96].

### The Geographic and Doctrinal Intractability

Geography favors Iran in the Strait; the last 2% is what insurance cannot absorb; and US and Israeli aims "may have in certain circumstances worked against each other" [82]. [James Slife]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/james-slife.html) frames the Strait of Hormuz as a problem military planners have struggled with for years: Iran controls three sides of a narrow, shallow, crowded waterway [82]. "If there was a great option for how air power could unblock the Strait of Hormuz, it would probably be unblocked at this point" — but "geography just doesn't favor us in this regard" [82]. Hinote identifies the structural dilemma: the US joint force can prevent the majority of attacks on shipping — even into the 98% range — but "that last 2%" is what the [insurance]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/insurance-and-trade-warfare.html) industry cannot absorb, because Iran's mountainous shores provide protective cover for intermittent attacks [82]. Slife notes that the US and Israel pursued "very different target sets which may tell us something about what our respective national aims are" [82]. Hinote articulates the Israeli theory: Israel believes the Iranian regime will always be hostile and must go back repeatedly to re-strike regime targets — a strategy of "mowing the grass." The US theory is a coercion campaign demanding the strait open and Iran give up its nuclear aspirations [82]. The two aims "not only were they not necessarily totally aligned, they may have in certain circumstances worked against each other" [82]. The intractability stems from the disparity between what the US is trying to achieve militarily and what those military means can actually produce politically [82].

### Service-Reliability Drag and the F-15E Rescue

Heavy deployments have exceeded what the Air Force and Navy can routinely sustain, producing a delayed cumulative effect on readiness. The pace of operations "has probably exceeded the Air Force and the Navy's ability to maintain levels of readiness going forward" — an impact Slife says we will need to "pay close attention to" over the next three to five years [82]. Hinote highlights the F-15E back-seater rescue as a critical but underreported success that may stand as "one of the best we've ever seen" — comparable in significance to Operation Eagle Claw (1980) [82]. The operation required "tremendous integration across multiple types of forces in a very difficult time frame" [82]. Had the pilot been captured and paraded on Iranian television, "any political objectives that we were trying to achieve would be all the more difficult to achieve" — the rescue preserved not just a pilot but the political conditions for the campaign [82].

### The Operational Pause and Its Hidden Costs

[Norman Roule]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/norman-roule.html) frames the current state not as a ceasefire but as an "operational pause": a "period of coercive restraint" in which Iran continues attacks on ships while the US restrains its response [83]. The US is not approaching a significant core agreement; the diplomacy exhibits a consistent pattern in which the US announces negotiations, Iran denies them, and indirect negotiations follow through regional parties [83]. The Iran-Oman mechanism being discussed is likely overstated — if tolls are charged, a cascade of unresolved questions follows: what happens to a shipper who refuses to pay, how does it interact with US sanctions, and are American ships allowed to pay Iran [83]. If Iran acquires formal control over the strait, money paid by shippers would "inevitably go to its missile program and proxies," giving Tehran billions in revenue and "a permanent foot on the throat of the international community" — with direct implications for [Kuwait]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/kuwait.html)'s food security and the broader [Hormuz Toll System]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/hormuz-toll-system.html) [83]. The idea that the US can "build on trust" with an IRGC government "has no evidence in reality" — though that does not mean it may not happen [83]. Iran's cyber activities have continued unabated since the conflict began, fitting with the past pattern of Iranian cyber activity against SCADA and other systems in the United States [83]. Roule argues the appropriate response is an appropriate proportional response "just done differently" — congruent with multiple administrations' views and statements as to how they will treat Iranian aggression [83].

### Gulf State Defense and Moderation

Roule assesses Gulf States' defense as "very, very successful" — the product of years of engagement with the US and the American private sector [83]. [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html) joined US strikes on Iraq — a first — demonstrating the quality of Saudi Air Forces and the closeness of military partnership [83]. The Gulf States talked [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) out of imminent escalation; if they face further strikes, they will demand a voice in any major attack on Iran. "It is their neighborhood. They know their region better than we do. We should respect that" [83].


## Saudi Nuclear Deal: Iran War Context and Proliferation Risk

The US-Saudi civilian nuclear agreement moves along a single axis: from the war that created its urgency, through the terms that weaken non-proliferation precedent, to the proliferation cascade those terms unlock, and finally to [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html)'s continued nuclear pursuit — which, combined with the Saudi track, makes a multi-nuclear [Middle East]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html) markedly more probable. [Guido Steinberg]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/guido-steinberg.html) carries this assessment in its entirety (see [Nuclear Proliferation and Arms Control]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/nuclear-proliferation-and-arms-control.html)) [84].

The agreement was concluded during ongoing fighting in the Persian Gulf. Iran's military responded to the latest US strike waves by firing rockets and drones at [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html) and other Arab Gulf states — a pattern maintained since the war's beginning in late February [84]. The current war in the Persian Gulf, the closure of the Strait of Hormuz, and Iranian bombardment have also made the Saudis aware of their own vulnerability [84]. Despite deep dissatisfaction in Riyadh with the poorly planned US war effort, MBS and his circle see no alternative to the alliance with Washington [84].

Two details of the agreement are "wegweisend" (path-breaking): Saudi Arabia is to be allowed to enrich uranium domestically, and inspections of the program are to be less stringent than those under the neighboring UAE's 2009 agreement with the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) [84]. That 2009 accord included a renunciation of domestic enrichment and accession to the Additional Protocol of the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty, which provides for especially rigorous inspections. The UAE agreement set standards ("setzte Maßstäbe") whose binding force could now erode against the US-Saudi accord [84].

Abu Dhabi will likely seek the same conditions for its own program retroactively; [Turkey]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/turkey.html), as Riyadh's rival for leadership positions in the Middle East, could be tempted to respond to Saudi efforts with its own nuclear program; and Iran will point to Saudi conditions in future negotiations, insisting on its own right to enrichment at least as persistently as before [84]. A nuclear-power Saudi Arabia ("Atommacht Saudi-Arabien") has become, at minimum, a possibility [84].

Iran's new, heavily militarized leadership will likely do everything to deter opponents such as the US and [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html) more effectively in the future. That it now holds the Strait of Hormuz as a new weapon will likely not dissuade it from a military nuclear program it has pursued since 1987 [84]. The combination of Iran's continued nuclear pursuit and the US-Saudi nuclear agreement makes a Middle East with three, four, or five nuclear powers very much more probable than before [84].


## Hormuz Legal Framework

The legal contest over the Strait of Hormuz runs from a pre-war doctrinal dispute through wartime closure, controlled passage, and ultimately the question of whether Iran's toll regime has any basis in international law. [SWP Berlin](actors/authors/swp.html)'s Schaller traces the through-line [97].

The core dispute predates the war: [Iran](actors/countries/iran.html) maintains that only innocent passage — not transit passage — applies in the Strait, a reading that would grant it wider regulatory authority over transiting vessels. Iran is not a party to UNCLOS, having signed but not ratified the convention, while [Oman](actors/countries/oman.html) is a party — leaving the two littoral states under divergent legal regimes [97].

On February 28, 2026, the same day the US-Israeli air strikes began, the IRGC allegedly radioed ships in the region that transit was no longer permitted; two days later, on March 2, a senior IRGC commander declared via Iranian state media that the Strait was closed and that any ship attempting to pass would be fired upon [97]. UKMTO, the Royal Navy's maritime security reporting channel, recorded approximately 100 maritime incidents across the Persian Gulf, the Strait of Hormuz, the Gulf of Oman, and surrounding waters between February 28 and July 31, 2026 [97]. In March 2026 the UN Security Council responded with Resolution 2817, affirming that any attempt to block lawful transit passage or freedom of navigation through the Strait constitutes a threat to world peace; efforts to authorize military measures to secure shipping failed, blocked by Chinese and Russian vetoes [97].

By early April the situation shifted from blanket closure to controlled passage. On April 8, Araghchi announced the opening of the Strait within the framework of a ceasefire; apparently Iran had already begun permitting selected ships to pass on specific routes and charging fees [97]. The US countered on April 13, when CENTCOM announced a blockade of all shipping to and from Iranian ports in both the Persian Gulf and the Gulf of Oman — not a blockade of Strait traffic itself [97]. A 14-point plan agreed between the US and Iran in mid-June collapsed by late July as the US reimposed its naval blockade and [Trump](actors/trump.html) declared the agreement failed [97].

Iran meanwhile institutionalized its fee regime, establishing the Persian Gulf Strait Authority (PGSA) to regulate shipping and collect tolls; according to media reports, Iranian authorities had demanded up to $2 million per ship [97]. The toll system lacks any basis in international law. Under UNCLOS Article 26, states may not charge fees for the mere passage of foreign ships through their territorial sea; the prohibition applies directly to innocent passage through the territorial sea and, via Article 45, to innocent passage through straits. For straits where the more extensive transit-passage regime of Articles 37ff applies, coastal states' regulatory authority is further constrained — making Iran's proposed toll system illegal regardless of which passage regime governs the Strait [97].


## Iran's Cyber Posture

A spate of cyberattacks against the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) water sector has impacted at least 12 states and 100 municipalities, suggesting a coordinated campaign at scale against US critical infrastructure; the CyberAv3ngers — a hacktivist group believed to be linked to the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps — has claimed responsibility for the attacks [98]. [CSIS]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/csis.html)'s Shah assesses [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html)'s cyber posture as enabling rather than escalatory: if these attacks are indeed Iran's doing, they should be regarded as opportunistic disruption owing to weak US cyber defenses, rather than a turning point in the conflict [98]. Iranian military doctrine prioritizes the information domain as a key battlespace, meaning Iran will actively seek and foment incidents it can leverage for cognitive effect; through disrupting critical infrastructure in the United States, Iran can sow fear and a sense of insecurity, projecting its power into the US homeland far beyond its own borders [98]. Hormuz remains Iran's primary form of leverage and escalation; cyber serves more as an enabling capability — shaping the information environment to Iran's advantage — than a strategic turning point [98]. The regime's internal fragmentation complicates any attribution of intent: the Iranian regime is fragmented politically, and its communications infrastructure degraded from the course of the war — compounded by the decentralized approach Iran shifted to in response to the US decapitation campaign to remove its top leaders — so it is highly unclear, and possibly unlikely, whether the cyberattacks on the water facilities were ordered by Iranian leadership; it could be the work of middle-ranking operators, or even one side of the Iranian apparatus seeking to actively undermine another by conducting an attack against the US homeland to disrupt already fragile negotiations [98].


## Contested: Is US Iran strategy a deliberate offloading doctrine or adrift improvisation?

[Kamran Bokhari]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/kamran-bokhari.html) reads US strategy as a coherent, deliberate, and irreversible transition from hegemon to offshore balancer — the Trump administration's geostrategic doctrine of offloading posture predated the Iran war, and US assets will not return to prior posture. [81]

[S. Clinton Hinote]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/s-clinton-hinote.html) reads the same phenomena as effectively adrift — US forces are accomplishing military objectives but the conversion to strategic success is the weakness of the entire operation. The outcome "would not have been in the US theory of success" and reflects a branch plan "not going so well." [82]

The split is methodological, not evidential — Bokhari reads structural doctrine (grand strategy as structural inevitability); Hinote reads operational outcomes (tactical success without strategic conversion). Neither disputes the other's facts.

**Tiebreaker:** whether the post-ceasefire architecture (Oman mechanism, Gulf state custody transfer) coheres into a durable doctrine or dissolves into ad hoc management through Q1 2027.
**Status:** open fork.

## The Political Escalation Trap

[Robert Pape]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/robert-pape.html) argues that [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) has shifted from survival to leverage: its target is no longer the US military but the [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) presidency and American voters [101]. Iran does not need to fully close Hormuz — only to make costs persistently painful and politically visible [101]. The strategy aims to raise the political cost of restarting the war so high that no future president would attempt it [101]. A Republican midterm loss attributable to the Iran war could convert military stalemate into a structural deterrent against renewed US intervention [101]. Trump's central dilemma is thus temporal: escalate now while he still can, or accept losing the political ability to escalate later [101].

## Iran's Post-MoU Escalation Preparations

Iran's hard-line leadership interpreted the [MoU]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/iran-ceasefire-mou.html) as a US and Israeli stratagem to buy time rather than a genuine de-escalation, according to Wall Street Journal reporting [102]. Iran accordingly pursued a two-track policy: overt diplomacy alongside covert security preparations [102]. The overriding goal is deterrence-by-pain — inflicting enough cost to prevent future attacks on the scale of [The Twelve Day War (2026)]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/twelve-day-war.html) [102]. A deep mutual trust deficit makes a durable deal unlikely in the near term [102].

## The Iran War's Pacific Drain

[Phillips P. O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) argues that the Iran war has compounded [Indo-Pacific]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/indo-pacific.html) military degradation by consuming advanced munitions and carrier availability [103]. Pacific weakness is not a single anomaly but follows from the profligate use of US assets against Iran, the Pentagon's refusal to adapt, and the broader deterioration of the military-industrial base — a pattern tracked under [Defense Industrial Resilience]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/defense-industrial-resilience.html) [103].
