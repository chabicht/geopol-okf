Last week, **Iran** ’s Supreme National Security Council issued a set of **unilateral demands for reopening Hormuz**. This comes on the heels of weeks of hard-power moves—including Iranian strikes on ships passing through the Strait and support for the escalating Houthi war against Saudi oil—that have brought energy flows out of the Middle East back toward the low levels seen in the opening weeks of the Iran war.

Yesterday, President **Trump** announced a **new set of U.S. demands**, adding another layer of unilateral conditions to an already widening gap between Washington and Tehran.

The result is not a negotiated pathway out of the war, but **two sides trying to dictate the terms of the outcome.**

Unilateral demands are not themselves evidence that either side is winning. They are evidence that **both sides believe their leverage is sufficient to dictate terms**.

Iran is no longer simply fighting for regime survival. It is no longer merely trying to prove that it can withstand American and Israeli attacks or talking about future ambitions.

Rather than accepting a settlement that would restore the prewar stability, **Iran** is using its growing leverage to push for greater regional power: **moving from survival to ambition to hard power**. That shift will create new risks for the United States, the region, and the global economy.

America’s restraint has lowered the risk of an imminent spectacular Iranian attack. But the absence of a U.S. strategy changes the incentives for everyone.

**The central issue is not simply that Iran is gaining leverage over the United States. It is that a temporary U.S. strategic pause is allowing a regional balance of power to change without Washington participating in the balancing process.**

Simply put: ***Who is gaining relative power while the United States is not acting?***

**As Iran moves from survival to ambition to hard power, the consequences will shape decisions in the region and across the globe during the rest of August.**

To see what comes next -- and what Iran and other countries will do -- four questions are most important:

- **Why is Iran’s leverage growing so significantly since early July?**
- **How is Iran’s growing power leading to ambitions beyond the demands of the MOU?**
- **What does Iran want to open Hormuz, and can Trump politically afford the price before the Midterms?**
- **If not, what does Iran do next—and what are the near-term risks to the global economy?**

**The answers will affect near-term energy prices, medium-term political stability of front-line Gulf states, and the long-term ability of the United States to retain significant forward military presence in the region.**

My answers are below.

### The Oil Inventory Clock and Iran’s Maximum Leverage

**The most important strategic fact may be the clock.** Every day that Hormuz remains constrained, the world consumes another layer of the buffers that have prevented a much larger oil shock. The IEA reported global observed inventories falling by **368 million barrels from March to August 1**, while stocks on land in OECD countries fell sharply.

The United States has an emergency reserve. China has accumulated substantial commercial and strategic stocks. Producers have rerouted some Gulf oil around alternative corridors. But these are **buffers, not substitutes for normal supply**. And the Red Sea route—the most important bypass for some Gulf exports—is itself under pressure from Houthi attacks. Reuters reports that shipping through both Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb remains disrupted.

This changes the bargaining calculus. **Time is not neutral.** As the buffers shrink, the economic cost of continued **disruption rises—and so does Iran’s leverage**.

That gives Tehran less reason to accept an early settlement that restores the **prewar balance of power**. Washington, by contrast, faces increasing pressure from oil prices, allied vulnerability, and domestic economic costs.

**The longer the strategic pause lasts, the more the oil clock works in Iran’s favor.**

### Iran’s Demands Have Hardened—Because Its Bargaining Position Has Changed

Iran’s demands reveal the change in its strategic position. **Earlier proposals sought an end to the fighting and a negotiated rollback of U.S. pressure.** The latest demands go much further: Tehran now wants the United States to provide financial relief **up front**, end the naval blockade, and withdraw U.S. forces from the region. Iranian officials have also tied reopening Hormuz to these conditions.

The progression matters:

**War termination → negotiated rollback → unilateral demands.**

This maps on to the phrase I use in the media:

**Survival → Ambition → Hard Power**

The June MOU itself contemplated a ***negotiated and phased*** **$300 billion U.S.-backed reconstruction framework**, while earlier Iranian proposals sought sanctions relief, frozen assets, and withdrawal of U.S. forces from the region.

What has changed is Tehran’s assessment of American leverage.

**Washington has stopped bombing, but Iran has not been forced to surrender its leverage over Hormuz.** That is **deterrence** working in a direction Washington did not anticipate earlier: Iranian resistance has constrained the further use of American force.

Tehran therefore has an incentive to demand more **before** making concessions, while unilateral demands shift the focus to what the US must concede, **eliminating any constraint on Iran’s nuclear program**.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!wQaP!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc728aab7-397a-4b1f-96f0-0d140c6eda0d_663x388.png)

The most consequential demands are those that alter the regional balance itself: **up-front money from Washington and an immediate, comprehensive U.S. military withdrawal from the region.** Accepting both would not simply end the war. It would **remove the principal American military counterweight to Iranian power in the Gulf**.

**Iran is no longer bargaining only over the terms of peace. It is bargaining over who will possess the stronger hand after the war.**

### Beyond Narrow Survival: The Logic of Regional Hegemony

**Iran’s objective is no longer simply survival. It is regional power.** That distinction matters because controlling the Strait of Hormuz alone gives Tehran leverage over one chokepoint; shaping the political and military balance across the Gulf, Red Sea, and eastern Mediterranean gives it something much larger: **the ability to sustain control over all the energy and exports leaving the Gulf region, even as the West attempts pipeline and other Hormuz bypasses.**

In May, Iran telegraphed these aims as IRGC leaders articulated the need for a **“Resistance Security Belt.”** At the time, many interpreted this as mere rhetorical support for Iran’s allies Hezbollah and the Houthis. However, it reflects Iran’s interest in establishing firm control over the **region’s energy chokepoints** – as the map below shows.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!QhRz!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2e57bbc2-edcb-4b96-91b1-9f52be8dfba9_671x447.jpeg)

For Iran, establishing regional hegemony concretely means:

- **Geographic Control:** The security belt allows Iran to project power far beyond its borders, linking nodes from the Persian Gulf to the Red Sea and the Mediterranean.
- **Controlling Outflows:** By maintaining influence along these paths, Tehran positions itself to dictate or disrupt how energy leaves the broader Middle East in multiple directions.
- **Economic Leverage:** Disruption or selective passage in these vital zones affects global GDP, particularly in energy-dependent Asia, decoupling regional states from total reliance on U.S. protection.

It also directly undermines Washington’s ideas for **pipeline bypasses in the coming years.**

When U.S. officials like Scott Bessent argue that alternative underground pipelines will render the Strait of Hormuz largely irrelevant within two years, it signals a Western strategy to bypass traditional maritime choke points.

This looming obsolescence incentivizes Iran to lock in land-based territorial depth, political alignment, and proxy-enforced control across the entire “resistance belt” now, ensuring its long-term structural power cannot simply be engineered away by alternative pipelines.

This also creates **urgency for Tehran** to establish regional hegemony, since its Hormuz chokepoint leverage has an expiration date.

**By keeping these chokepoints within operational reach of its proxy networks, Iran ensures that any attempt by international coalitions to bypass or isolate it economically carries a high risk of multi-point maritime disruption.** This structural leverage forces global energy markets—and major Asian consumers—to continually factor Iranian interests into their long-term security calculations.

This is where Iran’s asymmetric strategy becomes strategically important.

**Tehran does not need to match American conventional power.** Drones, missiles, maritime attacks, and allied forces can impose costs across multiple theaters, forcing Washington to defend a much larger geographic area while Iran chooses where and when to apply pressure.

The logic is **horizontal escalation**: widen the battlefield, multiply the costs, and make American power increasingly expensive to sustain.

**And do so unilaterally, without the constraint of US negotiations.**

### What Would Trump Have to Concede to Reopen Hormuz?

Because Iran is seeking systemic power rather than simply economic relief, persuading Iran to voluntarily reopen Hormuz would require structural U.S. concessions that rewrite the geopolitical rules of the Middle East. This is not MOU 2.0 and requires much more than another round of negotiations. Trump would have to accept a substantially different regional order.

**First, Washington would have to accept Iran as a regional power center.** That means tolerating an Iranian sphere of influence stretching from the Gulf toward the Red Sea and Mediterranean, while abandoning demands to eliminate the missile, drone, and allied networks that give Tehran strategic depth. It would also require **credible limits on Israeli military action** against Iran and its regional partners.

**Second, Washington would have to fund Iran’s expansion as a rising regional power, providing money and revenue up front.** That could mean major sanctions relief, access to frozen funds, unrestricted oil exports, and acceptance of some form of **Iranian transit fees for managing Hormuz**. The issue is not merely compensation; it is establishing a continuing revenue stream from Iran’s geographic leverage.

**Third, Trump would have to reduce the U.S. military footprint to the point of operational worthlessness.** The bombing campaign has demonstrated the limits of airpower against Iran’s geography and asymmetric strategy. A settlement would therefore require **accepting limits on U.S.-led regional primacy** rather than assuming military pressure can restore the old order.

Finally, Washington would have to confront the hardest reality: **a stronger Iran with greater financial resources, territorial depth, and strategic leverage would be far harder to prevent from eventually acquiring a nuclear deterrent.**

That is the price of voluntary de-escalation: **not Iran’s surrender, but America’s acceptance of a new regional balance of power.**

No American president could accept even one of these concessions, much less the whole package.

**That is why Trump and Iran are on a collision course – and why Hormuz is likely to remain shut and Saudi oil exports from Yanbu are likely to continue declining, with growing risks to the world economy for weeks if not months.**
