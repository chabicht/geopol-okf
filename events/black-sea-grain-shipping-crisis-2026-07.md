# Background

[Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) has been waging a campaign to destroy shipping in and out of Ukrainian ports — vital for exporting Ukrainian grain. While much attention has focused on Ukrainian attacks on Russian shipping, the Russian campaign against Ukrainian vessels has been sustained and consequential. [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html) makes its money selling grain; [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) makes its money selling oil — both commodities must go out by sea [1].

# What Happened

Between June 20 and July 20, 2026, Russian forces hit 28 merchant vessels around Odesa, killing 21 sailors. The largest single loss was a corn-laden vessel under Guinea-Bissau flag where 10 sailors died. On July 22, at peak harvest season, not a single merchant ship entered Ukrainian waters to load grain. Ukrainian Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha tweeted: "Russia is holding global food security hostage… Today, zero vessels passed through Ukraine's Black Sea maritime corridor — right at peak harvest. This is deliberate economic and humanitarian terror. Just as Iran targeted energy routes in the Strait of Hormuz, Russia is now targeting global food markets in the Black Sea." Ukraine requested an urgent UN Security Council meeting for July 27 [1].

# Analysis

[Phillips O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) frames the crisis as the output-side complement to the input-side fertilizer disruption from the Hormuz crisis. Russia has been forced to import gasoline, diesel, and jet fuel it used to provide — reducing outward flows and depriving Russia of income. Ukraine's European partners should be able to deal with the disruption better than their enemies, but the situation remains dire. The parallel between Russia's Black Sea grain campaign and Iran's Hormuz energy campaign is structural: both target the adversary's export revenue stream through maritime chokepoint denial [1].
