# Background

Talks on transferring civilian nuclear technology from the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) to [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html) have been underway for nearly two decades. The most concrete round occurred under President Biden, when Washington sought to tie a nuclear deal to Saudi-Israeli normalization as part of a broader Middle East peace framework. Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman (MBS) signaled in September 2023 that an agreement was close, but the Gaza war from October 2023 sank Riyadh's appetite for the comprehensive package [1].

The [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) administration revived the negotiations in April 2025 — but this time as a standalone bilateral accord, decoupled from any Saudi-Israeli normalization plan. For Washington, the agreement serves multiple purposes: arresting Saudi Arabia's drift toward deeper ties with [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) and [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html), reaffirming US primacy in the kingdom, and generating billions in contracts for American nuclear firms. For Riyadh, the reactors support the "Vision 2030" reform programme by freeing domestic oil for export while meeting long-term electricity demand [1].

The agreement was concluded while the [US-Iran War 2026]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) was actively underway, with [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) firing missiles and drones at Saudi territory as it had since the war's outbreak in late February. This wartime context sharpened the proliferation question: whether MBS might eventually redirect a civilian programme toward military purposes [1].

# What Happened

In July 2026, Washington and Riyadh finalized a civilian nuclear cooperation agreement. Saudi Arabia plans to build two reactors initially, with the option to add more. Two provisions drew particular scrutiny [1]:

- **Domestic uranium enrichment:** Unlike the 2009 US-UAE agreement — which required [UAE]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/uae.html) to forgo enrichment entirely and to accede to the IAEA Additional Protocol with its intrusive inspections — the Saudi accord permits Riyadh to enrich uranium on its own soil, with inspections described as less thorough than the UAE gold standard.
- **Inspection regime:** US officials insisted that cooperation with American firms would ensure full-scope oversight of the Saudi programme, though the durability of that assurance remains to be tested over time.

# Analysis

## The Military-Flip Risk

The possibility that Saudi Arabia's civilian programme could pivot to military use is not speculative. MBS stated repeatedly in 2018 that if Iran developed a nuclear weapon, Saudi Arabia would follow suit. Although the crown prince has since cultivated a more measured public persona, that declaratory position likely still reflects the thinking of Saudi leadership. By granting domestic enrichment rights, the agreement converts a theoretical Saudi bomb into a concrete option — even if Riyadh currently has no intention of exercising it [1].

The context compounds the risk. Iran's militarized new leadership is unlikely to abandon the nuclear weapons programme it has pursued since 1987, especially now that the Strait of Hormuz has proven to be a usable asymmetric weapon. The combination of an Iran determined to acquire a deterrent and a Saudi Arabia with enrichment capability makes a nuclear-armed Middle East more probable [1].

## The Signal Effect and Proliferation Cascade

The second problem is precedent. The 2009 US-UAE agreement set a gold standard: a regional state voluntarily forswearing enrichment and accepting the Additional Protocol's stringent inspections. The Saudi deal erodes that benchmark [1].

- **UAE:** Abu Dhabi is expected to seek retroactive parity, demanding the same enrichment rights now granted to Riyadh.
- **[Turkey]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/turkey.html):** Ankara and Riyadh are rivals for regional leadership; a Saudi nuclear programme could trigger a Turkish response.
- **Iran:** Tehran will cite Saudi enrichment terms in future negotiations, insisting on at least equivalent rights with even greater insistence than before.

[Guido Steinberg]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/guido-steinberg.html) argues that these dynamics make a Middle East with three to five nuclear powers substantially more likely than before the agreement. The erosion of the UAE precedent, combined with Iran's continued nuclear ambition and Turkey's regional rivalry, creates conditions for a proliferation cascade [1].

## Strategic Rationale Despite the Risks

Despite the proliferation concerns, the agreement reflects convergent interests. Saudi Arabia's vulnerability was laid bare by the [Hormuz crisis]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/hormuz-toll-system.html) and Iranian missile strikes — the war demonstrated that even a US-aligned kingdom cannot fully insulate itself from Gulf conflict. Although Riyadh is dissatisfied with Washington's prosecution of the Iran war, MBS sees no alternative to the US alliance. The nuclear deal anchors that relationship and channels Saudi energy diversification through American technology rather than Chinese or Russian alternatives [1].
