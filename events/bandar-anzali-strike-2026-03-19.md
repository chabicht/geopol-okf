# Background

Bandar Anzali is an Iranian port city on the Caspian Sea coast, hosting naval vessels, headquarters facilities, and a shipyard. Its strategic significance derives from its role as a node in the Iran-Russia arms-smuggling route: [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) supplies [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) with Shahed drones, drone-production expertise, artillery shells, and short-range ballistic missiles for the [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html), with some of this materiel transiting through Caspian ports. In return, Russia provides Iran with advanced drones and tactical intelligence [1].

The [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) had been underway since February 28, 2026. [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html), operating as a parallel actor with overlapping but not identical objectives, sought to disrupt Iran's military logistics and its support for Russia's Ukraine campaign — both of which flowed through Caspian infrastructure.

# What Happened

On March 18–19, 2026, Israel conducted a strike on Bandar Anzali port — the first military strike in the Caspian Sea in modern history. The strike damaged naval vessels, headquarters facilities, and a shipyard. The targeting was consistent with an effort to disrupt the Iran-Russia arms-smuggling route that flows through the port, rather than a conventional naval engagement [1].

# Analysis

## Opening the Caspian as a Theater

The Bandar Anzali strike's significance extends well beyond its tactical results. By conducting the first military strike in the Caspian Sea, Israel opened a new theater in the broader Middle East conflict. The Caspian had previously been a sanctuary from the wars raging to its south and west — the US-Iran conflict in the Gulf and the Russia-Ukraine conflict in the Black Sea. The strike demonstrated that the Caspian is no longer insulated from either conflict [1].

## Disrupting the Iran-Russia Arms Route

The strike's targeting — naval vessels, headquarters, and a shipyard — was designed to degrade the infrastructure through which Iranian weapons flow to Russia. Iran's military support for Russia's Ukraine campaign has been substantial: Shahed drones, drone-production expertise, artillery shells, and short-range ballistic missiles have all flowed through Caspian logistics networks. Disrupting these flows serves both Israeli and Ukrainian interests, linking the two wars through a single strike [1].

## Implications for Caspian Stability

The strike placed all six Caspian littoral states on notice that the sea is now a potential battlefield. The trans-Caspian states promoting the Middle Corridor have a shared incentive to keep the Caspian a commercial waterway, but Israel's strike demonstrated that external actors can project force into the basin regardless of littoral state preferences.
