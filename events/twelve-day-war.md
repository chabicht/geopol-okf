# Background

The [US-Iran War 2026]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) has been the most significant US military engagement in the Middle East since the Iraq War. It encompasses direct US-Iran hostilities, Iranian strikes on [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html), and Iranian attacks on Arab Gulf states. The war has placed extraordinary demands on US munitions inventories — particularly missile defense interceptors and long-range strike munitions — that were already strained by prior Middle East engagements [1].

Before the Twelve Day War, US munitions had been heavily consumed in the regional war precipitated by Hamas's October 7, 2023 attack on Israel. Under President Biden, Operation Poseidon Archer against the Houthis used hundreds of SM-2/3/6 ship-based interceptors and at least 135 Tomahawk missiles. The April and October 2024 Iran-Israel exchanges of fire drew additional interceptor expenditures. These campaigns depleted inventories that the post–Cold War defense industrial base — restructured for efficient peacetime production rather than surge — was not designed to replenish quickly [1].

# What Happened

The "Twelve Day War" was a discrete phase of the broader US-Iran War, characterized by an intense air exchange between Iran and Israel with direct US participation. The CSIS analysis references it as occurring in summer 2026; the exact start and end dates are not specified in the source material, but the event is treated as a distinct operational phase with its own munitions-expenditure profile [1].

During the Twelve Day War:

- US forces fired approximately **150 THAAD (Terminal High Altitude Area Defense) interceptors** — a major expenditure of one of the Pentagon's most critical missile defense assets.
- The US also fired **24 Tomahawk land-attack cruise missiles** during the same period [1].

The exchange represents the single largest documented munitions expenditure event within the US-Iran War, and a critical data point in CSIS's assessment of US munitions shortages.

# Analysis

## Munitions Inventory Impact

[Mark F. Cancian]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/mark-f-cancian.html) and [Chris H. Park]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/chris-h-park.html) document that the US has used approximately half of its initial inventory for three key munitions — Patriot, THAAD, and PrSM — and about one-third for the rest (JASSM, SM-2/3/6, and Tomahawk). The 150 THAAD interceptors fired during the Twelve Day War represent a substantial drawdown of a system that is both expensive and slow to replenish. Current deliveries reflect funding from before FY 2024, as it takes two to four years for munitions to be delivered once a contract is signed [1].

The immediate problem is not money but time. Production is surging — the process began under Biden, accelerated with the Trump administration's reconciliation bill, and would accelerate further under the proposed FY 2027 budget — but the window of vulnerability persists until production catches up with demand, which is several years away [1].

## The Broader Munitions Crisis

The Twelve Day War did not create the munitions shortage in isolation. CSIS identifies six interlocking causes [1]:

1. **The Iran war itself** — the most immediate driver, with the Twelve Day War as its peak expenditure event.
2. **Post–Cold War strategy** — the shift from great-power war planning to two limited regional conflicts reduced procurement requirements.
3. **Industrial base contraction** — the 1993 "Last Supper" consolidation reduced aerospace and defense primes from 51 to 5, eliminating surge capacity.
4. **Budget competition** — munitions compete poorly against platforms (aircraft, ships, vehicles) in the Pentagon's budget process because platforms contribute to deterrence for 30 years while munitions sit in bunkers.
5. **Recent war consumption** — Biden-era Houthi campaigns, the 2024 Iran-Israel exchanges, Christmas 2025 strikes on ISIS in Nigeria (16 Tomahawks), and the January 2026 Venezuela raid (several dozen JASSMs) all drew down inventories.
6. **Ukraine transfers** — a minor factor overall ($85 billion in total aid), but significant for four specific munitions also used in the Iran war: Patriot interceptors (~600 sent to Ukraine), ATACMS, HARM, and Stinger.

## Implications for a Pacific Conflict

The Twelve Day War's munitions consumption is most concerning in the context of a potential future conflict with [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html). The same munitions depleted in the Iran war — THAAD, Patriot, SM-2/3/6, Tomahawk, JASSM — would be critical in a western Pacific scenario. While enough remains for any plausible scenario in the war against Iran itself, the risk lies in a potential future war, particularly with China but also with Russia or North Korea [1].

Long-range anti-ship munitions (LRASM, Maritime Strike Tomahawk, Naval Strike Missile) have not been significantly used in the Iran war but would be rapidly depleted in a conflict with a near-peer naval power. The Twelve Day War thus represents not just a historical event but a warning about the depth of US munitions inventories for a two-theater scenario [1].
