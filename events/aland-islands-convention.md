# Background

The Åland Islands lie in the Baltic Sea between the Finnish and Swedish coasts — an archipelago of roughly 6,500 islands and reefs inhabited by about 30,000 Swedish-speaking Finns. The islands were part of the Kingdom of Sweden for centuries before passing to Russian rule in 1809. During the Crimean War, Russia constructed a fortification on Åland that was subsequently destroyed by British and French forces. The 1856 Treaty of Paris prohibited refortification — the first legal layer of what would become Åland's demilitarized status [1].

After Finland gained independence from Russia in 1917, the status of Åland was contested between Sweden and Finland. The League of Nations Council resolved the dispute in 1921, awarding sovereignty to Finland while granting the islands cultural autonomy and establishing a convention that prohibits fortifications, troop stationing, foreign military forces, and unauthorized military overflight. This convention is a full demilitarization — unlike the [Svalbard Treaty]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/svalbard-treaty.html), which bars only naval bases and fortifications [1].

The regime has been reinforced by successive agreements. A bilateral Finnish-Soviet treaty in 1940, confirmed by the Paris Peace Treaty of 1947 and reaffirmed in a 1992 Protocol, established a Russian consulate in Mariehamn to oversee compliance — a consulate that remains open today. The demilitarized status thus rests on multiple overlapping legal instruments, not a single treaty [1].

# Current Situation

Åland's significance lies in its position as a potential control point astride sea lines of communication and air corridors between Sweden and Finland. That concern intensified after both countries joined NATO. Alpo Rusi described Åland as "the Achilles' heel of Finland's defence." Pekka Toveri argued that the demilitarization clause no longer holds. A 2022 poll found that 58 percent of mainland Finns supported military presence on the islands, though Ålanders themselves remained more cautious [1].

## The Debate Over Revision

Russia's anti-access/area-denial (A2/AD) capabilities in the Baltic are formidable and do not depend on Åland. Missiles from Kaliningrad and St. Petersburg can threaten much of the Baltic Sea. Finnish air and naval forces have been enhanced since 2022, and the combined Nordic air fleet exceeds 250 fourth- and fifth-generation aircraft. Remilitarization of Åland would not transform the military balance — the islands are not the pivot on which Baltic security turns [1].

Gardberg's 1995 survey remains relevant: increased emphasis on mobile amphibious units decreases the need for stationary fortifications. Surveillance aircraft, naval minelayers, and fast-response amphibious units need not be stationed on the islands. The convention does not prohibit coast guard or police presence, over-the-horizon radar, or P-8 maritime patrol operations conducted outside the demilitarized zone. Much of what defense planners need can be accomplished within the existing legal framework [1].

# Analysis

## Integration Without Remilitarization

The 30,000 Swedish-speaking Finns living in the demilitarized zone are unconvinced about jettisoning the status quo. The Åland Islands Peace Institute continues to produce peer-reviewed publications on the regime's viability. At the May 2025 Nordic Summit, participating states agreed to include Greenland, the Faroe Islands, and Åland in future Nordic ministerial meetings — a signal that integration and cooperation can proceed without remilitarization [1].

The [Svalbard Treaty]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/svalbard-treaty.html) has lasted over a century; the demilitarized status of Åland traces back to 1856. The restrictions pose more of an inconvenience than a strategic liability. Finnish forces are adequate without permanent military presence on the islands, and Article 7(ii) of the convention already provides for emergency defensive measures if circumstances require. The question for [Finland]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/finland.html) and Norway is not whether they can defend themselves under these treaties — they can — but whether the diplomatic and normative costs of abrogation outweigh the modest military gains, as explored in [Layered Security]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/layered-security.html) [1].

# Key Dynamics

- **Full demilitarization**: Unlike Svalbard, Åland's convention prohibits all military presence — fortifications, troop stationing, foreign forces, and unauthorized overflight.
- **Strategic geography**: Åland astride Baltic sea lines of communication and air corridors between Sweden and Finland gives it outsized symbolic importance relative to its modest military significance.
- **Russian consulate**: The ongoing Russian consulate presence in Mariehamn provides Moscow a compliance-monitoring foothold.
- **A2/AD independence**: Russia's Baltic threat posture relies on Kaliningrad and St. Petersburg missiles, not Åland — remilitarization would not shift the balance.
- **Local agency**: Ålanders' caution about revision constrains Finnish political maneuvering, even as mainland opinion shifts toward militarization.
