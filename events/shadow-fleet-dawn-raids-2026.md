# Background

Russia's "shadow fleet" — a loose collection of aging and reflagged tankers operating outside Western insurance and regulatory frameworks — has become the lifeline of the Russian economy, enabling [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) to export crude oil to buyers in [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) and India despite [sanctions]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/sanctions-and-financial-warfare.html) imposed after the full-scale invasion of [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html). The fleet numbers approximately 1,300 tankers according to Ukrainian intelligence, with roughly three shadow-fleet tankers transiting the Baltic and North Seas daily from the Russian ports of Primorsk and Ust-Luga [1].

International maritime law guarantees the right of peaceful passage through territorial waters, meaning vessels cannot be stopped simply for belonging to the shadow fleet. European states have instead relied on legal pretexts: forged papers, manipulated AIS (Automatic Identification System) signals, and false flag operations — violations that render a vessel "stateless" or illegal under maritime law and provide grounds for boarding [1].

# What Happened

A series of increasingly assertive European boarding operations against shadow-fleet tankers unfolded from March through June 2026:

- **March 20, 2026:** French special forces boarded the tanker *Deyna* from a helicopter off the French North Sea coast.
- **May 3, 2026:** The Swedish coast guard, together with police, stopped the *Jin Hui* off the Swedish coast — the fifth vessel detained by Sweden in 2026.
- **June 14, 2026:** UK elite forces stormed the *Smyrtos* in a night operation in the English Channel.

Over a dozen vessels were detained, inspected, or seized in European waters during the first half of 2026 — a significant increase over previous years. Sweden's particularly aggressive posture has caused many shadow-fleet tankers to avoid Swedish waters entirely, rerouting along the [German]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/germany.html) Baltic coast near Rügen, according to a June 2026 Greenpeace analysis [1].

# Analysis

## The Scale Gap

Despite the headline-grabbing operations, European interdictions reach only a fraction of the shadow fleet. Julian Pawlak of the Stiftung Wissenschaft und Politik (SWP) estimates that approximately three shadow-fleet tankers transit the Baltic daily, and nearly half of Russia's total oil exports flow through the Baltic. Of roughly 1,000 annual transits, only about ten had been stopped by June 2026 — approximately two percent of the traffic. European actions, while growing in frequency and boldness, remain a pinprick against the scale of Russian seaborne oil exports [1].

## Legal Constraints and the German Eventin Precedent

The legal basis for interdictions is narrow. Vessels are stopped for document fraud, AIS manipulation, or false flags — not for shadow-fleet membership. Each boarding carries escalation risk: Russia could interpret it as a hostile act, and shadow-fleet tankers increasingly carry armed security personnel or are escorted by Russian warships [1].

The legal aftermath has often favored the shipowners. In Germany's only shadow-fleet action — the January 2025 seizure of the *Eventin* off Rügen — the Greifswald Finance Court invalidated the customs seizure, and the Federal Tax Court confirmed that ruling in March 2026. The owner invoked "distress port law," which prohibits seizing ships that entered port under duress. The case remains unresolved, and the costs of vessel custody fall on the seizing state: maintaining the ship, provisioning the crew for months, and managing an ecological time bomb moored in domestic waters. These deterrents have made many states, including Germany, reluctant to act [1].

## Ukraine's Drone Campaign Against the Shadow Fleet

While European states conduct limited boardings, [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html) has pursued a far more destructive approach. Ukrainian surface and underwater drones have damaged or disabled 50 to 60 oil tankers — roughly four to five percent of Russia's total shadow fleet. Among tankers serving Black Sea routes, the attrition rate is estimated at 25 to 35 percent [1].

The economic impact is substantial. KSE Institute calculations indicate that March 2026 drone attacks on the Baltic ports of Ust-Luga and Primorsk alone cost Russia approximately $1.76 billion in lost oil revenue. At Ust-Luga, departing tankers dropped from 16–17 per week to just two. Together with the subsequently attacked Black Sea port of Novorossiysk, these three ports handle approximately 60 percent of Russia's seaborne oil exports. The drone strikes effectively erase the revenue gains Russia accrued from higher oil prices driven by the [Hormuz crisis]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/hormuz-toll-system.html) [1].

## The Need for Coordinated European Action

Pawlak advocates a more coordinated European approach. Currently, each state pursues its own strategy with no common line. A joint framework — particularly around false-flag violations, where an established legal basis exists — could enable more systematic interdictions. Pawlak identifies Germany as one of the countries not yet fully exploiting available legal tools. Even small delays and complications in oil-sale revenue, accumulated across many operations, impose meaningful costs on the Russian state budget [1].
