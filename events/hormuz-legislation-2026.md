# Background

The [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) demonstrated that [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html)'s leverage over the [Strait of Hormuz]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/hormuz-toll-system.html) is structural rather than tactical — it persists regardless of the conventional force balance. Prior to this legislation, Iran's chokepoint control was exercised through military coercion: missile and drone threats against vessels that disregarded Iranian directives, alongside a negotiated toll-system proposal channeled through [Oman]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/oman.html). The legislation moves the mechanism from the diplomatic track to the legislative track — from negotiation to codification.

# What Happened

Iran's parliament began reviewing the "Strategic Action for the Security and Sustainable Development of the Strait of Hormuz and the Persian Gulf" bill. The proposed law would:

- Bar vessels and cargo linked to the United States, Israel, and other "hostile nations" from transit.
- Impose transit fees of 5–7% of cargo value — approximately $7.5M–$10.5M per VLCC voyage for a tanker carrying ~$150M in oil.
- Establish financial penalties and compensation requirements for non-compliant vessels.
- Convert Iran's wartime control into a permanent legal and economic regime governing the strait.

The bill is distinct from the Oman-mediated [Hormuz Toll System]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/hormuz-toll-system.html) negotiations: it is unilateral legislation rather than a negotiated agreement; it bans US/Israeli/hostile-nation cargo entirely rather than charging fees on all traffic; and it codifies rather than bargains for the legality of Iranian maritime authority.

# Analysis

[Robert Pape]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/robert-pape.html) frames the legislation as a strategic shift more profound than any single military action — the transition from military coercion to institutionalized maritime authority. If enacted, Iran "would no longer be threatening to control the Strait of Hormuz. It would be legislating that control as the normal operating rule for international commerce." [1]

Drawing on his *Bombing to Win* thesis, Pape argues that air power alone rarely compels a determined adversary to abandon a core national interest: it can destroy capabilities, raise costs, and delay programs, but seldom changes the underlying political objective. The Hormuz legislation confirms this limit — Iran's strategic calculus has not fundamentally changed; it has moved from survival to ambition. Imposing substantial military and economic costs did not produce political capitulation. The bill is the signature of a strategic calculus that airpower reached its familiar limit but did not reverse. [1]

Pape argues that no US president could accept these terms without gutting American credibility — accepting Iranian authority to tax international commerce, prohibit US-linked shipping, and dictate Hormuz passage would mean accepting "a new regional order written in Tehran" and "the collapse of the perception of America's escalation dominance, invincibility, and leadership around the world." Yet escalation carries its own enormous risks. This defines the [Escalation Theory Critique]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/escalation-theory-critique.html) trap: neither accepting Iranian terms nor escalating restores the pre-war strategic position. [1]

If the terms cannot be accepted, Pape defines Stage 3 of the Escalation Trap as the transition from coercion to physical control — an amphibious or ground campaign to secure the coastline, reopen shipping, and deny Iran the ability to reimpose restrictions. The unairpowerable nature of the coastline — 600 miles long — combined with mounting domestic political pressure drives what Pape calls the "Hail Mary" ground war threshold: a president may gamble on a limited ground operation for symbolic victory before the midterms because he has little to lose. [1]
