# Background

The Rhine is the bloodstream of Europe's largest integrated chemical manufacturing system — a production commons connecting BASF's Ludwigshafen complex with Covestro, Evonik, Bayer, LyondellBasell, LANXESS, INEOS, and dozens of other chemical, refining, steel, and manufacturing sites along its banks [1]. Physical flows of naphtha, gases, acids, solvents, intermediates, fuels, cooling water, and finished chemicals move by barge between these sites in a tightly integrated network that BASF calls its *Verbund* — where the output of one plant becomes the input of another [1].

Kaub is the critical bottleneck: a narrow, shallow section of the river between the great industrial systems of northern and southern [Germany]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/germany.html). When water levels at Kaub fall below navigable thresholds, the Rhine effectively splits into two transport systems — the northern river remains connected to Rotterdam, Antwerp, and Amsterdam, while the Upper Rhine industrial basin to the south loses its continuous river connection to the North Sea ports [1].

The 2018 Rhine crisis established the precedent. BASF reported that restricted raw-material supply and insufficient cooling-water availability simultaneously constrained production at Ludwigshafen, reducing its 2018 earnings by approximately €250 million [1]. Under normal conditions, roughly 40% of incoming volumes at Ludwigshafen arrive by ship; during the 2018 crisis, river deliveries became almost impossible for extended periods [1]. Chemical companies subsequently adapted by investing in low-draft vessels, expanding alternative transport arrangements, and increasing storage buffers — but each adaptation operates within physical limits that merely shift the threshold at which failure begins [1].

# Current Situation

In August 2026, exceptionally low water has pushed the Rhine to record lows around Kaub. Commercial barge traffic has become severely restricted, some sailings have stopped altogether, and freight is being pushed onto roads and railways that cannot approach the river's carrying capacity [1]. The crisis coincides with a Super El Niño climate event affecting European river systems [1].

The first signs of production stress have surfaced through force majeure declarations across the chemical corridor [1]:

- **Covestro** declared force majeure on polyether polyols at Dormagen, citing transport capacity lost on the Rhine that could not be fully replaced by road and rail [1].
- **BASF** declared force majeure on DINP and DPHP plasticisers at Ludwigshafen, citing extreme weather and Rhine logistics constraints, and later declared force majeure on several European surfactants following disrupted raw-material deliveries [1].
- **LyondellBasell** declared force majeure on butadiene from its Wesseling unit after low Rhine levels restricted feedstock deliveries [1].
- **LANXESS** was reported to have declared force majeure on phthalic anhydride at Uerdingen, though the company had not publicly confirmed the declaration when reported [1].
- **Evonik** acknowledged production constraints at Marl [1].

The pattern of force majeure declarations is itself diagnostic: their significance depends on where in the chemical tree they appear. Disruptions to base-network chemicals such as ethylene, propylene, chlorine, ammonia, and butadiene carry enormous weight because they support large downstream branches [1]. LyondellBasell's butadiene declaration is particularly significant because butadiene sits far upstream, feeding synthetic rubber and polymer chains that eventually become tyres, hoses, gaskets, and engineered components throughout transport and manufacturing [1].

The crisis is compounded by a dual constraint: chemical plants and power stations use enormous quantities of water for cooling, so extreme low-flow periods simultaneously reduce both transport capacity and cooling flexibility [1]. A train can deliver naphtha, but a train cannot deliver a functioning Rhine-sized heat sink [1].

The global context amplifies the Rhine's significance. Earlier in 2026, Hormuz-related disruptions to Middle Eastern naphtha flows forced Asian petrochemical producers to cut operating rates, shut units, and declare force majeure [1]. With both European and Asian producing regions under simultaneous pressure, the assumption that spare production, inventory, or logistics capacity will always exist elsewhere becomes less reliable [1]. In a more fragmented, multipolar trading system, governments increasingly treat critical chemicals, energy systems, and minerals as national security matters — shrinking the pool of substitutes that actually matter when multiple regions experience concurrent stress [1].
