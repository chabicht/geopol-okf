# Background

The [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) has degraded the perceived reliability of US security guarantees in the Gulf. As American power has been "knocked down several pegs," a power vacuum has opened in the regional security architecture. States fill vacuums. The Mecca Joint Defense Agreement between [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html), Turkey, and [Pakistan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/pakistan.html) emerged as a diversification move — an attempt to hedge against US decline by constructing a regional defense pact that does not depend on Washington.

# What Happened

A defense agreement was reached among Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan, framed defensively and described by participating governments as not directed at any country. The pact was concluded in the context of the broader post-war regional realignment, alongside the [Houthi war on Saudi oil]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) and Iran's escalating demands.

The agreement coincides with a period of domestic political turbulence in the US: the [Hegseth generals purge]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) — in which military leaders seen as insufficiently compliant with presidential directives have been retired — reinforces the perception that US security commitments are subject to domestic political volatility. Trump told 800 generals and admirals at the Pentagon on September 30, 2025 that the military would treat liberal cities as training grounds and that they could "get on board or get out."

# Analysis

[Robert Pape]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/robert-pape.html) offers a structural reading: the pact fills a power vacuum left by US decline. As America has been knocked down several pegs of power, states fill the resulting vacuum. The agreement is not "a new NATO" — it lacks the integrated command structure that made NATO a war-fighting alliance. NATO had a US four-star commander running integrated command of all alliance forces; Pape does not see Turkey and Saudi Arabia handing over control of their forces to a Pakistani general. [1]

The pact is unlikely to be tested by a conventional force commitment. Pape questions whether Turkey and Saudi Arabia would actually send conventional forces if Pakistan faced a crisis — and Turkey's internal vulnerabilities constrain its intervention appetite. Turkey has 18 million Kurds; stirring Kurdish unrest inside Turkey is a known counter-leverage point that limits how far Ankara can project power. [1]

The agreement is best read as a signal of regional hedging rather than a tested war-fighting alliance. It complements the [NATO Ankara Summit]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/nato-ankara-summit-2026.html) architecture as a parallel regional structure, and it reflects the same dynamic that produced Iran's [six-point demands]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/iran-six-point-demands-2026-08-09.html): a Gulf regional order increasingly written outside Washington's exclusive influence.
