# Background

The Pentagon has dispersed US assets away from vulnerable Gulf bases — Al Udeid Air Base in Qatar and facilities in Bahrain — to Jordan's Muwaffaq Salti Air Base. Iran has grown its arsenal of precision ballistic missiles, maneuvering warheads, and mass-produced drones, and the forward concentration of aircraft close to Iran has become structurally unsafe. The dispersal doctrine has elevated Jordan to a strategic role it did not previously hold: roughly 4,000 US personnel are now stationed across the country, supporting advanced fighters, drones, intelligence assets, and air-defense systems. Jordan is not simply "another American base." It has become a pillar of the geographic architecture that allows the United States to sustain military operations while reducing the vulnerability of its frontline Gulf facilities. The IRGC explicitly stated that its Aerospace Force targeted a US military CENTCOM center inside Jordan — a rare public attribution that named the type of facility under attack.

# The Strike

July 29, 2026. Shortly after Trump's meeting with Netanyahu, Iran launched an attempted surprise ballistic missile attack targeting US forces in Jordan. All incoming projectiles were successfully intercepted by air defenses; no US casualties were reported. The strike shattered a brief multi-day lull in the regional conflict. Global oil prices jumped 5% following news of renewed hostilities. The IRGC publicly claimed responsibility and identified the target as a CENTCOM center — an unusual degree of explicitness for an Iranian attack on US infrastructure. The timing, coming right after a presidential meeting with the Israeli Prime Minister, signaled that Iran's aerospace reach was now operating on its own decision cycle rather than as a function of US escalation.

# Analysis

Pape frames this as confirmation of his June 15 "Period of Maximum Leverage" forecast — Iran has entered its window of maximum leverage as oil inventories run to tank bottoms, and is using that window to shift from absorbing American pressure to raising the political and military costs of sustaining US operations across the region. The strike marks entry into the spiral phase: the conflict is no longer characterized primarily by isolated retaliation, but is becoming a geographically interconnected contest across multiple theaters — military strikes, proxy operations, maritime disruption, and political signaling that reinforce one another.

Iran's strategic objective has shifted from destroying American military power to making that power progressively more expensive, politically difficult, and operationally distant. Every additional mile of dispersal increases sortie times, refueling requirements, logistics costs, and operational tempo for US air power while raising the political pressure on host governments. Jordan is valuable precisely because Washington increasingly depends upon it — and that same dependence made the IRGC's choice of target a strategic one rather than a symbolic one. The strike demonstrates that Iran has begun contesting not merely American forces, but the entire regional system through which the United States projects military power.
