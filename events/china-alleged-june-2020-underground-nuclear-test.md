# Background

Since August 2019, US State Department compliance reports have suggested that [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) and [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) are probably conducting secret underground nuclear testing in violation of international testing moratoria and the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (CTBT). Although the CTBT has not formally entered into force — it requires ratification by 44 designated states, of which only 35 have done so — an internationally respected norm against testing has become established under Article 18 of the Vienna Convention, which obliges signatories not to undermine a treaty's object and purpose [1].

China has signed but not ratified the CTBT. It has conducted only 45 nuclear tests historically (by 1996), compared with 1,054 by the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) and 715 by the Soviet Union/Russia. China's tests employed comparatively rudimentary diagnostic instrumentation, leaving Beijing with less confidence in its arsenal's safety, security, and reliability than its peers — and thus stronger incentives to obtain additional test data for modernization [1].

Russia revoked its CTBT ratification in 2023, in the context of the [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html), to put itself on an equal footing with the United States. In October 2025, Trump announced that the US would resume nuclear testing, citing Russian and Chinese activities — though Energy Secretary Chris Wright quickly rolled back the announcement [1].

# What Happened

In February 2026, the United States presented new evidence at the United Nations alleging that China conducted a secret underground nuclear weapons test on June 22, 2020, at the Lop Nur nuclear test site in northwestern China [1].

## Seismic Evidence

A CTBTO International Monitoring System (IMS) seismic station at Makanchi, [Kazakhstan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/kazakhstan.html), recorded a magnitude 2.75 seismic event on June 22, 2020. The signal exhibited two clearly distinguishable explosions approximately 12 seconds apart. This seismic signature corresponds neither to natural earthquakes nor to typical mining explosions. The event was also recorded by national monitoring stations [1].

The US State Department estimates the nuclear yield at 10 tons of TNT equivalent in the coupled configuration. However, if the test was conducted in an underground cavern with seismic decoupling — where the explosion's energy is attenuated by the surrounding void — the measurable yield can be reduced by a factor of 20 to 40. Under a decoupling hypothesis, the actual yield could be 200 to 400 tons of TNT equivalent, which strongly suggests a nuclear explosion rather than a conventional or subcritical detonation [1].

The dual seismic signature is consistent with a decoupled nuclear test followed by cavern collapse: the initial explosion generates a first, attenuated signal, and the subsequent collapse produces a second, time-delayed signal — precisely what was recorded at Makanchi [1].

## Satellite Imagery and Site Activity

Publicly available satellite imagery analysis from 2020 to 2024 documents extensive construction at Lop Nur, including expansion of roads, electrical infrastructure, storage facilities for high explosives, extensive tunnel construction, vertical boreholes, horizontal tunnel systems, and diagnostic cabling. These activities raise verification issues that are difficult to resolve absent CTBT ratification. Similar infrastructure development has been observed at the Nevada National Security Site and Russia's Novaya Zemlya test site, complicating any assessment [1].

## China's Response

China has categorically rejected the allegations but has issued no formal counter-explanations, no counter-allegations, and has not responded to calls for increased nuclear transparency. The absence of convincing Chinese counterarguments reinforces the impression that the US accusations are to be taken seriously. The Biden administration had invited P5 observers to the Nevada site as a transparency measure; China and Russia both declined [1].

# Analysis

## China's Strategic Motivation

[Philipp Rombach]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/philipp-rombach.html) argues that China has compelling reasons to conduct secret nuclear tests. With only 45 historical tests using inferior diagnostic equipment, Beijing lacks confidence in its arsenal's reliability. Test data would validate computer simulations, calibrate models, and potentially support development of new sub-kiloton tactical warhead designs — providing additional nuclear response options vis-à-vis the United States, particularly in a [Taiwan contingency]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/taiwan-standoff.html) [1].

China's arsenal has grown from approximately 300 to 600 warheads and is projected to reach 1,500 by 2035. The alleged test could support warhead miniaturization, improved manoeuvrability, and survivability — consistent with China's objectives of expanding nuclear capabilities in the western Pacific and achieving regional hegemony [1].

## Erosion of Testing Norms and Proliferation Incentives

Rombach warns that the erosion of international testing norms creates cascading risks. If major nuclear powers resume underground testing, secondary nuclear powers (India, Pakistan, North Korea) may follow. More significantly, latent proliferator states — including Poland, South Korea, [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html), and even [Germany]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/germany.html) — could gain new incentives to pursue nuclear testing. The norm against testing has acted as a barrier to proliferation; its erosion would lower that barrier [1].

## Why US Testing Resumption Would Benefit China Most

Experts largely agree that China would benefit most from a full resumption of nuclear testing. The United States has the most extensive testing history, the most advanced science-based stockpile stewardship capabilities, and therefore the most to lose from a return to testing. China, with its limited test base, has the most to gain. From a strategic perspective, a US decision to resume testing would play into Chinese and Russian hands [1].

The US maintains its unilateral testing moratorium under a clause that allows resumption if another state tests. The allegations against China could provide the legal justification — but doing so would accelerate the very norm erosion that disadvantages Washington [1].

## What Germany and Europe Can Do

Rombach argues that [Germany]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/germany.html) and the EU should take the allegations seriously, exert diplomatic pressure on China for greater transparency, and support confidence-building measures — including on-site inspections, more comprehensive data sharing, and voluntary advance notification of civilian underground explosions. Germany should also draw up an independent assessment of the evidence and emphasize to the US the importance of comprehensive testing moratoria. At a minimum, Berlin can help ensure adequate funding for the IAEA and the CTBTO's International Monitoring System [1].
