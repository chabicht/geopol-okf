# Background

Since August 2019, US State Department compliance reports have suggested that [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) and [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) are probably conducting secret underground nuclear testing in violation of international testing moratoria and the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (CTBT). Although the CTBT has not formally entered into force — it requires ratification by 44 designated states, of which only 35 have done so — an internationally respected norm against testing has become established under Article 18 of the Vienna Convention, which obliges signatories not to undermine a treaty's object and purpose [1].

China has signed but not ratified the CTBT. It has conducted only 45 nuclear tests historically (by 1996), compared with 1,054 by the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) and 715 by the Soviet Union/Russia. China's tests employed comparatively rudimentary diagnostic instrumentation, leaving Beijing with less confidence in its arsenal's safety, security, and reliability than its peers — and thus stronger incentives to obtain additional test data for modernization [1].

Russia revoked its CTBT ratification in 2023, in the context of the [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html), to put itself on an equal footing with the United States. In October 2025, Trump announced that the US would resume nuclear testing, citing Russian and Chinese activities — though Energy Secretary Chris Wright quickly rolled back the announcement [1].

# What Happened

In February 2026, the United States presented new evidence at the United Nations alleging that China conducted a secret underground nuclear weapons test on June 22, 2020, at the Lop Nur nuclear test site in northwestern China [1].

## The Evidence

The case rests on three reinforcing layers. The first is a seismic signal. On June 22, 2020, a CTBTO International Monitoring System station at Makanchi, [Kazakhstan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/kazakhstan.html), recorded a magnitude 2.75 event beneath the Lop Nur test site, with two distinguishable explosions roughly 12 seconds apart — a signature consistent neither with natural earthquakes nor with typical mining blasts, and corroborated by national monitoring stations [1]. The dual signal maps onto a decoupled nuclear test followed by cavern collapse: the initial explosion, attenuated by the surrounding void, produces a first, weaker signal, and the subsequent collapse produces a second, time-delayed one. The US State Department estimates the coupled yield at 10 tons of TNT equivalent, but seismic decoupling can reduce the measurable yield by a factor of 20 to 40, placing the true yield at 200 to 400 tons — well above what conventional or subcritical detonations would produce, and strongly suggestive of a nuclear explosion [1].

The second layer is the physical site. Satellite imagery analysis from 2020 to 2024 documents extensive construction at Lop Nur — road expansion, electrical infrastructure, high-explosive storage, tunnel systems, vertical boreholes, horizontal tunnels, and diagnostic cabling — activity difficult to reconcile with purely permitted subcritical work, and raising verification issues that China's CTBT non-ratification leaves unresolved. Comparable infrastructure has appeared at the Nevada National Security Site and Russia's Novaya Zemlya test site, complicating any single-site assessment [1].

The third layer is the absence of a counter-case. China has categorically rejected the allegations but has offered no formal counter-explanation, no counter-allegation, and no response to calls for increased nuclear transparency. Beijing also declined a Biden-administration invitation to send P5 observers to the Nevada site as a confidence-building measure; Russia declined as well. The lack of a credible Chinese rebuttal does not make the seismic data conclusive, but it reinforces the impression that the US accusations warrant serious consideration [1].

# Analysis

## Strategic Motivation: Warhead Design and the Taiwan Contingency

[Philipp Rombach]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/philipp-rombach.html) argues that China's limited test history gives it pressing reasons to test. With only 45 tests by 1996 — against 1,054 by the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) and 715 by the Soviet Union/Russia — and comparatively rudimentary diagnostic instrumentation throughout, Beijing has less confidence than its peers in arsenal safety, security, and reliability. Underground test data would validate computer simulations, calibrate models, and potentially support new sub-kiloton tactical warhead designs, giving China additional nuclear response options vis-à-vis the United States, particularly in a [Taiwan contingency]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/taiwan-standoff.html) [1].

A yield of 200 to 400 tons would be unusually high for one-point safety testing, pointing instead toward a weapons-physics and warhead-design test. Such tests can advance warhead miniaturization, improved manoeuvrability, and survivability — consistent with China's objective of expanding its nuclear posture in the western Pacific. China's arsenal has grown from roughly 300 to 600 warheads and is projected to reach 1,500 by 2035, as Beijing positions itself as a third nuclear superpower [1].

## Cascade Risk: Erosion of Testing Norms and Proliferation Incentives

The systemic danger is that a breakdown of the testing norm cascades downward. If the major powers resume underground testing, secondary nuclear powers — India, Pakistan, North Korea — may follow, and latent proliferator states including Poland, South Korea, [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html), and even [Germany]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/germany.html) could gain new incentives to test. The norm against testing has functioned as a proliferation barrier; its erosion lowers that barrier [1].

Paradoxically, the power that would benefit most from a full resumption is China. The United States has the deepest testing record and the most advanced science-based stockpile stewardship, and therefore the most to lose; China, with its thin test base, has the most to gain. The US unilateral moratorium carries an escape clause permitting resumption should another state test — meaning the allegations against China could furnish the legal justification for Washington to resume, accelerating the very norm erosion that disadvantages it [1]. For Germany and the EU, leverage is limited but not zero: diplomatic pressure on China for transparency, support for confidence-building measures such as on-site inspections and voluntary advance notification of civilian underground explosions, an independent European assessment of the evidence, and adequate funding for the IAEA and the CTBTO's International Monitoring System remain the available instruments [1].

# Key Dynamics

- **Seismic decoupling signature**: The Makanchi station's dual-pulse signal matches a decoupled nuclear detonation followed by cavern collapse, with an estimated true yield of 200–400 tons TNT — far above conventional or subcritical levels.
- **Limited Chinese test base**: Only 45 historical tests with rudimentary instrumentation leave Beijing with stronger incentives than Washington or Moscow to obtain fresh test data.
- **Arsenal trajectory**: China's warheads have grown from ~300 to 600, projected to 1,500 by 2035, framing the alleged test as part of a broader modernization drive.
- **Asymmetric testing benefit**: China gains the most from a global resumption of testing; the US, with the deepest test record and stockpile-stewardship capability, has the most to lose.
- **Moratorium escape clause**: US law permits resuming tests if another state tests — the allegations could supply the legal trigger, accelerating norm erosion.
- **Chinese non-response**: Beijing's categorical rejection without counter-evidence or transparency reinforces the credibility of the US allegations.
