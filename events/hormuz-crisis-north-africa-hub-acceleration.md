# Background

The [US-Iran War 2026]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) and Iran's blockade of the Strait of Hormuz disrupted global energy supplies, trade routes, and commercial relations. While the world's attention fixed on the Gulf, developments in North Africa received less attention in Europe — yet the crisis accelerated existing trends across the five North African countries and deepened their geostrategic interdependence with Europe [1].

North Africa is crucial to Europe as an energy supplier, a geographically proximate trading partner, a source of skilled labour, and a tourist destination. The EU's 2025 "Pact for the Mediterranean" framed a largely transactional, security-oriented relationship focused on European border and energy security. The Hormuz crisis exposed the limitations of this framework [1].

# What Happened

The Hormuz blockade's impact rippled across North Africa with differentiated effects:

## Energy Importers Under Pressure

- **[Egypt]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/egypt.html):** Consumer price inflation reached 13.4 percent year-on-year in February 2026, with food prices rising 4.6 percent. Suez Canal revenues, already depressed by Houthi attacks, benefited from a nearly 30 percent increase in diverted oil tanker traffic — but remained below half of 2023 levels. Remittances from approximately five million Egyptian migrant workers in the Gulf are vulnerable to continued uncertainty. Rising living costs and worsening job prospects for young Egyptians increase the risk of protests and authoritarian crackdowns [1].

- **[Tunisia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/tunisia.html):** Public debt stood at just under 80 percent of GDP. Food costs increased 7.1 percent in June 2026 year-on-year and are predicted to continue rising. The economy, previously expected to grow 2.1 percent in 2026, is now projected to shrink by 0.8 percentage points — pressuring the government to depreciate the dinar and cut subsidies, raising social instability risks [1].

- **[Morocco]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/morocco.html):** In the wake of Gen Z protests in 2025, Rabat increased social spending, maintained subsidies on gas, sugar, transport, flour, and electricity, and raised the minimum wage. The Hormuz crisis made this commitment considerably more expensive: compensation spending is projected to reach approximately 1.2 percent of GDP. Headline inflation has stayed low, but price stability is being purchased rather than achieved. Despite renewable energy accounting for 17 percent of production, Morocco still relies on imported fossil fuel for 90 percent of domestic energy demand [1].

## Energy Exporters Benefiting — With Caveats

- **[Algeria]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/algeria.html):** Since Russia's invasion of Ukraine, Algeria has become the EU's second-largest natural gas supplier, providing 17.4 percent of total EU gas imports (rising to 18 percent during the crisis). The Transmed pipeline to Italy and the Medgaz pipeline to Spain have become cornerstones of European energy security. However, Algeria uses more than half its gas production domestically, and infrastructure is facing limits. New investments — including licenses awarded to ExxonMobil and Chevron — could enable future growth, though controversial shale gas extraction carries environmental and protest risks [1].

- **[Libya]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/libya.html):** Oil revenues continue to finance the two main rival political factions — the Government of National Unity in the west and the Libyan National Army in the east. Despite additional revenues from higher prices, Libya remains exposed to higher fertilizer and food costs and reduced remittances from migrant workers in the Gulf [1].

# Analysis

## North Africa as Geostrategic Hub

The Hormuz crisis is likely to accelerate North Africa's emergence as a geostrategic hub between Europe, Africa, and the Middle East. Morocco has invested heavily in this transformation: Tanger Med is the leading port in Africa; an automotive manufacturing centre includes battery factories partly in partnership with Chinese firms; and the country's phosphate reserves and fertilizer production support regional trade. China and other countries leverage Morocco's free trade agreements to access over 50 markets [1].

Egypt's Suez Canal Economic Zone (SCZONE) is undergoing a new phase of logistical expansion, including new port capacity with significant Chinese involvement — though development remains hostage to the threat of Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping [1].

The crisis has also prioritized renewable energy infrastructure. The EU's "AccelerateEU" package addresses rising energy costs and reduces reliance on oil and gas. Investment promises and partnerships for hydrogen production for energy use and fertilizer production may reinvigorate the sector and secure long-term Mediterranean cooperation on renewable energy [1].

## Beyond the Buffer-Zone Model

The Hormuz crisis has revealed that North Africa can no longer be viewed primarily as a "buffer zone" for migration control. The [Ceuta mass-crossing]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/ceuta-migration-crisis-2026-07-31.html) in July 2026 illustrated the limits of the buffer-zone approach. [Annabelle Houdret]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/annabelle-houdret.html) and [Mark Furness]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/mark-furness.html) argue that a more holistic EU–North Africa partnership is needed: one encompassing trade and logistics, mutually beneficial economic development, circular migration of skilled workers, climate change adaptation and mitigation, and socio-political dialogue [1].

The increased importance of North Africa to trade routes may also support faster implementation of cooperation in industrial production, technology transfer, joint vocational training, and research. North African countries have a renewed opportunity to diversify their industrial base and reduce reliance on the rentier economic model — supported by European investment that fosters more resilient, multifaceted economies less vulnerable to geopolitical shocks [1].

## The Regulatory and Standards Dimension

For partnerships to develop sustainably, social and environmental standards in Deep and Comprehensive Free Trade Agreements must be negotiated, and corporate due diligence obligations under the EU Supply Chain Due Diligence Directive and the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism should be implemented. These measures support decarbonization in third countries and shape deeper regional integration. Improving regulatory quality, transparency, and accountability are prerequisites for growth that benefits society as a whole, not just businesses or elites [1].
