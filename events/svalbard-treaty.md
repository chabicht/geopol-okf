# Background

Svalbard is an archipelago roughly 650 km north of Norway's North Cape, situated midway between mainland Norway and the North Pole. For centuries the islands — then known as Spitsbergen — were terra nullius, no man's land. Coal discoveries in the late nineteenth century created a practical need for legal administration: mining camps and seasonal settlements sprang up without any sovereign authority to adjudicate disputes or regulate extraction. Norway pressed for a resolution from around 1890 onward, but it was the Versailles negotiations following the First World War that finally provided the diplomatic opportunity. The resulting treaty was signed in Paris on February 9, 1920, granting Norway full and unrestricted sovereignty over the archipelago while reserving economic rights — mining, hunting, fishing, and commercial activity — to nationals of all signatory states, now numbering more than forty [1].

Article 9 of the treaty is the crux of Svalbard's security regime. It prohibits Norway from establishing naval bases or fortifications on the archipelago and from using the territory for "warlike purposes." This was not a full demilitarization in the sense of the [Åland Islands Convention]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/aland-islands-convention.html): Article 9 bars fixed military installations but does not prohibit naval patrols, military transit, or non-warlike military presence. The Norwegian Coast Guard regularly docks at Longyearbyen, and the Norwegian navy sends a frigate to the archipelago annually. The distinction between "no naval bases" and "no military presence" is central to the contemporary debate over the treaty's viability [1].

Both the Svalbard Treaty and the Åland Convention originated from the post-WWI conviction that treaties and international law could serve as alternatives to armed force — a belief that the Second World War would shatter. Yet both treaties survived that disillusionment and persisted through the Cold War, providing a legal framework for managing strategic flashpoints without militarizing them [1].

# Current Situation

Russia's invasion of Ukraine and NATO's enlargement to include [Finland]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/finland.html) and Sweden have generated serious debate about whether the Svalbard Treaty regime remains viable. Critics argue that the Article 9 restrictions are strategically obsolete in an alliance facing a revisionist Russia on its northern flank. Russia maintains a civilian settlement at Barentsburg — the only other state with a community on the archipelago — giving it a persistent foothold and a stake in the status quo that complicates any Norwegian move toward revision [1].

Svalbard's military relevance stems from its position near the GIUK-N gap and its proximity to [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html)'s Northern Fleet on the Kola Peninsula: the archipelago overlooks the routes Russian submarines must transit to reach the Atlantic. James Wither (2018) warned that Svalbard's distance from Norway, Russia's Barentsburg presence, and the absence of dispute arbitration mechanisms leave it "exposed both politically and militarily" [1][2].

# Analysis

## The Case Against Overturning the Restrictions

Østhagen (2024) leans against overturning the treaty's restrictions. Russia benefits from the status quo as the only other state with a community on Svalbard, and Norwegian law already provides tools for managing security concerns — vessel safety regulations and environmental protections under nature-reserve designations — without abrogating Article 9. Baudu recommends that NATO limit exercises near the archipelago, avoid stationing combat forces, and instead demonstrate rapid intervention capability — essentially what Norway did throughout the Cold War. Putin's Russia is dangerous but is not the Soviet Union; its military has been severely degraded by attrition in Ukraine [1].

Abrogating the treaty would carry steep costs. It would antagonize the more than forty signatory states, validate Russian narratives about Western indifference to international law, remove the legal basis for Norway's management of civilian life on the archipelago, and accelerate great power competition over Arctic resources. Norway is a medium-sized power with more to lose in an Arctic scramble than in a constrained legal regime. Norway is already strengthening its military presence on the mainland — in Trøndelag, Nordland, and Finnmark — without needing to militarize Svalbard itself [1].

## Treaty Restrictions as Inconvenience, Not Liability

The [Svalbard Treaty]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/svalbard-treaty.html) has lasted over a century. Norway coped with its restrictions throughout the Cold War against a Soviet threat far more capable than today's Russia. The restrictions pose more of an inconvenience than a strategic liability — a framing that also applies to the [Åland]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/aland-islands-convention.html) regime. For smaller states, a reputation for keeping agreements is itself a strategic asset, as elaborated in [Layered Security]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/layered-security.html) [1].

# Key Dynamics

- **Partial demilitarization**: Article 9 bars naval bases and fortifications but permits naval patrols and non-warlike military presence — a narrower constraint than Åland's full demilitarization.
- **Russian stake in the status quo**: Russia's Barentsburg community gives it an interest in preserving the treaty regime, complicating any unilateral Norwegian move toward revision.
- **Medium-power calculus**: Norway's position as a medium-sized power means it has more to lose from a legal free-for-all in the Arctic than from constrained but stable treaty obligations.
- **Mainland reinforcement alternative**: Norway can and is strengthening its military posture on the mainland without needing to militarize Svalbard.
