# Background

War on the Rocks is a defense and foreign policy analysis publication featuring commentary from practitioners, scholars, and military officers. Its positioning — at the intersection of serving and former officers, area specialists, and policy analysts — gives it a distinctive voice: technically granular, operationally literate, and oriented toward the mechanics of warfare rather than the theoretical framing of it. The bundle draws on nine WOTR contributions that cluster around five analytical lenses: the architecture of Middle East coercion and diplomacy, the Russia-Ukraine war's lessons on mass, drones, and wartime governance, the shape of the Indo-Pacific coalition and its logistical underpinning alongside China's own operational concepts for war against Taiwan, the reshaping of defense acquisition by agentic AI, and the argument that cyber governance reform must precede the creation of a separate Cyber Force. The publication also produces region-focused digests such as *The Ukraine Compass*, which curates Ukrainian-language commentary to widen Western coverage beyond the battlefield [1].

# Key Frameworks

## Middle East: Doctrine and Diplomacy

Two WOTR contributions frame the Middle East settlement from opposite ends — the coercive doctrine that creates leverage, and the diplomatic pattern that determines how leverage is converted (or not) into sovereignty-restoring agreements.

Pnina Shuker and Andrew Milburn document how [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html)'s success in the [US-Iran War of 2026]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) was not improvised but the product of a twenty-year campaign plan. The mosaic defense doctrine — formalized in 2005 by the IRGC's Center for Strategy under Mohammad Ali Jafari and ideologue Hassan Abbasi — created a decentralized command structure designed to survive decapitation strikes. Iran's theory of victory was narrow but robust: survive long enough to impose costs through maritime coercion (Hormuz), economic disruption, information dominance, and political will. The deal sequence favors Iran: before it has to resolve a single hard issue — the nuclear stockpile, the missile program, or its regional proxies — the United States lifts the naval blockade, withdraws forces, issues sanctions waivers, and makes frozen assets available. "Pay first, verify later is not a strategy. It is leverage leaving the room." The full analysis is dissolved into [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html), [US-Iran War of 2026]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html), [Iran Ceasefire MoU]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/iran-ceasefire-mou.html), and [Middle East]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html) [2].

Ahmed Saber Abbas — a contributing analyst at New Lines Institute — supplies the complementary diplomatic lens: peace treaties do more than end a phase of fighting, they redistribute leverage, sovereignty, and risk across the fronts left outside the agreement. The pattern is "front separation": closing — or attempting to close — the front that imposes the greatest immediate cost while detaching the unresolved fronts from the wider political and bargaining context that had linked them. The [Camp David Accords]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/camp-david-accords.html) addressed the Egyptian front first because [Egypt]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/egypt.html) possessed the greatest capacity to impose direct military and political costs on [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html); the Palestinian and Syrian fronts were left outside a comprehensive settlement. The same pattern is reasserting itself in the negotiations around the [Iran Ceasefire MoU]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/iran-ceasefire-mou.html): [Lebanon]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/lebanon.html) is being detached from the Iran track and recast as a security problem, with Israeli withdrawal made conditional on Hizballah's disarmament rather than governed by a defined timetable. The danger is that peace becomes fragile when it closes the front that imposes the greatest immediate cost while leaving sovereignty unresolved on the others — the agreement preserves the grievances, political vacuum, and unequal security conditions through which future resistance can grow. The full analysis is dissolved into [Lebanon]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/lebanon.html), [Camp David Accords]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/camp-david-accords.html), [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html), [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html), [Egypt]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/egypt.html), and [Middle East]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html) [6].

Read together, the two frameworks describe a single structural problem: Iran's mosaic defense manufactures the coercion that makes front separation attractive to the stronger party, and front separation in turn preserves the unresolved sovereignty through which Iran's leverage renews itself.

## The Russia-Ukraine War: Mass, Drones, and Wartime Governance

Two WOTR contributions address the Russia-Ukraine war's central paradox from opposite sides: why Russia's mass answer failed, and how Ukraine's techno-reformist answer is being politically undone.

Greg Whisler and Michael Kofman provide the most detailed account of why [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html)'s wartime force expansion failed to deliver battlefield success despite sustaining a manpower advantage through most of the [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html). The most significant Russian military expansion in decades grew the deployed force to over 700,000 personnel, yet as Russia fielded more forces, the value of that mass steadily diminished: cycles of technological innovation — particularly Ukrainian investment in [drones]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/drone-warfare.html) — led to greater dispersal, negating advantages from manpower superiority, while the Russian military chose a cost-ineffective approach to offensives. The first large-scale extended war Russia has fought using primarily contracted volunteers challenges prior assumptions about mobilization. The full analysis is dissolved into [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html), [Drone Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/drone-warfare.html), and [Defense Industrial Resilience]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/defense-industrial-resilience.html) [3].

The Ukraine Compass special edition documents the other side of the paradox: the dismissal of Ukrainian Defense Minister Mykhailo Fedorov in July 2026 and the protests it sparked in Kyiv. The analysis identifies three elements central to Ukrainian political life since 2022 — trust (which partners, officials, and media can be trusted), technology (Fedorov's doctrine of rapid tech iteration over mass mobilization, making drone warfare Ukraine's most lethal capability), and turnover (the fifth Defense Minister since the full-scale invasion, in a system where appointment and dismissal run on loyalty rather than parliamentary process). The divide between Kyiv's streets and the frontline is real: a democracy at war cannot survive without public trust and a hopeful way forward, and an army at war cannot survive without soldiers who have learned not to wait for a savior. The full analysis is dissolved into [Fedorov Dismissal (July 2026)]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/fedorov-dismissal-2026.html), [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html), [Zelensky]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/zelensky.html), [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html), [Drone Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/drone-warfare.html), and [NATO Ankara Summit 2026]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/nato-ankara-summit-2026.html) [7].

Whisler/Kofman explain why Russia's mass failed against Ukraine's drone-innovation answer; the Ukraine Compass explains how that same techno-reformist answer is being rolled back by a trust and turnover dynamic that rewards loyalty over capability. The war's military logic favors Ukraine's approach; its political logic may undo the very reforms that produced the advantage.

## Indo-Pacific: Alignment and Sustainment

Two WOTR contributions address the Indo-Pacific coalition from its strategic premise and its operational geography.

War on the Rocks argues that [India]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/india.html) will stick with the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) despite Trump-era friction because [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) is the overriding strategic concern driving the alignment. The analysis is dissolved into the [Indo-Pacific]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/indo-pacific.html) region concept [4].

Dan Justice — a U.S. Navy foreign area officer serving as a federal executive fellow at the RAND Corporation — maps the logistical geography that this alignment presupposes. As the Navy shifts from a decade of developing unmanned systems to fielding them in the operational fleet, it lacks a framework for fixing these platforms overseas. Medium and large unmanned surface and undersea vehicles are subject to the same unforgiving maintenance realities as manned warships (oil filters, O-rings, corrosion control, sensor calibration) but carry no crew to perform on-station maintenance or casualty control. The paradox of access: shallower drafts and smaller footprints allow unmanned vehicles to use port locations physically inaccessible to large warships (HMAS Cairns, Lombrum), and uncrewed diesel-electric submarines can bypass restrictions limiting manned nuclear submarines. But uncrewed hulls lack a worldwide settled legal status as warships, requiring individual host-nation agreements for transiting territorial waters — archipelagic states in the Pacific may deny transit, and compulsory pilotage may be enforced. The Navy should build a distributed network of smaller, austere ports (Australia, PNG, Philippines, Vietnam, Indonesia) rather than concentrating repair capability at large, static, vulnerable bases like Guam or Yokosuka. The organizational choice is between organic expeditionary repair squadrons and a contractor-led "lead sustainment integrator" model — a blended approach may prove optimal. The full analysis is dissolved into [Indo-Pacific]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/indo-pacific.html), [Drone Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/drone-warfare.html), [Defense Industrial Resilience]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/defense-industrial-resilience.html), and the relevant country concepts [5].

Both arguments take the same coalition as given and identify China as the organizing pressure: the strategic argument explains why the alignment holds, the sustainment argument explains where the alignment's hardware actually lives and how it would be repaired in conflict.

Howard Wang and Nathan Beauchamp-Mustafaga — both RAND Corporation analysts — open a different window onto the Indo-Pacific: not the coalition's cohesion or logistics, but [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html)'s own operational thinking about how a war over [Taiwan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/taiwan.html) would be fought. Writing about a March 2025 article in *Military Art*, the CMC-overseen internal journal, they describe the concept of "all-domain megacity incapacitation warfare" — a proposed campaign to rapidly subdue Taipei through three integrated lines of effort: military blockade, precision kinetic strike, and cognitive attack. The objective is incapacitation rather than annihilation: breaking the Taiwan people's will to fight rather than physically destroying their forces. Cognitive effects are treated as co-equal with kinetic operations, not as a supporting element — a shift from traditional "systems confrontation" (paralyzing information nodes) to cognitively engineering adversary submission. The concept's authors, including researchers from the Academy of Military Science and the People's Armed Police Command College, frame the entire city of Taipei as a system to target, dramatically expanding the list of legitimate targets to include civilian communications channels from the outset of conflict. Non-military capabilities — media, financial systems, information operations — are expected to deliver cognitive effects under a national-level coordination system, operationalizing China's broader strategy of "national total war": whole-of-nation mobilization to build civilian capabilities in peacetime that can be weaponized in conflict. Wang and Beauchamp-Mustafaga trace this strategy from its roots in comprehensive national power theory, through systems confrontation, to its expansion following the COVID-19 "people's war," and note that it is now embedded in Xi Jinping's Strong Military Thought and the military strategic guideline. China's analysts draw lessons from Ukraine, where they interpret U.S. intelligence declassification and strategic messaging as a "cognitive smoke bomb" that induced Russian underestimation — though they acknowledge China lacks the alliance networks and financial chokepoints that enabled the Western response. The authors identify a critical gap in U.S. wargaming: most models focus on kinetic exchanges or treat will to fight as a static input, rarely adjudicating cognitive effects on military, civilian, and political leadership. They urge incorporating Taiwan's All-Out Defense Mobilization Agency and societal resilience indicators into routine wargames rather than treating them as special cases. The 15th Five-Year Plan's prioritization of strategic capabilities offers a benchmark for tracking whether this concept matures from proposal to doctrine. The full analysis is dissolved into [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html), [Taiwan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/taiwan.html), [Taiwan Standoff]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/taiwan-standoff.html), and [Cognitive Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/cognitive-warfare.html) [9].

## Defense Technology and Acquisition

Jacob Green — a U.S. Army armor and acquisition officer serving as executive officer in the Office of the Assistant Secretary of the Army for Acquisition, Logistics and Technology — argues that agentic AI has fundamentally shifted the Make-or-Buy line for DoD software. The secretary of defense's commercial-first acquisition policy was sound when the alternative was long development lead times, but the exponential growth of large language models and agentic tooling has changed the calculus: the department should buy infrastructure, agentic tools, and model access commercially, then build its applications in-house with full government ownership at a fraction of current cost. Green's own pilot experience is illustrative — he built a 60,000-page retrieval system wrapped in a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server over two weekends for approximately $1,700 in token costs, a prototype that COCOMO estimates would have cost $840,000–$1.2 million through a traditional small business award. The architecture he envisions rests on several pillars: AI gateway services for model-agnostic procurement (hedging against rapid capability shifts among competing models), MCP as an open standard enabling a Data Mesh where data lives in authoritative domain systems and is retrieved via agent-to-agent calls rather than costly system-to-system integrations, pre-accredited development environments with streamlined onboarding to compress the 6-to-18-month accreditation wait, and internal competition among the department's existing software organizations (Army Software Factory, Science and Technology Centers) via reimbursable models and cross-service work bidding. Green contends that the $178 billion in Q1 2026 venture funding for AI model companies — over three times the entire DoD FY2027 AI and CJADC2 budget — means the department cannot match industry on model development and should instead harness commercial capability while retaining control of applications. He also urges engagement with open source tools (Gemma, Hermes Agent, Open Code) treated with the same scrutiny as any software, and warns against closed-system platforms and glorified chatbots masquerading as agentic capabilities. The full analysis is dissolved into [Defense Industrial Resilience]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/defense-industrial-resilience.html), [AI Military Race]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/ai-military-race.html), and [Revolution in Military Affairs]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/revolution-in-military-affairs.html) [8].

## Cyber Governance and Force Design

Justin Hardy — a career U.S. Navy submarine officer who commanded the ballistic missile submarine USS Alaska and served as acting deputy director of operations at U.S. Cyber Command — argues that the growing chorus for a separate Cyber Force has the debate's priorities backward: before the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) picks out uniforms, it must answer who will govern cyberspace. The error at the center of the conversation is the conflation of force-generation problems — how to man, train, and equip cyber forces — with the deeper issue of governance: who owns the budget, sets policy, provides civilian oversight, and stands before Congress to account for the warfighting domain. The Department of Defense has spent decades making cyberspace into both a commodity and something so special that it must be treated differently from all other warfare domains: cyber warrants its own combatant command and a seat at the warfighter's table, yet in the same breath it is treated like enterprise information technology to be governed as a utility. Through accretion rather than design, cyber terrain is scattered across the Services, resourcing is ad hoc, and accountability remains perpetually undefined. No Cyber Force, no matter how well designed, resolves the governance question alone. The full analysis is dissolved into [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) and [Revolution in Military Affairs]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/revolution-in-military-affairs.html) [10].

Hardy draws on his submarine background to make the structural argument through the example of Admiral Hyman Rickover, founder of the Nuclear Navy. Rickover knew that responsibility cannot be diluted — "you must be able to poke the man in the chest when it fails." That culture of accountability was not conjured by force of personality; it was possible because the nuclear propulsion program had a single owner with genuine authority over the entire enterprise — reactor plants, training pipeline, industrial base, and funding. The structure made the culture enforceable. Cyber, by contrast, is an enterprise where everyone owns a piece and no one owns the whole, and Hardy found himself preaching Rickover's culture of accountability in a field whose structure gave it nothing to attach to. The cultural aversion to direct tasking he observed at Cyber Command — routine orders circulated for comment, wording negotiated, consensus required before execution — reflected a command that was primarily a coordinator among organizations with misaligned motivations, not an owner with directive authority. The Rickover model demonstrates that accountability flows from structure, not the reverse: without a single owner with genuine authority, no culture of accountability can take hold [10].

Congress, Hardy observes, has already begun addressing the governance question — and both chambers opted for governance over a new service. The House Armed Services Committee's cyber subcommittee mark includes Section 1502, directing the Secretary of Defense to establish clear accountability for the cybersecurity of DoD information networks, including identification of "one official designated as the single accountable official." The Senate went further, proposing a new Undersecretary of Defense for Cyber, Information, and Networks — dual-hatted as the department's Chief Information Officer and Principal Cyber Advisor — fusing the enterprise network architecture and requirements role with the operational advisory role in a single position. That consolidation closes a real seam between what constitutes cyber operations and what keeps cyber running in the background, placing both priorities in the hands of one person who answers for the balance. But Hardy warns that an undersecretary who only advises and coordinates, without controlling funding, would be a more senior version of today's undefined lanes: genuine accountability requires directive authority over the domain's terrain and real ownership of the force-generation and systems dollars the Services primarily control. Congress should designate someone who controls the money, sets the requirements, and compels the Services to resource the field — otherwise they will have picked the person to poke without giving them the authority to prevent failure [10].

The historical record, Hardy argues, points in the same direction. Before creating the Air Force, Congress first rebuilt the governance of the entire defense establishment through the National Security Act of 1947 — creating the Secretary of Defense, the Department of the Air Force, and a new structure of civilian oversight — and only then established the Air Force as a service within it. The initial decision was governance; the service followed. The Nunn-Cohen reforms that created Special Operations Command followed the same integrated pattern: Congress assembled the command, civilian oversight, authorities, and resources as a single design, giving the special operations enterprise its own service-like funding line (Major Force Program 11) that converted responsibility from an org-chart abstraction into something a person could answer for. Cyber has accumulated analogous components, but piecemeal over nearly two decades rather than by deliberate design. Even the Cyber Force advocates' own commission — the most rigorous implementation study yet — could not resolve the question of where a Cyber Force should be institutionally housed within the Department of Defense, and its proposed force would not absorb the Services' individual networks, leaving cyberspace's terrain fragmented at the network layer where most operational friction actually lives. The proper sequence, Hardy contends, is to answer the governance question first — name the single accountable official and arm the office with budget and directive authority — then fix the governance layer, and only then take up the service question inside a structure actually built to receive a new service. A service commissioned before the governance fight is settled inherits the fragmentation as a birth defect, institutionalizing it beside its brand-new flag [10].
