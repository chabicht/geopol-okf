# Background

The Kiel Institute for the World Economy (Kiel Institut) is a research institute focusing on global economic policy, international trade, and macroeconomic analysis. Its June 2026 report "Endgame: The State of the Russian Economy" provides a comprehensive assessment of [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html)'s wartime economic trajectory, produced by a distinguished group of economists. The report's quantitative rigor and institutional credibility make it a key reference for analysts tracking the sustainability of Russia's war effort [1].

# Key Frameworks

## Russia's Unsustainable Macro Stance

The Kiel report concludes that Russia's current macro stance is "not sustainable." High interest rates are stifling the economy while loose fiscal policy and quasi-fiscal operations prop up the defense sector. The Q1 2026 budget deficit exceeded the full-year target in just three months. The choice is between fiscal consolidation or monetary accommodation resulting in even higher inflation. Export revenues from the sale of raw materials remain the decisive variable: with fiscal buffers spent, Russia's war capacity is more than ever directly coupled to hydrocarbon export income [1].

## Fiscal Buffers Exhausted

[Putin]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/putin.html) has thrown away the entirety of Russia's once large sovereign wealth fund. The liquid portion has shrunk from 6.5% of GDP at the start of the war to 1.8% in April 2026. Russia is essentially living hand-to-mouth, with all buffers spent and the war effort funded entirely through energy sales. This creates a structural vulnerability: any disruption to hydrocarbon export revenue — whether from [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html)'s long-range strike campaign or from sanctions enforcement — directly translates into reduced war capacity [1][4].

## European Opportunity

The report argues that Russia's economic fragility creates an opportunity for Ukraine's European partners. Because Russia's war capacity is now directly coupled to hydrocarbon income, strict enforcement of sanctions on Russia's shadow fleet and secondary sanctions on facilitating banks in [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html), Hong Kong, and other countries could push the Russian economy toward exhaustion. The Kiel report frames this as a time-constrained window: the moment is ripe for a pressure campaign [1][4].

## KITE Model and Bottleneck Extension Methodology

The Kiel Institute Trade policy Evaluation (KITE) model is a quantitative general equilibrium trade model extended with a "bottleneck" mechanism that captures asymmetric cost transmission through critical inputs where substitution is severely limited. The bottleneck extension introduces a critical-input layer distinguishing ordinary intermediates from bottleneck inputs (e.g., methane as feedstock for the Haber-Bosch ammonia/urea process). When critical inputs become more expensive, a one-sided penalty applies; when they become cheaper, no symmetric windfall is generated. This asymmetry captures the real-world observation that bottleneck costs bite hard on the upside but do not produce symmetric gains on the downside. The extension breaks the Cobb-Douglas symmetry that standard trade models assume, showing that those models systematically underestimate the welfare cost of chokepoint closures because they miss the cascade from energy → chemicals/fertilizers → food. Short-run scenarios use elasticities at one-quarter of long-run values, reflecting severely limited substitution in the immediate aftermath of a disruption [2].

## Hormuz Closure Economic Damage Simulation

KPB 206 (March 2026) simulates the economic consequences of a full closure of the Strait of Hormuz under counterfactual conditions that, by the time of publication, had already been triggered. The simulation's central distributional finding: the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) loses just −0.07% of welfare under the short-run full-closure scenario, while countries in South Asia and Africa face losses 10–20 times larger (Zambia −5.49%, Sri Lanka −3.47%, India −1.78%, Pakistan −1.83%). The price cascade runs from crude oil (+11.94%) through chemicals (+5.75%) to food (wheat +4.24%, vegetables/fruit +5.21%), with Zambia facing food prices up 30.7%. The bottleneck mechanism anchors the cascade from energy through fertilizers to food regardless of the precise oil price level. The brief also documents that only Chinese-flagged tankers carrying yuan-denominated cargo were permitted sporadic passage — direct evidence of bifurcation operating in real time. An eight-state upper bound (blocking Saudi and Oman exports as well) roughly doubles the effects. The model explicitly excludes speculative and financial market dynamics, hoarding, SPR releases, and commodity futures pricing — the results represent the structural cost of rerouting supply chains, not the spot-price spike. Military analysts' assessment that just two well-placed attacks would suffice to halt commercial transit makes a forced reopening effectively impossible without a sustained large-scale military operation [2].

## Proportional Benchmark Methodology and Third-Market Decomposition

KPB 213 (June 2026) introduces a "proportional benchmark" that measures what market-share loss each advanced economy should experience purely as a mechanical consequence of [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html)'s growing share of a third market, assuming all other competitive forces remain unchanged. Actual market-share changes are then compared to the benchmark and decomposed into (i) the share mechanically attributable to China's rise, and (ii) an "excess loss" share reflecting domestic or European competitiveness deterioration relative to other non-Chinese exporters. The EU+7 bloc definition (all EU member states plus Norway, Switzerland, the UK, the US, Canada, Japan, and South Korea) serves as the implicit advanced-economy bloc. The method is deliberately descriptive — it stops short of causal claims, framing the decomposition as a statistical attribution rather than a structural estimate. The cross-country dispersion is the brief's most original empirical finding: excess-loss rates range from 89.6% (UK) to 41.7% (Italy), with Germany at 80.2% [3].

## China Third-Market Displacement Analysis

The proportional benchmark applied to BACI/CEPII trade data over a ten-year window (2012–2014 vs 2022–2024) yields the brief's central findings. China's goods exports grew more than seven-fold, from $471 billion in 2002 to $3.5 trillion in 2024, lifting its world-export share from 7.6% to 16% and broadening from ~1,800 to ~2,500 product categories. China's export basket has shifted decisively toward capital and intermediate goods with rising product complexity, while advanced economies' baskets have remained largely unchanged. For [Germany]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/germany.html), only about 31% of its total 1.98pp third-market-share loss is mechanically attributable to China's rise; 69% (1.36pp) is overproportional — reflecting home-grown competitiveness deterioration. The sectoral decomposition reveals that pharma and aircraft losses are nearly pure excess (4–8% China-attributable), while iron articles and steel are nearly pure China displacement (92–97%). The brief explicitly rejects blanket tariffs as a defensible response — Chinese cost advantages in lithium-ion batteries and solar modules are now so large that any leveling tariff would have to be implausibly high — and prescribes targeted, time-limited trade defence conditional on investment commitments, deeper European integration, and sustained innovation investment. For Germany, high energy costs are identified as a prime suspect in the home-grown excess loss [3].
