# Background

Julian Hinz is an economist affiliated with Bielefeld University and the Kiel Institute for the World Economy. He is the lead developer of the KITE (Kiel Institute Trade policy Evaluation) quantitative general equilibrium trade model and its bottleneck extension. His research bridges trade theory and computational economics, with applications to chokepoint disruptions, food security cascades, and third-market competition. He is a recurring contributor in the bundle, co-authoring both Kiel Policy Brief No. 206 on the Hormuz closure's economic consequences and Kiel Report No. 213 on China's displacement of advanced-economy exporters in third markets. See [Kiel Institute](kiel-institute.html) for the institutional context.

# Key Frameworks

## KITE Model and Bottleneck Extension

The KITE model is a quantitative general equilibrium trade framework that simulates the welfare and price effects of trade disruptions. Hinz's bottleneck extension introduces a critical-input layer that distinguishes between ordinary intermediates and bottleneck inputs — such as methane as feedstock for the Haber-Bosch ammonia process. The key innovation is a one-sided penalty: when critical inputs become more expensive, the cost bites hard, but when they become cheaper, there is no symmetric windfall. This asymmetry captures the real-world observation that chokepoint closures impose costs that standard Cobb-Douglas trade models systematically underestimate. The extension is calibrated with short-run elasticities set to one-quarter of long-run values, reflecting severely limited substitution possibilities in the immediate aftermath of a disruption [1].

## Third-Market Decomposition Methodology

In KPB 213, Hinz and co-authors construct a "proportional benchmark" that measures the market-share loss each advanced economy should experience purely as a mechanical consequence of China's growing share of a third market. Actual losses are then compared to this benchmark, decomposing each country's losses into the share mechanically attributable to China's rise and an "excess loss" share reflecting domestic competitiveness deterioration. The finding that only 31% of Germany's third-market-share loss is China-attributable — with 69% being overproportional — is a direct output of this methodology [2].
