# Background

Sarah Mock writes on Substack about agrarian economics, scaling critique, and the structural economics of American farming. Her work combines farm-level economic analysis with systemic critique of the corporate sustainability movement. She brings an insider's perspective on agricultural economics — visiting real farms, analyzing cost structures, and challenging the gap between regenerative agriculture rhetoric and outcomes.

# Key Frameworks

## The Scaling Paradox

Regenerative agriculture is structurally incompatible with economies of scale because genuine regen outcomes require "knowing your context" — observation-driven, farm-specific, de-standardized management that runs directly counter to the cost-spreading logic that makes 5,000-cow dairies and 10,000-acre row-crop operations profitable. If a regen-ag program is scalable (standardized across a vast landscape), it is by definition not actually regenerative. [1]

## Practices ≠ Outcomes

A farm may adopt every nominal regen practice (cover crops, no-till, manure application) while still producing lifeless, compacted soil. Mock documented a 5,000-cow "regenerative" dairy standing on "compacted, parched, beige dust actively blowing away" — performing the practices without achieving the outcomes because "know your context" was skipped. [1]

## The Earl Butz Inheritance

U.S. commercial-scale agriculture remains structured by Earl Butz's 1970s "get big or get out" doctrine — farmers compete by spreading capital costs over more units, buying larger equipment, and driving yields up to depress per-unit cost. This structural imperative is precisely what regen ag breaks, making the transition economically impossible for mega-operations. [1]

## Capital Lock-In at Mega-Scale

The 5,000-cow mega-dairy is structurally untransitional. The centralized milking parlor is the binding capital constraint — its cost can only be recouped by running at full capacity with 5,000 confined cows on a precision ration. A pasture shift collapses both milk output and cost recovery. Diversification also loses scale economics: planting oats on 250 acres alongside corn on 250 acres raises per-acre cost from $50 to $70 because a specialized oat harvester is no longer spread across a single enterprise. [1]

## Farm-Splitting as Transition Path

The path forward is not convincing big operations to adopt regen practices, but structurally breaking large farms into smaller ones — more farmers, more decentralized milking parlors, more land-attention density. "The most valuable input on a farm is the farmer's shadow." [1]

## Corporate Greenwashing

Major agrifood companies (Nestlé, Pepsico, ADM, Danone, Oatly, SAI Platform) are scaling standardized regen practices — the very thing Mock identifies as non-regenerative. No enforceable definition of "regenerative agriculture" exists, and corporate stakeholders exploit the ambiguity to claim credit for practices that don't produce outcomes. [1]
