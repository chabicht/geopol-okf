# Background

Robert Pape is a tenured academic who has modeled the bombing of Iran for over 20 years [8]. He compiled the first complete database of all suicide attacks after 9/11 and applies 25 years of research on suicide terrorism to contemporary conflicts [8]. His work is grounded in his *Bombing to Win* thesis — argued "for decades" — that airpower can destroy targets but rarely compels determined regimes to abandon core political objectives, a finding drawn from years teaching the strategy of coercive air campaigns to U.S. Air Force officers [14][17][19][25].

Pape's analytical approach centers on leverage dynamics: how the distribution of coercive advantage shifts during a war and how those shifts determine whether a conflict moves toward termination or new escalation. The foundational logic is compact — "States gaining leverage rarely hurry to end wars" [8] — and from it he built the three-stage Escalation Trap model, tracking the progression from tactically successful initial bombing, through the target's horizontal counter-escalation, to a ground-war stage in which the attacker becomes strategically trapped [8]. His coercion framework distinguishes tactical military success from strategic political success, a distinction he applies to chokepoint control, civilian-infrastructure targeting, and the gap between destroying launchers and restoring commercial confidence [16][18].

Applied to the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html), Pape's framework reframes the Strait of Hormuz from a transit corridor into a lever that [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) controls — "Hormuz remains open because Iran permits it to remain open" [2] — and tracks the war through discrete escalation thresholds, a spiral-phase descriptor, a cost-distance strategy, and the concept of oscillation between tactical pauses and strategic off-ramps [21][23]. He frames the conflict as a closed choice — either [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) capitulates and accepts Iran as the rising regional hegemon, or he escalates — with two domestic-pressure engines driving both sides toward deeper commitment regardless of any single decision-maker's preferences: "It takes two to taco" [7][8]. The economic clock and the military clock run in the same direction, binding the war's duration to the interaction of Iran's accumulating leverage and America's political calendar [8].

# Key Frameworks

## The Escalation Trap: From Foundational Theory to Exit Condition

The arc of Pape's analytical project runs from a single aphorism about leverage and war termination to a formal escape condition for the trap it names. What begins as a general theory of why states gaining leverage rarely hurry to end wars evolves, over four and a half months of war, into a staged model tracking discrete thresholds, a spiral-phase descriptor, a concept of oscillation, and finally a falsifiable criterion for exit. Each analytical refinement arrives when the prior concept can no longer account for what is happening on the ground — the framework's maturation is itself a trajectory, and the war's deepening is the axis along which it moves.

### Leverage and War Termination

The foundational logic is compact. "States gaining leverage rarely hurry to end wars." This aphorism, articulated in Pape's June 15 analysis, supplies the engine for everything that follows: if leverage disincentivizes settlement, then a state acquiring leverage will continue to fight, and the war will follow the logic of that accumulating leverage rather than the logic of diplomatic pressure. The corollary is structural — the side losing leverage faces a narrowing window in which its bargaining position deteriorates while its opponent's improves, and the passage of time itself becomes a strategic variable.

### The Three-Stage Model

Pape has modeled the bombing of Iran for over 20 years, and he defined the war's likely trajectory in several stages, beginning when the initial bombing is tactically successful [8]. Stage 1 is exactly that: the initial bombing is tactically successful — bombs hit targets, kill leaders, destroy missile launchers — but the target can lash back [8]. This is what happened in the opening phase of the war.

Stage 2 is the target lashing back — horizontal escalation, in which Iran counter-escalates and the conflict broadens beyond the initial strike's footprint [8]. Tactical success against a capable opponent does not produce strategic resolution; it produces a counter-move, and the war enters the trap.

Stage 3 is the ground war — the stage Pape argues the United States is now approaching. He frames it as a closed choice: either [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) capitulates and accepts Iran as the rising regional hegemon — likely the fourth center of world power within a year or two — or he escalates. "Everybody wants there to be a third choice. That doesn't make a third choice" [7][8].

The trap that gives the framework its name occurs when the attacker in the initial stages is tactically successful but becomes strategically trapped [8]. Pape assesses that this Escalation Trap framework has really been the most accurate of anybody, he thinks, over the last four and a half months [8]. To operationalize the model into trackable signals, Pape identifies three indicators he is watching over the coming weeks: first, the amplification of Iran's revenge campaign; second, the expansion of US bombing that targets deeper in Iran; and finally, media reports of reconnaissance by US Special Forces [8]. These indicators bridge the staged model and the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html)'s observed trajectory — they are the mechanism by which the framework tracks the transition from one stage to the next.

### Trajectory Forecast: Posture Toward Stage 3 and War Duration

The first major application of the framework is a trajectory forecast: where is the war headed, and on what timeline? Pape assesses that the US military is already pre-positioning and preparing the battlefield for Stage 3. According to information from CENTCOM, the US has repositioned its carriers and Marines into the Gulf of Oman — a significant shift from their previous position a thousand miles away in the Indian Ocean, which kept them out of drone and missile range. Each of those carriers has 5,000 to 6,000 men and women, now in range and vulnerable to a much greater extent. The bombing campaign is now classic suppression of enemy defenses along the coast [8].

Pape forecasts August 2026 as the decisive month of the war. The [SPR depletion crisis]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/spr-depletion-crisis.html) is on course to approach its congressionally mandated operational floor of roughly 243 million barrels by the middle or latter part of August, precisely as hurricane season begins threatening Gulf Coast production, while the [Supreme Leader's funeral]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/supreme-leader-funeral-2026.html) has stiffened Iranian resolve through massive nationalist mobilization [5]. By August, Iran is likely to possess three forms of leverage simultaneously: growing nationalist confidence at home, continuing influence over maritime traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, and an opponent with steadily diminishing economic room for more disruption [5].

The war aim is itself expanding. Massive nationalist mobilization makes a more ambitious objective increasingly plausible: using the political momentum created by the war to weaken — and ultimately end — the permanent American military presence on Iran's southern frontier. Tehran can impose the highest economic costs on states that continue supporting the American military presence while offering lower transit fees, more predictable shipping access, and greater commercial stability to governments that reduce or eventually eliminate US bases. Iran has not yet explicitly applied this principle to US basing rights, but the strategic logic points in that direction [5].

Two separate engines now drive the escalation [7][8]. Both governments are becoming politically invested in objectives that cannot both be achieved at the same time — every successful strike and every successful retaliation creates new domestic pressures to continue escalating [7]. The critical insight is that even if one of these engines slows, the other can drive escalation for both. "It takes two to taco" [7][8]. The interaction gives the war its own momentum, independent of any single decision-maker's preferences.

Pape believes the war will continue at least through January 2027 — the idea that it would be over by July 1 was "wishful thinking," and the balance of power is still shifting [8]. Americans may stop the war when gasoline prices reach $6 to $8 a gallon, but that has not yet happened [8]. The [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) thus enters a phase where the economic clock and the military clock run in the same direction.

### Threshold Tracking: Vertical, Horizontal, and Civilian-Infrastructure Mechanics

As the war deepens, the framework's mechanics become granular. Pape tracks discrete escalation thresholds — each one a point where the political cost of restraint rises while the space for diplomacy shrinks.

The casualty threshold (July 15). Iran's use of heavy anti-ship missiles with up to 1,000-pound warheads against tankers in the Strait of Hormuz marks the shift from controlled brinkmanship to high-risk, uncontrolled escalation. The weapons are capable of catastrophic, ship-sinking destruction — far eclipsing minor drone strikes. Numerous US Navy ships are dangerously repositioned close to Iran's coast, well within Iran's anti-ship cruise missile range. Even 30–40 US casualties would instantly shatter diplomatic options, forcing an uncontrollable, public-fueled demand for payback that could lock both sides into a costly spiral and could make Stage Three politically difficult to avoid. Neither Washington nor Tehran fully controls what happens once those casualties occur [11].

Horizontal escalation to a second front (July 16). The Houthi barrage of ballistic missiles and drones targeting Saudi Arabia's Abha International Airport — in direct retaliation for airstrikes on Sanaa — heavily risks horizontal escalation that would widen the Iran war by opening a second maritime front at the strategic Bab el-Mandeb gateway. Until then the war had centered on the Strait of Hormuz; a second front at the [Red Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/red-sea.html) transforms the crisis from a regional confrontation into a broader contest over the security of global energy flows. The move expands the political stakes for every major actor while shrinking their room to compromise — leaving two strategic chokepoints to remain under threat would appear politically unacceptable. Iran gains additional leverage without directly committing its own forces [12].

Shaping operations for ground assault (July 17). US Central Command's multiple waves of airstrikes against key bridges, railway junctures, and power lines in Hormozgan province and around Bandar Abbas are classic "shaping" operations in preparation for a ground assault, consistent with the air strikes over all six days in the previous week. Once the United States begins shaping the battlefield for possible ground operations, stopping becomes politically harder than continuing — every additional strike raises the political price of restraint while making a larger military commitment appear increasingly necessary to justify what has already been done. Iran's trap deepens in parallel: standing down after these strikes would risk signaling weakness both domestically and across the region [13].

Civilian-infrastructure targeting (July 18). Iran's direct targeting of a power and water desalination plant in [Kuwait]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/kuwait.html) — part of the system producing roughly 90% of the country's drinking water — crosses another escalation threshold: civilian survival infrastructure is becoming a direct battlefield target. Drawing on his Bombing to Win research, Pape's central finding is that when civilians suddenly lose access to life's basic necessities, survival rapidly overwhelms every other concern — "anger comes after survival." But the Escalation Trap operates in the opposite direction from civilian morale: leaders confronted with collapsing civilian conditions often seek to shift public anger outward, while neighboring states face growing pressure to respond to humanitarian and security spillovers. The result could be a wider regional war whose consequences become increasingly difficult for any side to control. The strike proves the concept for a vulnerability pattern that extends across the Gulf: Qatar and Bahrain each rely on only three to five desalination plants, while Saudi Arabia and the UAE rely on 30 and 70 respectively [14]. The [geoeconomics of water]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/geoeconomics-of-water.html) thus enters the framework as a new threshold category — one where the military instrument and civilian survival converge.

### The Spiral Phase and Cost-Distance Strategy

By late July, the framework undergoes its first major conceptual extension. Pape's Breaking Analysis #8 (July 29, 2026) introduces two linked concepts — the "spiral phase" descriptor and the cost-distance strategy — that reframe what the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) is now about. Iran's strategic objective has shifted from destroying American military power to making that power progressively more expensive, politically difficult, and operationally distant. Every additional mile imposed on US aircraft increases sortie times, aerial refueling requirements, logistics burdens, and operational costs while reducing the tempo at which American air power can be generated. Repeated attacks against facilities supporting US operations also raise pressure on regional governments that host American forces, forcing them to weigh the benefits of the US security relationship against growing domestic and military risks [21].

The conflict has entered the spiral phase: no longer characterized primarily by isolated retaliation, it is becoming a geographically interconnected contest in which military strikes, proxy operations, maritime disruption, and political signaling reinforce one another across multiple theaters. [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) is no longer firing on US forces only to retaliate for American strikes on its territory — it is willing to take the fight to the US on its own terms. Recent US-Saudi strikes on Iran-backed militias in Iraq, continued pressure on Red Sea shipping, and the renewed exchange of missile attacks all point toward the same dynamic [21].

The most consequential shift is the target class. Iran is contesting not merely American forces, but the entire regional system through which the United States projects military power. The July 29 strike on [Jordan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/jordan.html)'s Muwaffaq Salti Air Base — a CENTCOM center housing nearly 4,000 US personnel, supporting advanced fighters, drones, intelligence assets, and air-defense systems — illustrates the point. As the Pentagon has dispersed US assets away from vulnerable Gulf facilities (Al Udeid, Bahrain) to Jordan, Jordan has become a pillar of the dispersal architecture; the same architecture that justifies its strategic value has now made it a target. The IRGC's rare public attribution — explicitly stating that its Aerospace Force targeted a US military Central Command center inside Jordan — confirms the framework's reading [21].

The strike validates the framework's predictive power. Readers of the Escalation Trap could see this coming weeks ahead, as these events are exactly what the June 15 analysis expected would occur as Iran moved into its period of maximum leverage with oil inventories running to tank bottoms and on the expected timeline. The attempted missile strike on Jordan fits that logic [21] — the timing and target family match the forecast, validating the pre-war framework on a specific, dated prediction about the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) and [Jordan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/jordan.html)'s emerging role in it.

### Oscillation and the Ground Power Dilemma

The next conceptual extension arrives on August 3. Pape's Breaking Analysis #10 introduces the concept of oscillation to distinguish tactical pauses from strategic off-ramps. When [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) abruptly canceled planned American military strikes against Iran after claiming Middle Eastern allies had reached parameters for a peace agreement — a claim Iranian military officials dismissed and denied — the structural indicators continued to deteriorate: commercial tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz had slowed to a near-halt, with an estimated 80% drop in volume and only one inbound large crude carrier arriving over the past week. The pause is not de-escalation but the signature of a Spiral Phase in which political leaders search for alternatives because the available military options have become increasingly costly while the underlying strategic problem remains unresolved [23].

The ground power dilemma crystallizes the failure of airpower on three measures. Airpower has not restored secure commercial transit through Hormuz. It has not broken Iran's regional leverage. And it has not produced a political settlement. That creates an increasingly difficult ground power dilemma for Washington [23] — one that bears on the entire [Middle East]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html) posture and on [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html)'s rising regional position.

Pape identifies a strategy of maximum leverage now being explicitly exploited by Iran. The June 15 forecast of a "period of maximum leverage" has not only materialized but is being actively operationalized: Iran is unfolding a strategy to exploit its growing leverage by shifting the center of gravity from military bases and naval operations to pressure on the civilian energy system itself. The objective is no longer simply to complicate American military operations but to raise the economic costs of continuing the war until governments begin changing their political calculations [22][23].

The American political calendar is shifting incentives in ways that favor Iran, which treats time itself as a weapon. A pause without a political settlement allows Tehran to keep consolidating its security resistance belt while Washington's options become progressively more difficult. The indicators Pape identifies to watch: Hormuz and the Red Sea — not presidential rhetoric. Watch whether commercial traffic remains constrained, whether Chinese purchases of discounted Iranian oil continue, and whether global oil and refined-product inventories keep tightening. Those indicators will reveal far more about the trajectory of the conflict than another round of diplomatic headlines [23].

### The Exit Trap: Tactical-to-Political Conversion as Escape Criterion

The framework's final refinement, on August 10, formalizes the escape condition. Pape's essay specifies what would falsify the trap: he would change his assessment if sustained Iranian military degradation produced genuine political concessions, if Tehran accepted a durable settlement that addressed the central disputes, and if Washington could reduce military pressure without triggering another cycle of Iranian escalation. A pause in bombing would not by itself prove the trap is over. Nor would another temporary agreement [27].

What Pape looks for is harder: tactical success converted into political success — a stable settlement in which Iran accepts constraints because it believes the alternative is worse, while the United States and its regional partners accept a balance of power they can sustain. Until that conversion happens, any pause in bombing or temporary agreement is merely a pause inside the trap [27].

The standard Pape sets for judging US policy is not whether Trump can defeat Iran but whether, by January 2029, he has left behind a regional system more stable than the one the US entered in 2026 — a regional balance where Iran has enough power and incentive to accept balance rather than expand its leverage, and where Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Turkey, Pakistan, and other regional powers have enough capability to constrain Iran without requiring permanent American dominance. Trump will reject this advice because it sounds like accepting strategic defeat — but that is precisely the psychological trap. The choice is not between victory and withdrawal but between escalating without a credible theory of political victory and using remaining American leverage to construct something that can last [27].

The political sequence — war → energy shock → inflation and shortages → economic pain → political blame → pressure to escalate → deeper strategic failure — is now operating. Pape reads the Iran war as Trump's "Hurricane Katrina." The Saudi-Turkey-Pakistan defense agreement is what the Escalation Trap predicts when the hegemon declines: regional states hedge, build coalitions, seek their own deterrence, and diversify security relationships. The ultimate test is whether the regional system in January 2029 is more stable than what existed in 2026 [27].

## Iran's War Aims: From Survival to Hard Power

Pape tracks Iran's political objectives through three expanding phases — a framework for understanding how war aims mutate as leverage shifts, not a live tracker of day-to-day dynamics. The progression runs from regime preservation through regional reordering to an independent revenge objective, and is then refined by a parallel sequence — survival → ambition → hard power — that emphasizes the operational means executing ambition [8][28].

The first phase is survival. After the decapitation of senior leadership — "bombs kill leaders," as Pape puts it — Tehran's overriding goal was preserving the regime. In his framing, "Iran has been moving from survival, that changed everything in this war" [8]. The initial bombing campaign was tactically successful: it hit targets, killed leaders, destroyed missile launchers. But Iran's capacity to survive that shock redefined the conflict's trajectory, opening the path to expanding objectives [8][28].

Once Iran demonstrated it could contest Hormuz, the war's second phase opened: ambition. Iran began using its growing leverage to push for greater regional power rather than accepting a settlement that would restore the prewar stability. Pape frames the shift: "Rather than accepting a settlement that would restore the prewar stability, Iran is using its growing leverage to push for greater regional power: moving from survival to ambition to hard power" [28]. The ambition phase envisions a new regional security order centered on Iranian influence stretching from Lebanon through Iraq and the Gulf to Yemen and the Red Sea — a geographic span visible in the accompanying "Iran's Resistance Security Belt" map, which labels Hezbollah in Lebanon, Shia militias in Iraq, the Gulf, and Houthis in Yemen as nodes in a continuous arc from the Mediterranean to the Arabian Sea [28].

The third phase adds revenge as an independent political objective. The Supreme Leader's call to action — pledging to "avenge the blood" of the leaders killed and "all the martyrs," meaning the over 4,000 Iranians who had been killed — does not replace survival and ambition but layers onto them. Pape describes a dynamic in which "the more that the Iran war escalates, the more you can expect the desire for revenge, even if they have to kill themselves by Iran's supporters." Revenge becomes a separable engine that leaders must satisfy alongside survival and ambition, even when doing so increases escalation risk [8].

Pape then refines this three-phase political-objective tracker with a parallel sequence emphasizing the means dimension. Iran's demands progressed from war termination through negotiated rollback to unilateral demands — a trajectory he names "survival → ambition → hard power" [28]. "Earlier proposals sought an end to the fighting and a negotiated rollback of U.S. pressure. The latest demands go much further: Tehran now wants the United States to provide financial relief up front, end the naval blockade, and withdraw U.S. forces from the region." The progression — war termination → negotiated rollback → unilateral demands — maps directly onto his signature phrase [28].

Hard power emphasizes the operational means by which ambition is executed: proxy networks providing strategic depth, multi-chokepoint control across the security resistance belt, and a pipeline-bypass counter-strategy that neutralizes Western attempts to route around Iranian chokepoint leverage. The distinction is foundational for Pape: "Iran's objective is no longer simply survival. It is regional power. That distinction matters because controlling the Strait of Hormuz alone gives Tehran leverage over one chokepoint; shaping the political and military balance across the Gulf, Red Sea, and eastern Mediterranean gives it something much larger: the ability to sustain control over all the energy and exports leaving the Gulf region, even as the West attempts pipeline and other Hormuz bypasses" [28]. The accompanying table of "Iran's Expanding Demands" visualizes the progression across three columns — March–May (war & ceasefire), mid-June–July (MOU bargaining), and early July–today (unilateral dictates) — with each phase's demands escalating across military, shipping, and cash-and-nuclear rows, from "stop US/Israeli strikes" to "immediate, total U.S. military exit from region," and from initial war reparations to an "upfront $300B U.S.-backed rebuilding fund" [28].

The diplomatic signature of the hard-power phase is a shift from post-paid concessions to pre-paid demands. The June MOU contemplated a negotiated and phased $300 billion U.S.-backed reconstruction framework — an "open Hormuz first, then talk" structure. The latest demands invert that sequence: everything up front before Hormuz reopens, with unilateral demands shifting the focus to what the U.S. must concede, "eliminating any constraint on Iran's nuclear program" [28]. For the supporting doctrine, see [Iran's Security Resistance Belt]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/iran-security-resistance-belt.html).

## Hormuz as Leverage: From Chokepoint to Institutionalized Control

The arc of Iran's chokepoint leverage traces a deepening: from a conceptual reframe of what the Strait of Hormuz is, through a doctrine that transforms intermittent disruption into a sustained strategic mechanism, through the operational impossibility of reversing that leverage by force, to its legislative institutionalization — and finally to the concession structure that makes voluntary reversal politically impossible. Each step locks the last into place: the reframe generates a forecast; the forecast is confirmed; the confirmed doctrine reveals why suppression fails; the suppression failure invites institutionalization; the institutionalization defines concessions no president could accept.

### The Conceptual Reframe

Pape's framework reframes the Strait of Hormuz from a battlefield where shipping happens to pass through into a lever that [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) controls [2][4]. The real leverage is not the ability to close the chokepoint but the ability to decide whether it stays open — making every shipping passage a concession Iran grants rather than a right that exists independently. As Pape puts it, "Hormuz remains open because Iran permits it to remain open" [2]. The critical strategic reality is that "Iran no longer needs to close Hormuz completely to achieve leverage" [4]. The [Middle East]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html) chokepoint becomes, in this reading, an instrument of permission rather than a corridor of transit.

This reframe generated a confirmed forecast: commercial shipping would be the next escalation pathway, a prediction validated by subsequent attacks that flowed directly from the leverage framework — if Iran's leverage is chokepoint control, the natural escalation vector is the traffic that depends on the chokepoint [2][3]. Reports of a merchant vessel attacked near Oman suggested that the next phase "may already be underway," and Pape concluded that "the strategic logic of escalation continues to unfold exactly as expected" [2]. The three-day escalation that followed — an IRGC drone attack on the Singapore-flagged cargo vessel *Ever Lovely*, US limited air strikes against Iranian coastal facilities, and a second commercial vessel coming under attack near Hormuz — confirmed the forecast [3].

The reframe also diagnosed a phase transition in Iran's posture. Pape argues Iran shifted from negotiating to stop losing to negotiating to win more — a phase transition rather than a marginal change, flipping the basis of its posture from damage limitation to advantage extraction. "Iran is no longer simply fighting for regime survival," he writes. "It is no longer merely trying to prove that it can withstand American and Israeli attacks or talking about future ambitions. Rather than accepting a settlement that would restore the prewar stability, Iran is using its growing leverage to push for greater regional power: moving from survival to ambition to hard power" [28]. Earlier proposals sought an end to the fighting and a negotiated rollback of US pressure; the latest demands go much further. In Tehran's eyes, "negotiations are valuable not because they end the war, but because they help shape the political order that emerges afterward" [1].

The implication is that Iran's growing leverage may weaken the US military position in the Gulf and set the stage for Iranian nuclear weapons: the leverage is not merely military but strategic, creating space for nuclear escalation. Pape forecasts that "by late summer and fall, Iran will attempt to convert growing oil leverage into gradual American military withdrawal from the Gulf" [1], and that "a stronger Iran with greater financial resources, territorial depth, and strategic leverage would be far harder to prevent from eventually acquiring a nuclear deterrent" [28]. See [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html).

### Controlled Instability: From Binary Closure to Sustained Risk Premium

The reframe's doctrinal extension came in late May, when Pape identified "controlled instability" as Iran's post-war strategic doctrine [4]. The framework's hedged central proposition is that "if oil remains above $90 even after Hormuz reopens, then the central strategic outcome of the Iran war may already be decided." Iran "may have discovered something far more powerful than closing Hormuz entirely: that controlled instability can reshape the global balance of power without winning a conventional war" [4].

The mechanism is not binary — open or closed — but continuous. "Limited attacks, periodic disruptions, shipping uncertainty, drone incidents, insurance volatility, and the constant possibility of escalation may already be enough to sustain a large geopolitical risk premium in global energy markets" [4]. The payoff is not revenue extraction alone but a four-effect reshaping of the global balance: "Higher prices generate sharply larger revenues for the regime, strengthen its regional leverage, increase pressure on Western economies already burdened by debt and inflation, and weaken the political cohesion of U.S. alliances during a critical transition period in global power politics" [4]. Pape frames the ultimate prize not as a revenue stream but as price-shaping capability: "modern geopolitical power increasingly turns not simply on military control, but on the ability to shape the long-term price of energy" [4].

The mechanisms through which Iran could sustain this premium are speculative in Pape's presentation: it "could periodically harass shipping under the justification of 'security inspections' or retaliation for sanctions violations," "could impose selective transit fees on certain categories of commercial traffic moving near Iranian-controlled waters," "could tolerate deniable proxy attacks against energy infrastructure while formally denying responsibility," and "could periodically suspend maritime cooperation during moments of political tension, forcing insurance markets to repeatedly reprice Gulf transit risk upward" [4]. Even a sustained period of oil prices remaining 25–40 percent above prewar levels for one to two years "could solve multiple strategic problems simultaneously" [4]. The leadership would not present higher prices as aggression but as "reconstruction financing, sovereign compensation, and economic recovery after attacks on Iranian territory" — and "markets would respond not to legal arguments, but to risk" [4].

### The Political Clock: Why Reopening Fails

Pape's July 20 analysis applies his coercion framework to distinguish tactical attrition from strategic success [16]. The tactical standard measures military performance — have missile launchers been destroyed, have attacks declined, have islands been secured, has freedom of navigation been established? The strategic standard measures whether the political objective of the war has been achieved: the return of normal commercial shipping. Those standards are not the same. Destroying launchers is irrelevant unless insurers and shippers treat Hormuz as routine. "Only shipping companies decide whether to sail. Only insurers decide whether affordable coverage is available. Only commodity markets determine whether supply chains have become reliable again. One successful strike on a commercial tanker can reverse months of restored confidence almost overnight" [16].

Iran's dispersed weapons, forbidding coast, and existential time horizon make continuous suppression or a bounded landing implausible. Iran retains tens of thousands of drones and missiles according to US intelligence estimates leaked in April and May — and "may have more now" — with only 50–100 needed to swarm ships successfully, well within Iran's monthly production capacity [16]. The November midterm election gives Washington a deadline Tehran need only outlast: "Iran does not have to win the military contest. It only has to keep the contest going until America's political clock overtakes its military clock" [16]. See [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html), [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html), and [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html).

### The Impossible Drone Hunt: Why Suppression Fails

Pape's operational briefing on reopening the Strait of Hormuz reframes the military problem around a metric that no battlefield tally can satisfy: commercial confidence [18]. The United States is not fighting simply to defeat an army — it is fighting to restore the conditions under which shipping companies, insurers, and commodity traders treat Hormuz as routine. Those actors do not ask whether America destroyed most Iranian launchers; they ask whether enough remain to threaten the next ship. If the answer is yes, insurance premiums stay elevated, shipping companies reroute vessels, and commercial traffic fails to recover. One successful attack can undo months of military progress [18]. See [Middle East]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html).

The difficulty is structural rather than ideological. [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) covers roughly 1.65 million square kilometers — more than three times the size of Iraq and about four times California — with mountain ranges, deserts, islands, thousands of kilometers of coastline, hardened underground facilities, and dispersed missile and drone depots [18]. Iran's anti-ship missiles range from roughly 80–200 miles for widely deployed coastal systems, with newer systems reportedly exceeding 600 miles; Shahed-136 attack drones have demonstrated ranges of 600–1,500 miles [18]. The United States must therefore suppress launch platforms not just along Iran's coastline but deep into its interior across roughly 500 miles of waterway stretching from Kuwait to the Gulf of Oman. The drone launch area alone is roughly 1.5 times the size of California [18].

The "drone-hunt" problem compounds the geographic scale with a targeting problem. An Iranian Shahed-136 drone is approximately the size of an F-150 pickup — the most common truck in America — and there are an estimated 2.5 to 3 million comparable full-size and mid-size trucks operating in Iran [18]. US forces must find and destroy objects tiny in size and almost identical to millions of civilian vehicles. Destroying 80–90% of drones, missiles, and launchers is not enough: they must all be suppressed continuously, because mobile launchers relocate and every surviving launcher within the strike envelope can keep commercial shipping — and commercial confidence — at risk [18]. Critics who point to overwhelming American military superiority are "answering the wrong question: military superiority wins battles, but it does not eliminate every drone and every mobile launcher scattered across the drone launch zone in Iran." Scale converts Iran's drone advantage into time, moving the United States closer to a political deadline "measured not by battlefield conditions but by the American electoral calendar" [18]. See [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html).

### From Military Coercion to Institutionalized Maritime Authority

Pape's Breaking Analysis #12 (August 7, 2026) tracks Iran's parliament reviewing the "Strategic Action for the Security and Sustainable Development of the Strait of Hormuz and the Persian Gulf" — legislation that would bar vessels and cargo linked to the United States, Israel, and other "hostile nations" while imposing 5–7% cargo-value transit fees and compensation requirements [25]. For a modern Very Large Crude Carrier transporting roughly $150 million in oil, the proposed transit fee of 5% to 7% of cargo value would amount to approximately $7.5 million to $10.5 million per voyage [25].

The bill represents a shift from military coercion to institutionalized maritime authority: "If enacted, Iran would no longer be threatening to control the Strait of Hormuz. It would be legislating that control as the normal operating rule for international commerce — a profound strategic shift from military coercion to institutionalized maritime authority" [25]. Drawing on his *Bombing to Win* thesis, Pape argues that "air power alone rarely compels a determined adversary to abandon what it regards as a core national interest. It can destroy military capabilities, raise economic costs, and delay strategic programs. But it seldom changes the opponent's underlying political objective" [25]. The legislation confirms that "Iran's strategic calculus has not fundamentally changed — it has moved from survival to ambition," and airpower has reached its "familiar limit" rather than "accomplished nothing" [25].

Accepting the bill's terms would mean accepting "a new regional order written in Tehran" and "the collapse of the perception of America's escalation dominance, invincibility, and leadership around the world" [25]. If those terms cannot be accepted, Stage 3 becomes necessary — "a transition from coercion to physical control" via a major amphibious or ground campaign to secure the coastline, reopen shipping, and deny Iran the ability to reimpose its restrictions [25]. Airpower "can destroy launchers, ports, and command centers. It cannot permanently guarantee commercial navigation through a hostile coastline over 600 miles long" [25]. The "Hail Mary" ground war threshold is Pape's named concept for the political-calendar-driven version of this escalation: "as the costs to Americans inevitably rise due to Iran's relentless and punishing control of Hormuz, the president may find he has nothing to lose by gambling on a limited ground operation to score a symbolic, if not real, victory before the midterms. For the president, this would be like a Hail Mary Pass at the end of a football game when the quarterback has little to lose anyway" [25]. See [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html).

### The Four-Pillar Concession Matrix

Pape's August 11 essay provides a diagnostic for what any voluntary Hormuz reopening would structurally require of the US — four concessions that together rewrite the geopolitical rules of the Middle East [28]:

1. **Accept Iran as a regional power center** — tolerating an Iranian sphere of influence stretching from the Gulf toward the Red Sea and Mediterranean, while abandoning demands to eliminate the missile, drone, and allied networks that give Tehran strategic depth, and imposing credible limits on Israeli military action against Iran and its regional partners [28].

2. **Fund Iran's expansion with up-front money** — major sanctions relief, access to frozen funds, unrestricted oil exports, and acceptance of some form of Iranian transit fees for managing Hormuz. "The issue is not merely compensation; it is establishing a continuing revenue stream from Iran's geographic leverage" [28].

3. **Reduce the US military footprint to operational worthlessness** — accepting that the bombing campaign has demonstrated the limits of airpower against Iran's geography and asymmetric strategy, and therefore accepting limits on US-led regional primacy rather than assuming military pressure can restore the old order [28].

4. **Accept the prospect of an Iranian nuclear deterrent** — "a stronger Iran with greater financial resources, territorial depth, and strategic leverage would be far harder to prevent from eventually acquiring a nuclear deterrent" [28].

No American president could accept even one of these concessions, much less the whole package. "That is the price of voluntary de-escalation: not Iran's surrender, but America's acceptance of a new regional balance of power" [28]. The impossibility theorem defines the collision course: Hormuz is likely to remain shut for weeks if not months [28].

## The Limits of Airpower: From Pickaxe Mountain to Civilian Punishment

Airpower can destroy targets, but it rarely compels determined regimes to abandon core political objectives. Pape's analysis of the [US-Iran War 2026]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) traces two parallel failure modes in which the military instrument applied cannot achieve the political objective — one against hardened nuclear targets, the other against civilian morale — each narrowing choices toward ground operations or strategic defeat.

### Pickaxe Mountain and the Ground Power Dilemma

On July 21, 2026, Pape's Breaking Analysis #7 introduced a new strategic dimension to the Escalation Trap. [Israeli]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html) intelligence claims that [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) has transferred thousands of uranium-enrichment centrifuges into deeply fortified tunnels beneath Pickaxe Mountain near Natanz, buried roughly 300 to 450 feet under solid rock and beyond the reach of existing conventional bunker-busting bombs. Iran continues to deny the IAEA access needed for independent verification, while satellite imagery reportedly shows continued construction, reinforced tunnel entrances, and heavy vehicle activity. [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) has repeatedly identified the facility as a potential military target, but it remains untouched [17].

The core analytical move is Pape's long-standing argument — "As I have argued for decades" — that airpower can destroy targets but rarely compels determined regimes to abandon core political objectives. The U.S. and Israeli bombing campaign achieved major tactical successes, yet if Israeli intelligence is correct, Iran responded not by surrendering its nuclear program but by moving its surviving enrichment capability deeper underground. Airpower did not solve the strategic problem; it transformed it. A facility buried 300–450 feet beneath solid rock cannot be reliably eliminated by conventional bunker-busting strikes alone. Pape labels this "Stage Three: the Ground Power Dilemma." If Washington concludes Pickaxe Mountain has become Iran's protected nuclear sanctuary, the choice narrows dramatically: tolerate a hardened nuclear program or accept the far greater risks of operations that move beyond an air campaign [17].

Pape identifies a classic Escalation Trap mechanism at work: as Iran's critical nuclear assets become harder to destroy, future airstrikes yield diminishing strategic returns while pressure for more dangerous military options steadily grows. The dynamic is reinforced by fear-driven escalation. Drawing on the 2003 Iraq WMD precedent — when the fear that Saddam Hussein possessed weapons of mass destruction helped drive the invasion of Iraq, even though those weapons were never found — Pape argues that leaders escalate based on what they believe an adversary is capable of doing, not on what later proves true. If Washington concludes Pickaxe Mountain has made Iran's nuclear program effectively invulnerable, that belief alone can create overwhelming pressure for broader military action, regardless of what inspectors might eventually discover. In crises like this, uncertainty favors escalation — the absence of verifiable information about what lies beneath the mountain generates its own coercive logic. To reverse that dynamic, U.S. intelligence must do more than fail to confirm the Israeli claim; it must convincingly disprove it [17].

The political clock intensifies the pressure. If the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) cannot reopen the Strait of Hormuz before the November midterm elections, pressure for broader military escalation — including attacks on deeply buried nuclear facilities like Pickaxe Mountain — can still grow. Pape explains why America is unlikely to achieve that objective, and why the political clock matters as much as the military one, in his weekly [Escalation Trap]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/escalation-theory-critique.html) tracking across the [Middle East]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html) [17].

### Civilian Punishment and the Revenge Dynamic

The second failure mode generalizes the first. When bombing cannot reach the hardened target, the instrument turns toward what it can reach: civilian infrastructure. When [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) declared that Iranians "have not suffered enough yet" and signaled he was "considering a massive attack. Bigger than ever before. I am close to making a decision. We are all set for it," Pape reads the statement as a pivot from military denial to massive civilian punishment — striking bridges and electrical power infrastructure whose principal purpose is not military denial but the imposition of visible national pain. The obvious target is Tehran: with roughly ten million residents, it is Iran's political capital, economic center, communications hub, and transportation nexus, and nowhere else in Iran carries comparable political weight [19].

Drawing on years teaching the strategy of coercive air campaigns to U.S. Air Force officers, Pape walks through what the loss of electricity would actually mean for a city of ten million. Electricity is the invisible system that allows a modern city to exist; military facilities typically shift to protected generators during wartime, but civilians cannot. In July and August afternoon temperatures near 100°F (38°C), air conditioning is not a luxury but essential infrastructure. Pumps move close to 800 million gallons of treated water each day across the city, and most buildings have only limited rooftop storage, meaning many residents would lose running water within hours or, at most, a day. Refrigerated warehouses, food processors, and household refrigerators all depend on uninterrupted power; studies suggest roughly one-quarter of perishable food is rapidly lost when cold chains fail. Destroying all major bridges magnifies food shortages, paralyzing distribution by severing core trucking supply chains and dropping inbound delivery capacity by 50% to 90%. Tehran has roughly 170 hospitals with more than 25,000 beds, including approximately 5,000 dialysis patients who require treatment several times each week; as generator fuel, clean water, oxygen, and medicines become scarce, hospitals begin rationing lifesaving care. The closest American comparison is the 1995 Chicago heat wave, when 739 people died in just five days in a city of 2.7 million — less than one-third Tehran's population. A citywide blackout lasting one to three months would almost certainly produce casualties on a far greater scale — thousands a month [19].

The strategic consequences, however, are what concern Pape most. History strongly suggests that inflicting massive civilian suffering through electric-power targeting rarely produces rapid political capitulation when governments believe their survival is at stake. The United States Strategic Bombing Survey — which interviewed thousands of German civilians after World War II and examined captured Gestapo reports, civilian letters, and official morale documents — found that bombing civilian infrastructure lowered morale but did not produce political capitulation. Its central finding: the strongest emotional response among civilians who experienced direct personal loss was not surrender but anger. People whose homes were destroyed, whose families were injured, or whose communities ceased functioning became increasingly consumed by thoughts of revenge and retaliation. Even "lighter" bombing that disrupted daily life without completely destroying cities often intensified demands that Germany strike back against its enemies rather than compromise with them [19].

Thomas Griffith — a U.S. Air Force officer, Gulf War POW, and Pape's former student when he taught for the Air Force — studied electrical-system targeting across WWII, Korea, Vietnam, and Desert Storm and found that such attacks provide limited military utility and rarely achieve intended strategic goals. Historical evidence indicates that power disruption has minimal impact on winning wars or breaking civilian morale, leading Griffith to a recommendation against targeting grids due to low value and high collateral damage. For decades afterward, those lessons helped push electric-power targeting to the margins of American air strategy; U.S. doctrine increasingly emphasized precision attacks against military forces rather than civilian infrastructure. Trump's recent comments suggest that this long-standing restraint may now be under reconsideration [19].

A prolonged collapse of electricity in Tehran would not simply create humanitarian suffering — it would ensure that millions of ordinary Iranians personally experience the war inside their own homes for the first time. Every family forced to watch an elderly parent die from heat, every child without clean water, every patient denied dialysis becomes another powerful source of motivation for retaliation. If history is any guide, the result would be not political surrender but a generation of Iranians determined to seek payback against Americans, Israelis, and the countries hosting U.S. forces — whether that revenge comes next month or years from now. Iran has already attacked two of Kuwait's eight water desalination plants, which provide over 90% of the drinking water for the country's 5 million people. On July 22, Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi posted on X: "Our defense doctrine is clear: eye for an eye. Any aggression against Iran, including our infrastructure, will compel a powerful and decisive response" [19].

This is, for Pape, the essence of the [Escalation Trap]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/escalation-theory-critique.html). Once punishment replaces denial as the primary military strategy, both sides become convinced that more escalation — not less — is necessary. Each new round of punishment creates stronger incentives for retaliation, while every act of retaliation convinces the attacker that still greater punishment is required. The result is not a path toward ending the war but a self-reinforcing spiral in which the political logic increasingly overwhelms the military logic — a dynamic with direct implications for the [geoeconomics of water]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/geoeconomics-of-water.html), as Iran's attacks on Kuwait's desalination capacity already illustrate [19].

## Regional Expansion: From Single Chokepoint to Three-Theater War

The Iran war begins as a contest over a single waterway — the Strait of Hormuz — and expands across July 2026 to a doctrine linking three bodies of water into one strategic theater. The axis of expansion runs from a specific piece of bypass infrastructure Iran perceives as a threat, to the IRGC's formal geographic doctrine that gives that threat its coherence. What changes is not the intensity of the conflict but its map: where the fighting happens, and how many routes it reaches.

### The Aqaba Threat: Energy Bypass Infrastructure as Target

The first movement beyond Hormuz targets not a ship or a base but a pipeline that does not yet exist. In his Breaking Analysis #6 (July 19, 2026), Pape identifies a new escalation vector: [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html)'s threat to Aqaba, Jordan's only coastal city. The US Embassy in Amman issued an emergency security alert advising Americans to avoid Aqaba's international airport and seaport due to a specific and credible threat. As Pape was about to publish, air raid sirens went off in Jordan warning that Iranian missiles were incoming against Aqaba [15].

The threat follows a Washington meeting between [Iraq]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iraq.html)'s prime minister and Jordan's foreign minister, reportedly to speed up and break ground for the long-delayed Basra-Aqaba pipeline — a 1,600-kilometer, $18 billion project built to carry 2.25 million barrels a day from southern [Iraq]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iraq.html) to Jordan's Red Sea coast. The Iran war has cut southern Iraqi exports roughly 70%, exposing Baghdad's dependence on the Strait of Hormuz and making the bypass pipeline a strategic necessity. For [Iraq]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iraq.html), the pipeline is about security rather than profit: Aqaba costs about $6 per barrel versus 60 cents through the Gulf, but the premium buys insulation from Iranian coercion. For Jordan, the pipeline offers transit fees and 150,000 barrels per day for its Zarqa refinery [15].

Under the Escalation Trap framework, Pape argues that [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) has powerful incentives to widen the conflict beyond Hormuz itself and strike any infrastructure that could permanently reduce its coercive leverage. A functioning Basra-Aqaba pipeline would not simply bypass one waterway; it would weaken Iran's long-term ability to threaten global energy flows. The pipeline would undercut what Pape terms Iran's "security resistance belt" — a chain of control stretching from the [Mediterranean]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html) through the Red Sea to the Gulf — by creating a permanent alternative outlet for Iraqi oil that bypasses Iran's chokepoint. That makes projects such as Aqaba increasingly attractive military targets — not because of their immediate economic value, but because they challenge Iran's broader strategy of preserving leverage through control of regional energy chokepoints across the [Middle East]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html). Striking bypass infrastructure before it becomes operational is cheaper than competing with it after it exists [15].

### The Security Resistance Belt: Three Pillars, One Theater

Twelve days later, Pape's Breaking Analysis #9 (July 31, 2026) identifies the IRGC doctrine that gives geographic coherence to this escalation strategy. The drone strike at Damietta Port, Pape writes, "directly fits the operational doctrine of the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC), which has explicitly threatened to construct a unified 'security resistance belt' designed to hold global maritime trade hostage." Iran is "progressively connecting the Persian Gulf, the Red Sea, and now the Mediterranean into a single strategic theater" [22].

The three pillars of this doctrine are visible in a map accompanying the piece, captioned "The Three Pillars of the 'Security Resistance Belt'": the Persian Gulf Front, enforced by IRGC swarm boats and missile systems; the Red Sea Front, enforced by Houthi drone and missile blockades on commercial shipping; and the Mediterranean Front, marked by the strike on the US-owned gas tanker Energos Winter. The operational logic is that every new front forces Washington to defend another piece of the global energy system without reducing pressure anywhere else. By opening a third Mediterranean front, Iran is sending everyone — Washington, Israel, Gulf states, and neutrals in the region — a clear message: "No one gets oil until America goes home." The strike in Egypt was aimed as much at governments as infrastructure. Every neutral state hosting energy facilities now faces the same calculation: remain aligned with Washington and risk becoming part of the battlefield, or distance yourself and reduce the chance of being targeted. Pape's assessment is that neutrality may not shield economically vital infrastructure if the war continues to widen [22]. See [Iran's Security Resistance Belt]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/iran-security-resistance-belt.html).

The Damietta strike marks the geographic turning point. "The most important feature of this attack is not the drone. It is the map." Until that week, every major energy disruption in the Iran war lay south of the Suez Canal. Damietta changes that. For the first time, Iran or its proxies have demonstrated the ability to threaten both ends of the Middle East's export system. Hormuz is no longer the only choke point that matters [22].

The attack represents, in Pape's reading, the first unmistakable sign that the Iran war is evolving from a conflict over military access to the Gulf into a broader contest over the global energy system in the Middle East. It is becoming a contest over who controls the economic arteries of globalization. The dilemma facing Washington has expanded accordingly: in June, the dilemma was to concede to Iran controlling Hormuz or escalate; now, it is to concede to Iran controlling all three avenues of access to Persian Gulf energy or escalate. Every additional front raises the political cost of restraint while raising the military cost of escalation. That is the Escalation Trap [22].

## The Economic Clock: Buffers, Bottlenecks, and the Wealth Transfer

The economic dimension of the Iran war evolves along a single axis: the progressive decomposition of the offset stack that has delayed the oil shock. What begins as a catalog of three finite buffers — each eroding — becomes a pricing problem (what is the wartime-risk premium worth?), then a two-bottleneck problem (crude plus refining), and finally a structural clock in which every passing day tightens Iran's leverage. The trajectory runs from decomposition to countdown.

### The Two-Front Maritime Trap and the Three-Buffer Framework

Pape's July 28 analysis reframes the war as a two-front maritime contest across two interconnected theaters — Hormuz and the [Red Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/red-sea.html) — produced by Iran's activation of Houthi attacks on Saudi oil tankers [20]. The single-chokepoint problem has become a structural trap: closing one corridor no longer solves the bypass problem because the bypass itself can be attacked.

To understand what has delayed the shock, Pape decomposes the offset to the roughly 20 mbpd Hormuz baseline into three identifiable components [20]. Approximately 3–4 mbpd came from commercial inventories and strategic petroleum reserves: beginning in March, the United States and thirty-one partner countries agreed to release roughly 400 million barrels, including 172 million barrels from the U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve, with releases scheduled through the summer. Approximately 4–5 mbpd effectively disappeared because global demand weakened, driven primarily by lower Chinese imports following the Trump–Xi summit; world oil consumption fell from roughly 107 million barrels per day before the war to about 103 million. The largest adjustment — roughly 7 mbpd — came from physical bypass: Saudi Arabia shifted roughly 5 million barrels per day west through its East-West Pipeline to Yanbu on the Red Sea, while the UAE moved roughly 2 million barrels per day through the Abu Dhabi Crude Oil Pipeline to Fujairah on the Gulf of Oman, outside the Strait. Taken together, these adjustments offset roughly 16 million of the 20 million barrels per day that normally depended on Hormuz. They did not replace the Strait's full capacity. They bought time.

The Red Sea front now threatens the largest buffer. After Houthi attacks on Saudi oil tankers, over 40% of [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html)'s oil bypassing Hormuz via the Red Sea is not hitting the global market; more attacks could reduce this further [20]. The largest buffer — the Red Sea bypass — is now the biggest to begin failing. By the end of August, those offsets may have fallen by more than half as reserves become harder to draw, Chinese imports recover, and Red Sea bypass capacity remains under military pressure [20].

The closest historical test for what reopening a corridor requires is Operation Rough Rider (March 15 – May 6, 2025), which Pape treats as the minimum planning assumption [20]. Over roughly seven weeks, U.S. forces — anchored by the USS Gerald R. Ford carrier strike group and including B-2 bombers operating from Diego Garcia — conducted more than 1,000 strikes against Houthi command centers, missile sites, drone infrastructure, and senior leaders. The Houthis were not destroyed, not even decisively deterred. Instead, the Houthis agreed to stop attacking American naval vessels while preserving both the capability and the political willingness to continue threatening commercial shipping. Shipping through the Red Sea remains down 60% even a year later. The mechanism is straightforward: airpower can destroy launchers, warehouses, and commanders, but it cannot reliably suppress a dispersed, underground missile-and-drone network quickly enough to guarantee freedom of navigation across an entire maritime theater. If reopening one maritime corridor required months of sustained combat and still failed to restore commercial shipping, analysts should begin by assuming that reopening two corridors simultaneously will take at least as long — and probably longer.

The carrier arithmetic compounds the constraint [20]. Of America's eleven aircraft carriers, only four are currently deployed worldwide. Two — the USS *Abraham Lincoln* and USS *George H.W. Bush* carrier strike groups — are already committed to sustained combat operations against Iran. A third, the USS *Nimitz*, is deployed in the Caribbean, while the USS *George Washington* maintains America's only deployed carrier presence in the Western Pacific. The remaining carriers are in maintenance, overhaul, refit, or pre-deployment training and cannot be generated quickly for combat operations. There is therefore no hidden reserve. At most, Washington could shift the *Nimitz* from the Caribbean and the *George Washington* from the Pacific into the broader Middle East — merely increasing the available carrier force from two to four. Those four carriers would still have to sustain combat operations against Iran while reopening two separate maritime choke points: some combination of roughly two for the Red Sea and two for Hormuz, or perhaps three for Hormuz and one for the Red Sea. Neither represents the kind of overwhelming concentration of force that would justify expecting a rapid strategic result. Both imply another prolonged campaign against dispersed missile and drone networks operating across two interconnected theaters. The United States did not plan for a long war against Iran and now does not have the deployable forces for two long naval wars.

Two conditions could invalidate the planning assumption [20]. The first is a political settlement: Washington accepts terms that persuade Tehran and its partners to stop threatening shipping — likely a full military withdrawal of all US forces and bases from the region, which Pape judges implausible as of today. The second is military suppression: the United States generates massive new naval forces to the Red Sea battle beyond the one carrier battlegroup deployed in 2025. Absent either, the planning assumption survives the stress test.

Pape defines the condition this produces as strategic overstretch: "Strategic overstretch does not mean the United States is running out of ships or aircraft. It means the gap between military commitments and usable military capacity begins to widen" [20].

The August 2026 forecast is operational, not abstract. By the end of August, those offsets may have fallen by more than half as reserves become harder to draw, Chinese imports recover, and Red Sea bypass capacity remains under military pressure [20]. The chart accompanying the piece shows the U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve declining by nearly 100 million barrels since the start of the Middle East conflict on February 28, 2026, "aggressively closing the gap toward its statutory floor" — the dashed red line on the chart labeled "Congressional Mandated Minimum." Weekly releases have slowed markedly; world reserve drawdowns will likely drop to half their current rate or less. A second chart shows Chinese refiners accelerating purchases of Russian crude cargoes, with Russian crude flows to China recovering sharply to approximately 1.4 mbpd by late July after a mid-July trough. China's reduced demand could fall by a third or more from current levels. "Shortages – not just disruption – are about to begin in earnest" [20].

### Risk-Pricing Reframe and the Wealth-Transfer Arithmetic

Pape's Breaking Analysis #10 (August 5, 2026) reframes the Iran-Oman negotiation track not as a shipping arrangement but as a dispute over risk [24]. Treasury Secretary Bessent stated on CNBC's Squawk Box at approximately 7:15 AM ET that a deal to open the Strait of Hormuz was possible "today or tomorrow." Global stock markets rallied to record highs — the S&P 500 gaining 1.8%, the Nasdaq surging 2.6% — while global oil prices plummeted over 5% to a four-week low below $80 a barrel. Pape poses the question: was yesterday just another day like almost any other in the 157-day-old Iran war — with US administration promises of final peace in the offing, market reaction, and the war goes on and on?

The rumored 7% cargo-value fee — reported in Iranian media, not certain to become part of the final agreement — is not a Malacca-style navigation fee [24]. Seven percent is not an Iranian toll; it is today's wartime insurance premium, roughly what private marine insurers currently charge to cover commercial voyages through the Persian Gulf because of the risk of missiles, drones, and mines. This is a much different arrangement than the Malacca model — a modest flat transit service fee, possibly in the 1% range, shared equally between Iran and Oman to fund navigation safety, environmental protection, search-and-rescue, and maritime security.

A typical VLCC carries roughly 2 million barrels of crude worth about $160 million at $80 per barrel [24]. Even if only a fraction of prewar Gulf exports resumed, a 7% levy applied across Hormuz traffic could generate well over $100 billion annually — on the order of $140 billion under prewar shipping assumptions. If the rumored 7% cargo-value fee ever became reality, it would represent one of the largest geopolitical wealth transfers in modern history. An income stream of that magnitude would finance Iran's conventional military modernization, strengthen its regional proxy network, and provide substantial resources for its broader strategic ambitions.

It is therefore difficult to imagine any American president accepting a permanent arrangement that effectively institutionalizes Iranian geopolitical leverage over the world's most important energy chokepoint [24]. Either no agreement is reached, or any agreement that leaves this issue unresolved is likely to prove fragile from the outset.

Pape's prescriptive test for a genuine breakthrough is public confirmation from both Washington and Tehran, agreement on where, when, and how the deal will be concluded, and consistent official statements — not competing narratives [24]. As of today, Iranian officials and media are dismissing US assertions of direct bilateral negotiations as "repeated lies" and "desperate cries," maintaining that Tehran is only engaging via regional mediators. "Telephone Game" diplomacy often fails for known reasons: intermediaries can pass messages, but they cannot make concessions. When proximity talks do lead to breakthroughs, it is often after months and years. The US-Iran JCPOA seemed to come together quickly in Vienna in 18 days in 2015, but it was actually preceded by more than two years of grueling, secret proximity talks brokered by Oman in Muscat, beginning as early as 2012.

### The Crude-Refining Double Bottleneck and Stage Four

Pape's August 9 live briefing with energy analyst [Jeff Currie]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/jeff-currie.html) pivots the energy analysis from "supply deficit" to "actual shortage" by adding a second bottleneck: refining capacity [26]. Currie highlighted Russia's collapsing refining capacity because of [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html)'s attacks on Russia's refineries, constraining refined products globally. To produce gasoline and diesel, three barrels of crude at the refinery — which takes years to build and can be destroyed overnight — yield roughly two plus one barrels. The crack spread, the margin between buying crude and selling gasoline plus diesel, reached $70/bbl, the highest ever recorded. China has a tremendous amount of refining capacity and currently has refining surplus, whereas the US runs right at capacity, if not overdoing it. Both bottlenecks constrain simultaneously: the crude shortage is going to hurt poor countries the most, while the refining bottleneck is especially going to add extra pain for the wealthy countries.

Pape's sensitivity analysis layers scenarios on a $4.06 baseline [26]. Without the loss of Saudi oil, the current average gas price is actually going to likely improve after Labor Day. But adding the loss of Saudi oil — Houthi blockage forcing Saudi exports toward zero — adds almost a full dollar a gallon. Adding the refining crunch and SPR halt drives the price of gasoline to over $5.50 a gallon nationwide by October, over $6 in November–December, already at $6.50 in Chicago. The only part of this that is unsure is the Saudi part; the refinery crunch and the SPR halt are "almost baked into the cake" [26].

The briefing widens the strategic picture [26]. [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html)'s Supreme National Security Council has issued six demands before Hormuz reopens — roughly $300 billion up-front for damages, over $100 billion in frozen assets, and ending Israel's occupation of Palestine — which Pape reads as victor's-justice terms rather than bargaining. The MOU was a post-paid concession structure: "let's get together and let's talk, we'll open the Hormuz and then we'll talk and get concessions." This is exactly the other way around: they want everything up front. Pape thinks Iran thinks it won the war and is getting victor's justice — "it's their way or the highway."

Stage Four is the rising-hegemon scenario if US inaction continues: strategic paralysis — Trump's cancellation of the big bombing campaign, the CENTCOM top air commander's mid-war retirement ("very unusual in the middle of a war"), and pretty strong, not more than rumors, that bomber wings have had all weekends canceled — masks a trajectory toward Iran becoming the rising regional hegemon in the Persian Gulf, the fourth center of world power. Predicted actions include toppling MBS and organizing assassination attempts against MBS and UAE leaders. Iran is "playing balance-of-power politics here like a black belt" while the US behaves "like green belts." Between now and January is Iran's period of maximum leverage; Pape thinks they are going to try to "drive a stake in the heart of the Trump presidency" going right through the American economy.

The Saudi-Turkey-Pakistan "Mecca" defense agreement is not "a new NATO" — Pape does not see Turkey and Saudi Arabia handing over control to a Pakistani general of their own forces and vice versa, placing them very far from an actual war-fighting alliance. Turkey's 18-million Kurdish population limits its intervention appetite: if Turkey gets involved, a counter would be to stir up trouble among those Kurds against Turkey itself. The pact fills a power vacuum left by US decline: in balance-of-power politics, states do not like vacuum; states fill the vacuum and often have power rivalries to fill it. On munitions, Pape rejects the "showstopper" framing: states do not stop because they run out of weapons — "there's no war in World War II where they ran out of weapons and they all stopped the war." A JDAM, fundamentally, is a backpack with a GPS transmitter and wires connecting to the fins of the bomb; you can build a poor man's precision-guided bomb for about $40,000. Punishment strategy produces fight, not flight — a Bombing to Win finding generalized from 35 years of air-campaign study.

### The Oil Inventory Clock

Pape's August 11 essay introduces a structural variable distinct from the three-stage Escalation Trap: the oil inventory clock [28]. Every day that Hormuz remains constrained, the world consumes another layer of the buffers that have prevented a much larger oil shock. The IEA reported global observed inventories falling by 368 million barrels from March to August 1, while stocks on land in OECD countries fell sharply. The United States has an emergency reserve. China has accumulated substantial commercial and strategic stocks. Producers have rerouted some Gulf oil around alternative corridors. But these are buffers, not substitutes for normal supply. And the Red Sea route — the most important bypass for some Gulf exports — is itself under pressure from Houthi attacks; Reuters reports that shipping through both Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb remains disrupted. Time is not neutral: as the buffers shrink, the economic cost of continued disruption rises — and so does Iran's leverage. The longer the strategic pause lasts, the more the oil clock works in Iran's favor.

## Suicide Terrorism Strategic Logic

Pape, who compiled the first complete database of all suicide attacks after 9/11, applies that research to the current war. His central finding from that dataset is that 95% of suicide attacks are carried out for a strategic purpose — to coerce democracies to withdraw military forces from territory the terrorists prize [8]. This finding led to a prediction no other terrorism expert made at the time: that invading Iraq would touch off the largest suicide terrorist campaign in modern times. That prediction came true [8].

The data show that suicide terrorism exploded after the United States occupied Iraq and Afghanistan, and declined when the forever wars ended. In Pape's assessment, the United States is now "doing the reverse" in Iran — escalating in a way that historically generates suicide terrorism [8]. The greatest danger he identifies is not centrally directed operations but inspired attacks by individuals or small networks responding to a broader political appeal. The closest historical parallel Pape draws to the Supreme Leader's call to action is Ayatollah Khomeini's 1989 fatwa calling for the execution of Salman Rushdie, which led to attacks and deaths for decades around the world [8].

Pape expects "one possibly two attacks" — mostly inspired, not command-directed — to fuel public support for Stage 3 [8].

## Posture on Trump's Escalation Logic and Nuclear Taboos

Pape frames Trump's escalation not as a preference for ground war but as a political survival calculation. Two domestic-pressure factors, in Pape's reading, close off the exit ramp.

The first is the Hoover risk. Oil inventories are running down and at about the same rate as before the MOU. Accordingly, President Trump still faces the risk he himself acknowledged, that he could become the next Herbert Hoover.

The second is the posture of the MAGA base. According to the Reagan Institute's nationwide Summer Survey, conducted May 26–June 3, 51 percent of self-identified MAGA Republicans favor replacing Iran's government, while only 25 percent prefer a negotiated settlement.

Together these pressures produce, in Pape's analysis, a logic in which escalation is the only politically survivable path. Pape's view is that Trump is escalating to reopen Hormuz — not because he wants to fight a ground war or a long war, and not because the military option is certain to succeed. It is, Pape says, "because it is certain that if he doesn't open Hormuz, he's going to fail politically." Trump, in Pape's reading, "is trying to find a way to not have it be just about him" — searching for a framing that offsets the personal political stakes. [8]

On the oil market, Pape observes that traders are terrified to buck Trump. The mechanism is career risk, not political retaliation: a trader who goes long on oil and gets it wrong loses the money and is fired — "not politically fired, but that guy's gone." This produces a pervasive groupthink — "packed journalism, packed trading, packed everything" — in which career risk determines market behavior rather than analytical conviction. Pape contrasts his own position: "I'm tenured. My kids are through college. This is a different world for someone like me." [8]

On nuclear weapons, Pape is categorical. He rejects suggestions — including from [Lindsey Graham]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/lindsey-graham.html) — of using them against Iran. Radiation clouds cannot be controlled and travel for hundreds of miles, and the radioactive soot would kill something like 20,000 to 30,000 American troops over time through radiation sickness and cancers. "I just can't even imagine." [8]
