# Background

Ronald Wright is a Canadian-born archaeologist, historian, and novelist. He delivered the 2004 Massey Lectures, published as *A Short History of Progress*, in which he developed the concept of "progress traps" — innovations that yield short-term benefits but create compounding, unforeseen problems that cannot be easily solved because the solution would threaten the existing status quo. The book draws on archaeological and historical evidence from collapsed civilizations, including Stone Age megafauna hunters, Sumerian irrigation agriculture, and Easter Island deforestation, to argue that civilization is living off the "capital" rather than the "interest" of nature. Wright's framework is central to the bundle's [Civilizational Collapse and Progress Traps]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/civilizational-collapse-and-progress-traps.html) theme and is extensively referenced by [The Honest Sorcerer](the-honest-sorcerer.html) and in [Great Power Competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html).

# Key Frameworks

## Progress Traps

Wright's core concept: throughout history, societies have repeatedly fallen into "progress traps" — situations where a successful innovation creates dependency and unintended consequences that ultimately undermine the society that adopted it. The trap is that the solution would require abandoning the innovation itself, which has become structurally embedded in the society's population levels, social organization, and power structures.

### Stone Age Megafauna

Wright's first exemplar: hunters who perfected cliff-driving techniques ate well for a season but wiped out their local food source, forcing the transition to agriculture as an escape that paradoxically created deeper dependencies.

### Sumerian Irrigation

The Sumerians built irrigation canals that boosted agricultural productivity, but dissolved salts deposited on the surface eventually rendered the land barren — a case where the initial triumph of an innovation made society dependent on a technology that was simultaneously destroying its foundation.

### Easter Island

Wright's third exemplar: deforestation on Easter Island destroyed the shipbuilding capacity that gave the islanders their identity and means of contact with the outside world. The island's isolation made long-distance trade economically impossible, meaning settlement itself was the proximate condition for the trap.

### Civilization Living Off Capital

Wright's overarching moral accusation: civilization no longer lives off the "interest" of nature but off its "capital" — consuming the resource base on which future survival depends and treating depletion as growth [1].

## Contested Empirical Foundations

Wright's framework has been challenged on empirical grounds. A 2009 review in *Cicero* argues that the Stone Age megafauna "trap" is contestable — many large species were displaced by competition with pastoralists and farmers, not by hunter-cultures. The Easter Island example is described as circular: the trap had already snapped shut when humans settled an island so remote that long-distance trade was economically impossible, making settlement itself — not deforestation — the proximate cause. The reviewer characterizes Wright's method as "ideology critique" with only patchy empirical support, placing it in a German intellectual lineage from Walter Benjamin through Adorno and Horkheimer, and concludes that his policy prescriptions — consume less, redistribute more — are morally sincere but logically and economically unworkable, ending in "faith" rather than analysis [2].
