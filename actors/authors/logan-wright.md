# Background

Logan Wright is a partner at the Rhodium Group, an independent research firm specializing in China's economy and global implications. He is the author of *Broken China: How the Economic Miracle Shattered and What it Means for the World*. His analytical approach centers on China's financial system — banking, credit, fiscal, and property markets — as the structural engine of both growth and decline. Rhodium Group produces alternative estimates of Chinese GDP growth that challenge official figures, arguing that real growth has been significantly lower than claimed since 2022.

# Key Frameworks

## Financial-System Lens on China's Trajectory

Wright argues that China's policy tools for generating economic growth are fundamentally broken — not through crisis or collapse, but through steady decay. The proximate cause of the slowdown is the property market collapse; the ultimate cause is the end of an unprecedented post-2008 credit bubble in which banks added credit equivalent to one-third of global GDP. Post-bust, China lacks the capacity to influence economic outcomes through long-term plans, because credit flows were the mechanism through which plans became reality [1].

## Demand-Leverage Theory

Wright reframes US-China strategic competition as a negotiation over the pace and scope of decoupling, rather than a race to develop capabilities. The core insight: China has a surplus advantage on the supply side (export controls, rare earths) but no alternative to US and allied consumer markets. Western demand-side tools — import quotas, tariffs, technical standards — become more powerful over time, while China's supply restrictions weaken as alternatives develop. The Trump administration forfeited leverage by meeting China's supply threats with supply-side responses (rare earths deal → scaled back semiconductor export controls) rather than exploiting demand asymmetry [1].

## Deterrability of Chinese Power

Wright argues that China's threats are now contingent, time-limited, and more deterrable than inexorable. The capacity to execute long-term plans is impaired by fiscal and financial decay, and sticking with the decaying model shortens Beijing's time horizons. US-China strategic competition is not inevitable — it is the product of policy choices in the 2010s, mostly Beijing's [1].
