# Background

Phillips P. O'Brien frames the Revolution in Military Affairs as an unstoppable hardware-and-software transformation that is "de-humanizing" or "de-populating" the battlefield, drastically reducing humans in direct combat and changing how wars start, are waged, judged ethically, and decided. He calls it "the greatest revolution in military affairs since the development of metal weapons." See [Revolution in Military Affairs]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/revolution-in-military-affairs.html) and [Global Depopulation]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-depopulation.html) [15].

His framework distinguishes long-range strike, aimed at limiting or destroying an opponent's capacity to generate new military force through targets such as oil refineries and military production, from medium-range strike, aimed at disrupting the deployment of already generated force through road, rail, sea, and bridge logistics [9][10]. He defines strategic air power's proper role as "systems destruction": sufficiently accurate, survivable, and destructive air-power can disable the behind-the-lines systems that underpin an opponent's warfighting power [36].

# Analysis

## Analytical Framework

O'Brien traces explanatory application from technological change to how military force should be used and how political leadership can end a war. A Soviet idea from the mid-1970s, reframed by Andy Marshall as a Revolution in Military Affairs, describes a wide hardware-and-software technological leap that is producing an unstoppable "de-humanizing" or "de-populating" of the battlefield, drastically reducing humans in direct combat and changing how wars start, are waged, their ethics, and how they are decided; O'Brien calls it "the greatest revolution in military affairs since the development of metal weapons." See [Revolution in Military Affairs]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/revolution-in-military-affairs.html) and [Global Depopulation]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-depopulation.html) [15].

O'Brien distinguishes long-range strike, which seeks to limit or destroy an opponent's ability to generate new military force through strategic bombing of targets such as oil refineries and military production, from medium-range strike, which seeks to disrupt or destroy deployment of already generated force through logistics targets including road, rail, sea, and bridge traffic [10].

O'Brien argues that the proper role of strategic air power is "systems destruction": if a state has sufficient mass of accurate, survivable, and sufficiently destructive air-power technologies, it can disable systems behind the battle lines that underpin an opponent's warfighting power; he says the US campaign against Iran began with a political decapitation attempt that was "just not a very well planned out one" and switched to signalling, while shutting down Russia's ability to refine and transport fuel would devastate its war machine but would require ruthless concentration and repeat attacks, and the Wildberries campaign is signalling rather than a way to win the war [36].

Applied to the [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html), O'Brien assumes neither side will conquer the other and install a new government, identifies internal dissension in either [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) or [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html) as a definite possibility that could produce leadership change and end the war or at least a ceasefire, and holds that at no point have both leaderships determined that ending the war is preferable to continuing it in the hope of improving their position; ending the fighting requires both [Putin]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) and [Zelensky]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html) to decide on a war-ending scenario they can both live with [21].

## US Decline Across Domains

O'Brien argues that the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) revealed a US decline that was faster and broader than expected, stretching from military weakness through diplomatic failings to societal divisions: the military performed poorly in planning and execution, the US alienated its European, Japanese, and South Korean allies and demonstrated its unreliability to Gulf states, and the war produced no rally-round-the-flag effect but exposed a partisan death match [1].

Against a military culture that treats bravery as decisive, O'Brien says that the Japanese soldier was, in his view, the bravest in World War II yet lost, while Italian soldiers were enormously brave despite fighting in substandard, poorly supplied equipment; US and British soldiers had by far the highest chance of surviving the war intact, and wars are usually won by the smartest and most adaptable soldiers, who believe they are neither sacrificed for nothing nor inadequately equipped or looked after [19].

He applies that diagnosis to the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) under Secretary Hegseth: the Department of War's "High-T Department of War" announcement equated world-class warfighters with testosterone levels and offered screening and enhancements, although testosterone boosting is strongly linked to infertility and might reduce the possibility of future US soldiers; Hegseth's longstanding emphasis on hyper-masculine bravery included arguing on Megyn Kelly's 2024 podcast that American youth needed to show more bravery and using an E. B. Sledge quote to stress bravery's importance. O'Brien argues that the military instead stresses romantically destructive notions of sacrifice rather than the education, technological advancement, and thoughtfulness in which American soldiers have thrived, and is being set up for failure. See [Revolution in Military Affairs]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/revolution-in-military-affairs.html) and [Defense Industrial Resilience]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/defense-industrial-resilience.html) [19].

Institutionally, O'Brien argues that the US must completely reconstruct its understanding of war, its required systems mix, and the industrial base supporting it, because it was and remains equipped for short wars and glamorous missions; even with the best weapons, mix, and doctrine, it would struggle while toadies and sycophants are promoted, good officers keep their heads down or are pushed aside, and it remains an intellectually terrified institution that would make a war difficult to fight. Yet its technology, economy, entrepreneurship, and capacity to recreate an industrial base remain, so this is not Rome in 409 AD; Trump's War has, perversely, ripped the band-aid off US military complacency by making the weakness of its military position and culture impossible to conceal with flags and chest-thumping [28]. O'Brien also argues that autonomous systems are the future of war but warns against extreme boasts that make the transition seem easier than it will be; after the July escalation, he says US targeting and warfighting against Iran represented "one of the worst performances in the history of warfare" [30].

Fiscal constraints may prevent the US from escalating or lengthening the war even if Trump wanted to: national debt exceeds 120% of GDP, roughly its World War II endpoint, interest rates are relatively high, and the US is already struggling to sell debt before the war's increased costs are included [27]. Estimates put debt service at $2.1 trillion by 2036 even without a longer, more expensive war; O'Brien describes the [US yen intervention]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/us-japan-joint-yen-intervention-2026-08-03.html) as a worried act of self-interest to prevent [Japan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/japan.html) from selling Treasuries, contrasts the administration's claimed $37.5 billion war cost with the Defense Department's $67.1 billion supplemental request, and notes that Democrats have begun, in his view wisely, to balk at further defense funding [27].

## Ukraine: From Operations to Manipulation

O'Brien tracks the [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html) through Ukrainian long-range strikes, usually associated with attacks on Russian oil production [10], and an accelerating campaign to try to isolate [Crimea]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/crimea-isolation-campaign.html) by sinking or damaging Russian-controlled shipping in the Sea of Azov [17], while arguing that the ranged war is arguably at a hazy stage comparable to April or May 1943, nearer its beginning, and that both Ukrainian and Russian production retain scope to increase [9].

Using air and sea drones, Ukraine conducted strikes against Russian-controlled vessels in the [Sea of Azov campaign]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/sea-of-azov-campaign.html); through July 11, Ukrainians claimed attacks on 28 vessels, and it was claimed that Russia barred new Kerch Strait traffic on July 10 [17].

Ukraine's drone-and-cruise-missile counterstrike on [Moscow]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/moscow-drone-strike.html) showed its air-defense rings were thoroughly compromised, while O'Brien writes that how Moscow's exposure to future attacks will alter Russian public opinion is "difficult to judge" under censorship; he also notes that [India]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/india.html) is "gorging" on Russian oil, Trump's Iran war had generated an extra $15 billion for Russia by May, and there is no sign of a more effective Russian interception system or greatly increased anti-air production [11][14].

O'Brien writes that democracies often prepare inefficiently but "can also be better at adapting and channelling societal abilities and commitment"; he describes [Drapatyi]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/mykhailo-drapatyi.html) as a career soldier since 2014 who reportedly commanded the BMP that broke Mariupol barricades, became the youngest Ground Forces commander in 2024, resigned after a training-centre strike, and offered "no loud promises," gives a conditional mixed view of the Wildberries campaign and guesses it targets Putin's political standing, reports a claim of 13 further vessel strikes in the [Sea of Azov campaign]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/sea-of-azov-campaign.html), and says Russia has "so far" found no effective counter, while cautioning that if Zelensky retains acting Defense Minister Evgeniy Khmara, "we must still wait and see" [22].

By August 2, O'Brien called his April assertion that Ukraine understood the United States was not on its side "one of my big mistakes of the year," writing that Ukrainian hope in the United States had returned and that press accounts of a Patriot license, support for the Graham bill, and pressure on Russia were untrue, since Trump said he might not permit a Patriot license and the procedural Graham bill was about increasing his tariff powers rather than sanctioning Russia [26].

O'Brien contrasts a mainstream account of a genuine July pivot with his view that Trump never changed his fundamental beliefs and maneuvered for political benefit and to help Putin, reporting one interception from 27 ballistic missiles and at least 10 Ukrainians killed in one attack and nine the next night, declaring that "hope kills, literally," and describing flattery as a strategy "bound to fail"; see the [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html), [Ukraine Air Defense Crisis]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/ukraine-air-defense-crisis-2026.html), and [Fedorov Dismissal]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/fedorov-dismissal-2026.html) [26][23].

By mid-August, O'Brien writes that continuing small Patriot supplies gives the United States leverage to shape Ukraine's war and European preparation, with a small number of interceptors supplied through Europe forcing Ukraine to "dance more to the US tune"; he reports that Vance asked Zelensky to halt campaigns on non-Russian tankers using Novorossiysk and much of its infrastructure, that Ukraine subsequently did not strike tankers near the CPC terminal, and that this "brutal rationing" gives the United States leverage that can be used to do Putin a massive favor, while cutting supplies off entirely "would actually" have been worse for Russia [29].

## US-Iran War Assessment

### Initial Degradation

O'Brien identified three early lessons: US military weakness, including poor planning and execution, failure to protect facilities, and failure to achieve meaningful strategic objectives; diplomatic failures, with European allies thrown under the bus and Gulf states seeing US unreliability, while the idea of the US as an irresistible force was possibly gone for good; and societal division, with no rally-round-the-flag effect and a fully locked partisan death match [1].

He argued that cheap mass had gained an advantage over expensive small batches because the US used costly Patriot and THAAD systems against cheap Iranian drones and ballistic missiles, a mistake that meant it could not fight for long [1].

He also argued that wars are almost always choices made by individual leaders rather than state actors acting in the national interest: [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) faced no political pressure to attack [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html), but chose to start a war for personal gains, probably including money, that was in no way in the US national interest [1].

Iranian drones penetrated US defenses, killing at least six soldiers in a strike on a Kuwait command facility; an unnamed military official told CNN, "We basically had no drone defeat capability," while a lone drone destroyed a Fifth Fleet AN/TPS-59 radar in Bahrain after perhaps $30,000 of Iranian equipment struck hardware estimated to be worth tens of millions of dollars [6].

O'Brien compared this deterioration to Roman imperial decline, when the army still looked formidable but was no longer as advanced relative to its enemies, while three F-15E aircraft were shot down in a single friendly-fire incident, a Congressional Research Office list reported 17 advanced aircraft lost or damaged, bases were extensively hit with damage that would cost billions to fix, and in approximately six weeks the US burned through years, and in some cases decades, of military production [6][7].

He wrote that Iran seemed to be stringing the administration along while planning possible Hormuz tolls, musing about a nuclear weapon, and rebuilding ballistic-missile and air-defense capabilities; he warned that assembling a master list of foreign intelligence sources could blow up US intelligence collection for a generation [12].

Gallup reported Iran-action approval of 79% among Republicans, 26% among independents, and 8% among Democrats; O'Brien argued that the US had balkanized into two camps [13].

### Resumed-War Conditions

O'Brien wrote that Trump's war reignited after his [MOU/ceasefire]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/iran-ceasefire-mou.html) was revealed as a sham: Iran gave up nothing, and Trump faced either admitting a strategic defeat or reigniting military operations in hopes Iran would become more malleable [18].

His likelihood table gave 99% for no meaningful Iranian concession, 98.5% for [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) and [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) benefiting significantly as depleted US stocks meant no new Patriots for [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html) and a weaker Taiwan deterrent while higher oil prices aided Russian revenue, 90% that oil markets believed Trump would "TACO," 80% that ground-troop threats were hot air, 66% that Trump needed a deal by the midterms, and 99.9% that the US was haemorrhaging allies and partners [18].

O'Brien juxtaposed Trump's July 11 threat to decimate Iran after an assassination attempt with his argument that the US had pursued and legitimized leader assassination, making Trump a legitimate Iranian target; he characterized Colby as panicking, insulting Europeans while pleading with them not to pursue strategic autonomy [18].

He called the conflict Donald Trump's War and said its stability was that of Trump's personality: the [ceasefire]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/hormuz-ceasefire-collapse-2026.html) had completely broken down after four days of constant bombardment, but no one would be surprised if it instantly reasserted itself, while polls put opposition to the war at 60-66% [18].

### Strategic Self-Defeat

O'Brien called the US-Iran War a war of choice "to defeat itself": Trump seemed to hope attacks would produce Iranian terms on the nuclear issue and reopening Hormuz without tolls, but taking Kharg Island achieved nothing toward a full-scale invasion, taking Tehran, and installing a new government, which McCaffrey estimated would require 600,000 troops and a year and which O'Brien said was not happening [20].

He wrote that many Iranian attacks focused on the resources of US allies, including [Qatar]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/qatar.html), the [UAE]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/uae.html), [Kuwait]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/kuwait.html), [Jordan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/jordan.html), [Oman]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/oman.html), and [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html), showing Iran could strike them regardless of US protection; an anonymous official said, "We do not have enough to safely sustain operations, and I don't think the White House is aware of that," while estimated THAAD expenditure was 190-290 interceptors, or roughly 53-80% of the stockpile, and O'Brien described Congress as inert while the administration held no press conferences [20].

The munitions depletion led O'Brien to doubt the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) could even fight the opening battles of a China war: in best-case scenarios, roughly 1,500 combined Patriot and THAAD interceptors, at two interceptors per target, might intercept 1,000 Chinese systems before running out, although the real number would be much lower against a Chinese arsenal of somewhere around 3,500 missiles; Guam and other Marianas bases could be devastated at the outset, making a short-war scenario plausible [20].

Only the UAE seemed to have doubled down on US support, O'Brien wrote, while other Gulf states had serious doubts about US reliability; an investor he quoted identified exacerbated GCC fissures, diluted US influence as America's security guarantee appeared weaker, a divided GCC strengthening Iran, and China positioned to capitalize, with implications for [great-power competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html) and [defense industrial resilience]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/defense-industrial-resilience.html) [20].

### Iranian Calculus

O'Brien wrote that Iranian strategy seems to fit an Aims/Ways/Means format: ideally controlling Hormuz, weakening the US regional presence, restarting the nuclear program, and breaking up the US-led coalition; continuing to fight, denuding US stocks, showing US weakness to allies, maintaining oil prices, delaying talks, and escalating when necessary; and using existing missiles and cheap drones supplemented by Chinese and Russian deliveries and intelligence aid, sustainably with present resources [25].

He wrote that Iran seemed to calculate that time was on its side and that approaching the election would make Trump more desperate for a deal; White House sources confirmed that the US had not prepared and would soon struggle to protect its troops and allies, O'Brien called Waltz's contradictory statements tragi-comic, and after Trump stopped military operations Iran continued attacks directly or through proxies [25].

The likely shape of peace, in O'Brien's projection, is an Iranian statement that it has no intention of developing nuclear weapons, with few actual checks; tacit control of Hormuz through possible "administrative fees"; assets unfrozen; sanctions wound down; and Trump declaring victory, which O'Brien called "the quickest and arguably the most decisive defeat in US history" [25].

### External Exploitation

O'Brien compared US vulnerability to 1861, when outside powers directly threatened the United States and it had to accept this until becoming more assertive in 1863-64; unlike then, he wrote, the US is not on an upward trajectory and China seems to grasp that, while Trump had to a large degree created the present US weakness [24].

O'Brien wrote that China seemed to grasp US vulnerability and warned that the expected [transfer of up to 400 Chinese-made shoulder-fired air-defense launchers]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/china-manpads-transfer-iran-2026.html) could be a game changer: Trump might have to escalate before their arrival or admit he could not afford to escalate [24].

## It Is All One Fight: The Authoritarian Coalition

O'Brien argues that figures who take one acceptable position, such as supporting Ukraine, while maintaining authoritarian, racist, and conspiracist commitments should not be praised or normalized: [Laura Loomer]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/laura-loomer.html), Tommy Robinson, Trump, Viktor Orban, Robert Fico, Nigel Farage, and Tucker Carlson are, despite occasional disagreements, trying to destroy civil society in the USA and [Europe]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/europe.html) and replace it with an authoritarian, anti-liberal, discriminatory state; their similarities outweigh their differences on specific issues. See [Political Normalization]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/political-normalization.html) [23].

O'Brien presents Trump's pro-Ukraine and anti-Russia statements from January through October 2025 as examples of verbal pivots that gave him time and breathing space, reduced the pressure to help Ukraine, and gave Putin flexibility to escalate and expand the war; more than a year and a half into Trump's second term, all US aid to Ukraine has stopped, Ukraine is basically running out of Patriots, Russia is selling whatever oil it can ship, and Ukraine has no effective defense from Russian ballistic missiles; Trump is entirely self-interested and adjusts only when he understands it is in his interest, so his recent rhetoric on Ukraine reflects his recognition that Ukraine has cards rather than a change in outlook; a Graham-Blumenthal sanctions bill might not receive a vote, and even if it passes Congress Trump is under no obligation to impose sanctions, while his proposal to add Iran was, O'Brien argues, intended to muddy the waters and slow the process, a dynamic [Laura Loomer]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/laura-loomer.html) misread as greater pro-Ukraine commitment [23].

O'Brien's premise is that Europe has gained more by disregarding the United States and helping Ukraine fight its war than by flattering, praising, or trying to appeal to Trump's supposed better nature, a strategy he says is bound to fail; see [Laura Loomer Ukraine Visit]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/laura-loomer-ukraine-visit-2026.html) and [Political Normalization]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/political-normalization.html) [23].

## Posture Toward NATO

O'Brien calls the [NATO Ankara Summit]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/nato-ankara-summit-2026.html) "the worst summit in [NATO]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/organizations/nato.html) history," citing the absence of a US commitment to conventional European defense and characterizing Secretary Hegseth's six-month force-posture review as a threat; arguing that a nuclear umbrella without conventional forces is not credible; judging the Patriot-license pledge for [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html) hollow because its details and manufacturers' involvement were unclear and it would probably take years to produce at a reasonable rate; noting that the year's statement did not mention the 2027 meeting and that there might not be another leaders' summit while Trump is president; and pointing to Trump's three explicit threats against [Greenland]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/trump-greenland-crisis.html) over the three days before, during, and after the summit, alongside his claims of caring, human affection, and "tremendous love in that room," which O'Brien identifies as a reliable tell that he is lying [16].
