# Background

Phillips P. O'Brien is a military historian and analyst whose work challenges realist assumptions about war and state behavior. His central analytical premise holds that "battles rarely determine anything; they reveal" — the outcome of a war is shaped by structural factors such as industrial capacity, energy throughput, and alliance depth, which battles expose rather than create. This inverts the conventional focus on operational turning points and redirects attention to the underlying power base.

A second pillar of his framework is that wars are choices of individual leaders, not rational state actors pursuing national interest. He challenges realism directly, arguing that the unitary rational actor model obscures the most important variable: who is making the decision and why. This leader-centric approach extends to war termination: wars end when both leaderships independently conclude that peace is preferable to continued fighting, with leadership change — by death, overthrow, or political exit — as the principal mechanism. He rejects escalation-ladder theory as "simple nonsense," holding that escalatory behavior is a function of specific leaderships, not a generic strategic logic that can be modeled in advance. [21]

On military technology, O'Brien argues that cheap mass systems have greater advantage than expensive small-batch precision systems, and that industrial capacity and sustainment matter more than technological sophistication at the margin. He holds that the Revolution in Military Affairs is real and unstoppable, its central direction the "de-humanizing" or "de-populating" of the battlefield — drastically reducing humans in direct combat through autonomous systems and component miniaturization. [15]

His ranged-war doctrine distinguishes long-range strike (strategic bombing of industrial base) from medium-range strike (logistics disruption of supply lines and depots), arguing that what each side chooses to attack reveals war aims and capabilities. [9][10]

He publishes detailed real-time tracking of the [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html) and the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html), combining historical framework with operational assessment.

## Revolution in Military Affairs

O'Brien argues that the RMA debate — running since the Soviet Military-Technical Revolution of the 1970s, through Andy Marshall's Pentagon reframing, the Gulf War popularization, and the War on Terror detour — is, in O'Brien's view, resolved by the evidence of the Ukraine war. He argues the RMA is real and unstoppable, though skeptics like Kofman still dissent. Its central direction is the **"de-humanizing" or "de-populating" of the battlefield**: drastically reducing the number of humans in direct combat through autonomous systems (software) and component miniaturization (hardware — small sensors/cameras, solid-state batteries). This will transform how wars start (less human cost calculation), how they are fought (fewer humans in the loop), their ethics, and how they are decided. Legacy arms manufacturers (e.g., Rheinmetall CEO Papperger) dismiss the change by isolating it to the drone variable — a "cute intellectual trick" that ignores the broad technological leap. O'Brien calls it the greatest revolution since the development of metal weapons. See [Revolution in Military Affairs]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/revolution-in-military-affairs.html) [15].

## US Decline Across Domains

O'Brien's US decline thesis begins as a general claim and then extends domain by domain — from the empirical record of the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html), through military culture, to the material condition of the armed forces, and finally to the fiscal ceiling on escalation.

The war provided empirical confirmation: military planning failures, diplomatic alienation of allies, and the absence of domestic political consolidation — no rally-round-the-flag effect — all pointed to a superpower whose decline is compounding, not gradual [1].

The cultural dimension runs through O'Brien's challenge to one of the most romanticized assumptions in military thought: that bravery is a decisive warfighting virtue. The historical record, he argues, demonstrates the opposite. The bravest soldiers of World War II — the Japanese — lost. Italian soldiers were demonstrably brave yet underequipped, and their courage did not compensate for material deficiency. US and British soldiers, who enjoyed the highest survival rates of the war, prevailed not through superior valor but through superior logistics, technology, and adaptability. Wars are usually won by the smartest, most adaptable, best-equipped soldiers who believe they are not being sacrificed for nothing [19].

The framework has immediate diagnostic application to the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) under Secretary Hegseth. The Department of War's announcement of testosterone screening for soldiers — what O'Brien calls the "High-T Department of War" — equates warfighting capacity with testosterone levels, despite the medical reality that testosterone boosting is linked to infertility. Hegseth has long emphasized hyper-masculine bravery as a military virtue, citing E.B. Sledge's Pacific campaign accounts and praising the "brave" on the Kelly podcast in 2024. O'Brien reads this as a symptom of an institution stressing all the wrong things — romantic sacrifice narratives instead of the education, technological sophistication, and thoughtfulness that actually correlate with battlefield success. The US military, on this analysis, is culturally preparing for failure by celebrating the qualities that do not win wars while neglecting those that do [19]. See [Revolution in Military Affairs]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/revolution-in-military-affairs.html) and [Defense Industrial Resilience]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/defense-industrial-resilience.html).

The military's material condition extends the decline thesis from diplomatic and societal dimensions to the institution itself. The US military does not need stock replenishment but complete reconstruction: even with full pre-war inventories, it was equipped for short wars and glamorous missions, not sustained conflict [28]. The cultural damage compounds the structural problem — toadies and sycophants get promoted while good officers keep their heads down, watch what they say, or even get pushed aside; intellectually, the US military is a terrified institution today, and that alone would make a war difficult to fight [28]. Yet US fundamentals — technology, economy, entrepreneurship — remain present; this is not, for instance, Rome in 409AD, which even with great effort could not have created the forces needed to save itself. Trump's War has perversely ripped the band-aid off of US military complacency: the true weakness of America's military position can no longer be hidden behind waving flags and chest-thumping [28]. The transition to autonomous war-fighting is real but over-hyped; US targeting and warfighting against Iran represents, in O'Brien's words, "one of the worst performances in the history of warfare" [30].

The fiscal dimension imposes the hardest ceiling. O'Brien argues the US cannot afford to escalate or fight a long war even if it wanted to [27]. National debt exceeds 120% of GDP — about the same as at the end of World War II — interest rates are relatively high, and the US is already struggling to sell debt before the increased costs of this war have been figured in [27]. The Congressional Budget Office projects $2.1 trillion to service the debt by 2036 without a longer war, while the administration's low-balled war-cost figures — $37.5 billion claimed against a $67.1 billion supplemental request — reveal a fiscal ceiling on escalation [27]. The US yen intervention was self-interested: the Trump administration was desperate to keep the Japanese government from selling US Treasury bonds to defend the yen independently — no act of beneficence, but a worried act of self-interest [27]. Democrats have begun blocking defense appropriations, a break in wartime funding norms that would have been risky before [27].

## Ranged War Doctrine

O'Brien distinguishes **long-range strike** (strategic bombing — oil refineries, weapons production, industrial base) from **medium-range strike** (logistics disruption — road, rail, sea/bridge traffic) [9][10].

## How Wars End: Leadership Decides

O'Brien's war-termination framework begins by inverting the analytical sequence most commentators follow. Rather than starting with military operations and projecting forward to a war's conclusion, he starts with the endpoint — the political decision to stop fighting — and works backward to the factors that produce it. The relationship between battles and the ends of wars, he argues, is "extremely unstable," with military matters often mattering "far less and operating more indirectly than people believe." This extends his "battles reveal, they do not determine" thesis into the domain of war termination: the same flawed instinct that leads analysts to draw straight lines from military operations to victory also leads them to misunderstand how wars end.

The framework's central variable is leadership. Wars end when the political leaderships of both sides determine that ending the fighting is preferable to continuing it in the hope of improving their position — always a two-way street. In the most extreme case, that street is traversed by military conquest and the installation of a compliant leadership, as with Germany in 1945. But O'Brien notes even this mode of termination often fails eventually, citing the US experiences in Iraq and Afghanistan. Short of total conquest, the question becomes what drives a leadership to conclude that peace is better than continued war.

O'Brien identifies two principal mechanisms. The first is **change the leader**: a leader wedded to a war dies, is overthrown, or exits through political means, and the trajectory shifts under a successor with a different calculus — as when the death of the Empress Elizabeth of Russia in 1762 saved Frederick the Great's Prussia, or when Attila the Hun's sudden death in 453 CE collapsed the Hunnic expansion. The change can be entirely unconnected to battlefield events. The second is **make the leader believe it is worse to keep fighting than make peace**, a calculation shaped by military, social-political, economic, and personal-survival factors operating independently or in combination. Germany's leadership in late 1918 lost faith in its army; the US in Vietnam and Afghanistan lost the domestic political will to continue; Britain in the American Revolution judged the economic cost prohibitive despite holding major cities; and Emperor Hirohito in August 1945 concluded that surrender better served his personal survival and throne than continued war.

A critical component of this framework is O'Brien's direct critique of escalation-ladder theory. The idea that states move up and down an "escalation ladder" in some understood fashion is, in his words, "simple nonsense." Different leaderships can blow through the ladder in one step or never go up it at all; there is no way to predict a state's behavior by achieving "escalation dominance." Some leaders will not care and will keep fighting; others will fold long before dominance is achieved; still others will continue escalating regardless. This critique reinforces his broader rejection of assumptions of uniform leadership and state behavior: the escalatory behavior of a state is a function of its specific leadership, not something that can be predicted by imagining that states behave similarly.

Applied to the [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html), the framework yields a clear diagnostic: at no point have both leaderships determined that ending the war is preferable to continuing it. O'Brien excludes the scenario of either side conquering the other and installing a new government, but identifies internal dissension in either [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) or [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html) as a plausible path to leadership change and war termination. Absent that, the end requires both [Putin]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) and [Zelensky]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html) to independently conclude that a settlement is better than continued fighting — a condition that has not yet been met [21].

Tactical disagreements among them (e.g., on Ukraine) mask fundamental ideological alignment. See [Political Normalization]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/political-normalization.html) [23].

## Ukraine: From Operations to Manipulation

O'Brien's Ukraine assessment moves from granular operational tracking — Crimea isolation, long-range strikes, Sea of Azov drone warfare — to the diagnosis of an analytical trap (Trump's deceptive July pivot) and finally to the mechanism by which the US controls Ukraine through Patriot interceptor rationing.

He tracks the [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html) along two operational threads: long-range strikes extending deep into Russian industrial territory [9][10], and the [Crimea isolation campaign]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/crimea-isolation-campaign.html) — turning the peninsula from a Russian asset into a liability through strikes on Russian-controlled shipping in the Sea of Azov [17]. He argues the ranged war is still in its early stages, comparable to mid-1943 in WWII, with both sides capable of significantly increasing production [9].

Within the Crimea thread, he documents the under-covered [Sea of Azov campaign]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/sea-of-azov-campaign.html) — Ukraine using air and sea drones to attack 28 Russian-controlled vessels and force the Kerch Strait closure, a strategically significant extension of the isolation campaign [17]. He pairs this with the dramatic escalation in Russia's ballistic missile campaign: launches roughly tripled year-over-year (28→70+/month), Ukraine intercepting only 40% of ballistic missiles vs 89% of all threats, Patriot interceptors depleted. Trump's Patriot license pledge, in his reading, gifts Putin 1–3 winters of unrestricted bombardment. See [Ukraine Air Defense Crisis 2026]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/ukraine-air-defense-crisis-2026.html) [17].

O'Brien frames Ukraine's May 2026 [drone strike on Moscow]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/moscow-drone-strike.html) as Russia's Marianas moment — the point at which the propaganda narrative collapsed because the war reached the homeland, comparing it to Japan's loss of the Marianas in WWII. With Russia's 1M+ cumulative casualties and compromised Moscow air defenses, Putin can no longer hide the war's cost from the Russian public, though how this alters public opinion is "difficult to judge" under censorship [11]. On the Russian economic side, he documents (via Kiel Institute and CSIS data) that India is "gorging" on Russian oil despite sanctions, that Trump's Iran war generated an extra $15 billion for Russia while destroying the credibility of US sanctions, and that the adaptation cycle for long-range strike and air interception is much longer than commonly assumed — there is no sign of a Russian counter to Ukraine's strike campaign [14].

Weekend Update #195 frames the Drapatyi command change as an illustration of democracy's wartime advantage — democracies start slowly and prepare inefficiently, but "can also be better at adapting and channelling societal abilities and commitment" [22]. He profiles Drapatyi as a career soldier since 2014 who reportedly "commanded the BMP who broke through the Russian barricades" at Mariupol, Ukraine's youngest Ground Forces commander, who resigned over accountability after a training-centre strike and offered "no loud promises" [22]. He gives a mixed assessment of the new Wildberries warehouse strike campaign — possibly distracting from the refinery campaign, likely aimed at Putin's political bargain — and updates the [Sea of Azov campaign]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/sea-of-azov-campaign.html) (13 more vessels claimed, no Russian counter). He judges that if Zelensky keeps Fedorov permanently out, the crisis has not produced the best outcome [22].

By August 2, O'Brien's focus shifts from operations to the analytical trap that caught him. He identifies his April 2026 Atlantic article — "Ukraine Has Finally Given Up on Trump" — as his biggest analytical mistake of the year: Ukrainian hope never extinguished and came roaring back in July when Trump briefly adopted a pro-Ukraine posture [26]. Six major US press headlines between July 8–13 reported Trump's supposed pivot (Patriot production, Graham sanctions, praising Zelensky). None was true — Trump never agreed to a Patriot license, never backed the sanctions bill, and never pressured Russia [26]. By July 31, Trump said he might not let Ukraine have Patriots — Ukrainians "can't be trusted." The Senate's bastardized Graham bill increases Trump's tariff powers rather than sanctioning Russia [26].

O'Brien offers two interpretations: the mainstream press version (Trump honestly pivoted, then reverted) versus his own — Trump never changed his fundamental beliefs; he maneuvered for political benefit and to help Putin. The result: 1 of 27 ballistic missiles intercepted, 19 Ukrainians killed in two days, and another month of unrestricted Russian bombardment [26]. He is "most angry at the sloppy, delusional, and frankly moronic western press and analytical community that refuses to admit what Trump is and is doing" [26]. The framework's prescription: hope in Trump kills — literally — and the strategy of flattering Trump has demonstrably failed for 18 months [26][23]. See [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html), [Ukraine Air Defense Crisis]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/ukraine-air-defense-crisis-2026.html), and [Fedorov Dismissal]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/fedorov-dismissal-2026.html).

The mechanism behind the manipulation comes into focus by mid-August. O'Brien documents how the US uses Patriot interceptor rationing as leverage over Ukraine: by keeping Ukraine dependent on small monthly Patriot supplies, the Trump administration forces concessions — including halting Ukraine's effective Black Sea oil-interdiction campaign at Novorossiysk, a major favor to Putin [29]. The Patriot supply is "brutally efficient" as a control instrument: cutting it off entirely would actually be worse for Russia, so the US maintains just enough to keep Ukraine bending to its tune while slowing European development of independent air defense [29].

## US-Iran War Assessment

O'Brien's reading of the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) deepened over the summer of 2026 from an initial catalogue of quantitative degradation into a structural diagnosis of self-defeat, then to a reconstruction of Iran's strategic calculus, and finally to an assessment of [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) exploiting US vulnerability in real time.

### Initial Lessons and Quantitative Degradation

O'Brien headlined three early lessons from the war. First, the US is much weaker than understood — encompassing military weakness (poor planning and execution, failure to protect facilities, inability to achieve strategic objectives), diplomatic failings (European allies thrown under the bus, Gulf states seeing US unreliability, the idea of the US as an irresistible force possibly gone for good), and societal division (no rally-round-the-flag effect, a fully locked partisan death match) [1]. Second, the advantage of cheap mass is greater than previously understood — the US relied on pricey Patriots and THAAD to engage cheap Iranian drones and ballistic missiles, a costly mistake that limited how long it could fight [1]. Third, wars are the choices of individual leaders, not rational state actors — [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) faced no political pressure to attack [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) and did so for personal reasons in no way in the US national interest [1].

He catalogued the quantitative degradation in detail: Iranian drones penetrating US defenses (at least six soldiers killed in a strike on a command facility in Kuwait, an unnamed military official telling CNN "we basically had no drone defeat capability"), three F-15E aircraft shot down in a single friendly-fire incident over Kuwait, and a lone Iranian drone destroying a Fifth Fleet AN/TPS-59 radar in Bahrain with perhaps $30,000 of equipment — a dynamic he compares to Roman imperial decline, when the army still looked formidable but was no longer as advanced relative to its enemies [6]. Seventeen advanced aircraft were lost or damaged (fighters, AWACS, specialized SF helicopters, KC-135 tankers), confirmed by a Congressional Research Office list; bases were hit extensively with damage costing billions to fix; and a CSIS report confirmed munitions depletion — in approximately six weeks of fighting, the US burned through years, in some cases decades, of military production [7].

He now argues US weakness against Iran is total — Tehran strings Washington along, openly plans tolls in the Strait of Hormuz, muses about procuring a nuclear weapon, and rebuilds military capabilities in ballistic missiles and air defense — while mocking analysts who claim wars like Iran's can be won quickly and easily [12]. He warns that the Trump administration's plan to compile a master list of all foreign intelligence sources could be catastrophically destructive, serving only to sell or compromise information [12]. Using Gallup polling data, he argues America has balkanized into two partisan camps where loyalty to party determines views of war and America's global role — Republicans approved the Iran action at 79%, independents at 26%, Democrats at 8% — a loss of shared national identity he finds deeply destructive [13].

### Trump's War: Consequence-Free World

His Midweek Update #19 (2026-07-16) frames the reignition of the war as the inevitable consequence of a "consequence-free world" the US chose to inhabit [18]. Trump's fantasy [MOU/ceasefire]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/iran-ceasefire-mou.html) was revealed to be a sham — Iran gave up nothing in it, and when the truth inevitably dawned that Iran had no intention of making concessions on its nuclear program or its control of the Strait of Hormuz, Trump faced a binary choice: admit the greatest strategic defeat in US history or restart the bombing. He chose the latter [18].

O'Brien constructs a likelihood table: 99% that Iran has made no meaningful concession; 98.5% that [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) and China are both benefitting significantly (the US blowing through depleted stockpiles means no new Patriots for [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html) and a weaker Taiwan deterrent; rising oil prices boost Russian revenue); 90% that oil markets still believe Trump will "TACO" (Trump Always Chickens Out); 80% that ground-troop threats are hot air; 66% that Trump still needs a deal by the midterms; and 99.9% that the US is haemorrhaging allies and partners [18].

He juxtaposes Trump's July 11 tweet threatening to "completely decimate and destroy all areas of Iran" should Iran attempt to assassinate him, against Trump's own celebration of the assassination of Khamenei at the start of the war [18]. The US pursued and legitimized a policy of leader assassination; the same policy now makes Trump a legitimate target for Iran [18]. Undersecretary of State Colby's parallel tweet thread — insisting that a "collective middle powers strategy" is a distraction and that "access to the American DIB is a privilege, not a right" — is read by O'Brien as panic: the administration is realizing that its own Europe-withdrawal policies have produced the strategic autonomy it now pleads with Europeans not to pursue [18]. The insult-then-plea structure destroys its own case [18].

The war is Donald Trump's War — its stability equals the stability of Trump's personality (i.e., very little); the [ceasefire]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/hormuz-ceasefire-collapse-2026.html) could break down for four days and reassert itself today, and no one could be surprised [18]. O'Brien catalogs five consecutive days of US strikes (July 11–16) with Iran's parallel responses, characterizing the campaign as an extraordinary gamble: restarting the bombing in a weaker strategic position than when operations ended in April, with the midterm elections approaching and 60–66% of Americans opposing the war [18].

### Each Day The US Weakens Itself: The Self-Defeating War

His Midweek Update #20 (2026-07-22) crystallizes a synthesized diagnostic: the US-Iran War is a war of choice that is defeating the United States through its own continuation [20]. There is no achievable objective — Trump is hoping Iran will offer peace terms (a nuclear concession plus the reopening of Hormuz without tolls) that no military operation can produce [20]. Taking Kharg Island achieves nothing towards this [20]. The only military path to the political objective is a full-scale invasion and regime change, which General McCaffrey estimates would require 600,000 troops and one year — a commitment that is not happening [20]. Every day of strikes thus operates without a coherent theory of how military action produces the desired political outcome [20].

Iran's "sideways escalation" strategy compounds the political cost: rather than attacking only US forces, Iran systematically strikes the infrastructure of US-allied Gulf states — [Qatar]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/qatar.html), the [UAE]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/uae.html), [Kuwait]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/kuwait.html), [Jordan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/jordan.html), [Oman]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/oman.html), and [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html) — from July 12 through July 21, demonstrating that the US cannot protect its allies regardless of US protection [20]. Every day of operations depletes US munitions stockpiles of vital weapons — THAAD and Patriot interceptors in particular [20]. An anonymous US official told the Washington Post: "We do not have enough to safely sustain operations, and I don't think the White House is aware of that" [20]. THAAD stocks were already critically low before the latest round of strikes; an estimated 190–290, or roughly 53–80% of the stockpile, had been expended by the April ceasefire [20]. The Pentagon is panicking, seeking billions from a Congress that remains inert — the War Powers Act has not been invoked, no press briefings are being held, and CNN reported on Pentagon suppression of press coverage [20].

The munitions depletion has degraded US military readiness to the point that O'Brien now doubts the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) could win even the opening battles of a China war [20]. Combined Patriot and THAAD inventories stand at approximately 1,500 interceptors; with a two-per-target doctrine, that yields perhaps 1,000 intercepts against a Chinese arsenal of roughly 3,500 missiles — and the real number would be much lower [20]. US bases in Guam and the Marianas could be devastated at the start of a Pacific conflict [20]. A "short-war scenario" — in which the US runs out of systems and suffers such high losses it cannot continue — has become plausible [20].

The war is demonstrating to the world that America's security guarantee is weaker than once thought [20]. GCC fissures are widening: only the UAE is doubling down on US support, while the rest of the Gulf states are having serious doubts [20]. An investor quoted by O'Brien identified the cascading consequences: "exacerbation of pre-existing political fissures in the GCC, dilution of US influence, America's security guarantee weaker than once thought, divided GCC strengthens Iran, China positioned to capitalize" [20]. A war of choice, with no achievable objective, that depletes the US military on a daily basis, demonstrates the hollowness of US security guarantees to allies, and degrades readiness for the primary [great-power competitor]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html) — this is the defining strategic self-defeat of the Trump era, with implications for [defense industrial resilience]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/defense-industrial-resilience.html) [20].

### Iran Is Toying With the USA: The Strategic Framework

His Midweek Update #21 (2026-08-03) provides the most structured account of Iran's strategic calculus, reconstructed in Aims/Ways/Means format [25]. Aims: control over the Strait of Hormuz, a weakened US military presence in the region, the ability to restart the nuclear program, and the breaking-up of the US-led coalition. Ways: keep fighting, deplete US munitions, demonstrate US weakness to allies, sustain high oil prices, delay talks and escalate when necessary. Means: existing missiles and cheap drones supplemented by Chinese and Russian deliveries, sustainable with present resources [25].

Iran calculates that time is on its side — the longer the war drags, the closer to November midterms, the more desperate Trump becomes [25]. The White House's leaked admission of munitions stockpile depletion confirmed what O'Brien calls the worst-kept secret of the war [25]. Ambassador Waltz's self-contradicting Meet the Press appearance — simultaneously blaming Biden, denying and admitting a shortfall, and calling for leakers to be prosecuted — reached what O'Brien calls a tragi-comic level [25]. After the US halted strikes, Iran continued attacking Bahrain, Jordan, Saudi Arabia, and Iraq, demonstrating that the [ceasefire]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/hormuz-ceasefire-collapse-2026.html) existed only in Trump's imagination [25].

The likely shape of peace, as O'Brien projects it: Iran issues a non-concession statement on nuclear weapons (no intention of developing them, with few actual checks), tacitly controls Hormuz through "administrative fees," gets assets unfrozen and sanctions wound down, and Trump declares victory — representing "the quickest and arguably the most decisive defeat in US history" [25].

### The 1861 Analogy: US Vulnerability at Its Deepest

O'Brien's "Is China Going In For The Kill" (2026-08-03) introduces a historical comparator for US vulnerability [24]. He compares the current moment to 1861 — the first year of the US Civil War — when outside powers directly threatened the US in ways similar to what China is doing now, and the US had to take it [24]. The US did not recover assertiveness until 1863–1864 [24]. But unlike then, the US is not on an upward trajectory now — and China seems to grasp that [24]. The weakness Trump has to a large degree created is being exploited by adversaries in real time [24].

He catalogs the full range of Chinese support for Iran: missile propellant chemicals (sodium perchlorate, enough for roughly 785 missiles), satellite intelligence (TEE-01B spy satellite monitoring US bases, BeiDou navigation for targeting), chipmaking equipment (SMIC), and now the prospective [transfer of up to 400 finished MANPADS launchers]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/china-manpads-transfer-iran-2026.html) (FN-16 and QW-12) [24]. The qualitative shift from dual-use to finished weapons marks a threshold: MANPADS would deter US low-level airpower and create a binary choice for Trump — escalate before they arrive or admit he cannot afford to escalate at all [24].

## Posture Toward NATO

O'Brien offers a sharply dissenting assessment of the [NATO Ankara Summit]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/nato-ankara-summit-2026.html), calling it "the worst summit in NATO history." His critique centers on five points: no US conventional defense commitment (the summit declaration contained nothing specific about the USA, and Secretary Hegseth's six-month force posture review is a veiled threat); the nuclear umbrella is meaningless without conventional forces ("The US might not fight for the Baltic States conventionally, but it will 'blow up the world' if Putin uses a nuclear weapon on them? This is simply not happening"); the Patriot license for [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html) is hollow (no details, no consultation with manufacturers, will take years); the 2027 summit was dropped from the declaration, and stories have started circulating that there might not be another leaders' summit while Trump is President; and Trump made three explicit threats against [Greenland]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/trump-greenland-crisis.html) over three days before, during, and after the summit. O'Brien identifies Trump's claims about caring, human affection, and "tremendous love in that room" as a reliable tell that he is lying [16].
