# Background

Geopolitical Futures is a strategic analysis publication founded by George Friedman that provides structured assessments of global geopolitical dynamics. GPF's approach emphasizes structural drivers — geography, demographics, military capacity, and economic fundamentals — over personality-driven analysis. The publication produces detailed country and regional assessments, often accompanied by maps that illustrate the geographic constraints driving political outcomes. GPF also publishes regular analytical articles by contributors including George Friedman, Antonia Colibasanu, Kamran Bokhari, and Ekaterina Zolotova, covering global geopolitical developments, Middle East security dynamics, and Russia-Ukraine war analysis [18][19][20][21][22][23][24][29][30].

# Key Frameworks

## Structural Drivers Over Personality

GPF's analytical approach prioritizes structural factors — geographic constraints, demographic trajectories, institutional capacity — over the individual leader decisions that [O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) emphasizes. This was previously characterized here as "a complementary tension," but the characterization was asserted rather than tested. Friedman's "Thinking About Iran's Strategy" reads US Iran-war strategy as following a structural war-termination pattern comparable to Vietnam, Iraq, and Afghanistan — patient, structurally bounded drawdown — while O'Brien's framework predicts erratic, personality-driven escalation tracking Trump's political calculus. These generate different near-term predictions about US behavior, which makes this a genuine fork in the agency-vs-structure fault line, not a complementary pairing. See the `## Contested: Is the US in decline?` block in [Great Power Competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html) and [Analytical Fault Lines]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/analytical-fault-lines.html).

## GeoEconomicLens as Systemic Product

GPF's monthly GeoEconomicLens (GEL) report views the global economy through the lens of geopolitics — tracing how wars, sanctions, and chokepoint disruptions cascade through energy, food, defense, insurance, and financial markets. The methodology is systemic by design: rather than treating each crisis as a discrete event, each issue maps the propagation of geopolitical shocks across regions and sectors, with regional breakdowns, scenario sets, and a month-ahead outlook. The May 2026 issue examined the fallout from the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) across all major regions and sectors, with contributions from Friedman, Colibasanu, Davidson, Herczegh, Wordsworth, and Zolotova [15]. The June 2026 issue focused on inflationary pressures reshaping the global economy, covering the AI crisis, African resource nationalism, EU defense financing, Venezuela's reopening, ASEAN energy coordination, Russian logistics risks, a global inflation feature with three scenarios, and Australia-Vietnam hedging case studies [16]. The July 2026 issue focused on the coming defense boom and geoeconomic transformations — the US heavy rare earths program, Russia's fuel crisis, the yuan undervaluation debate, the structural defense boom feature, EV industrial competition, the geoeconomics of water, and de-dollarization trends, with new contributor Juan Francisco Munoz joining the GEL team [17].

## Friedman's Geographic Determinism

George Friedman's recurring posture is that geography, demographics, and institutional capacity set the envelope within which leader choices and events play out. His 2026 forecast framed US-[China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) accommodation as the most important geopolitical issue, with [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) as a regional power and the [EU]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/organizations/eu.html) devolving into a multiregional system [7]. He argues the Iran war's most important long-term effect is on US-China bilateral relations — China's abstention driven by economic dependence on the US market and Hormuz-transited energy, with potential for a post-war economic accommodation analogous to post-WWII US-Germany/Japan [15]. He frames India's July 2026 Asia tour (Indonesia, Australia, New Zealand) as an emerging coalition more significant than the Quad, with economic and naval dimensions that could fundamentally shift the global system [18]. His Adam Smith framing treats economic and military power as inextricable, with geoeconomics as the methodology for understanding geopolitical effects on national economies [19]. He argues Russia cannot expand the war to another European nation because of poor military performance, [Poland]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/poland.html) as a fundamental problem, and US troops in Poland [21]. His philosophical analysis of AI identifies a dual danger — failure to realize potential, and success that displaces workers en masse — with the singularity as modern myth (Golem/Talos parallels) and the Luddite lesson that job destruction without replacement creates social and economic chaos [16].

## Naval Escalation and the Black Sea Grain Trade

In an analysis by Ekaterina Zolotova, GPF examined how the Russia-Ukraine war's naval turn is choking the grain trade in the Sea of Azov and the Black Sea, with cascading consequences for global food security [29]. Ukrainian strikes on seagoing vessels forced Russia to suspend shipping through the Don-Azov Canal and the Kerch Strait, while Russian retaliatory strikes destroyed port infrastructure near Odesa. The Azov-Black Sea basin accounts for 30 percent of all Russian port cargo turnover and hosts the country's major grain terminals — Novorossiysk, Taman, Rostov, and Tuapse. Despite sanctions reducing traditional exports like oil and gas, Russian grain shipments actually rose in the first half of 2026, with increases to Turkey (86 percent), Egypt (45 percent), China (63 percent), and Iran (75 percent), making the grain trade a high-value target for Ukrainian interdiction.

GPF argues that the financial pressure on Russian farmers is mounting from multiple directions: drone strikes igniting winter wheat fields, rising diesel prices costing farmers about 1,000 rubles ($13) per ton, and Sea of Azov shipping restrictions effectively doubling that loss. Continued pressure could force farmers to reduce grain plantings, eroding Russia's position as a major grain exporter over time. The forecast for Russian wheat exports in July was already reduced by 20 percent due to the attacks, delayed harvest, and fuel crisis; if disruptions persist, Russia may be unable to deliver as much as 10 million tons of wheat to the global market. GPF notes that Russia cannot easily redirect shipments — grain terminals in the Far East and Caspian Sea lack the capacity, and Kazakhstan has imposed a six-month ban on Russian wheat imports to protect its domestic market.

On the Ukrainian side, GPF observes that more than 90 percent of grain, vegetable oil, and other agricultural shipments pass through just three ports in the Odesa region, making the concentration a critical vulnerability. Russian missile and drone strikes have taken roughly a third of Ukrainian grain export capacity offline and reduced storage facilities, limiting export capacity even if a safe corridor is restored. Ukraine could reroute some exports via the Danube to Romania's Constanta port, but European farmers would likely oppose such a move.

GPF frames the disruption within a broader global food security crisis: hot weather in Western Europe threatens wheat crops, Canadian producers hesitate to sell old harvest amid quality concerns over the new one, and the USDA revised its 2026-27 U.S. wheat production forecast to 42.45 million metric tons — the lowest in 54 years — driven by severe drought in the U.S. wheat belt reducing the hard red winter wheat harvest by 25 percent. Combined with the [Hormuz blockade]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/geopolitical-futures.html)'s upward pressure on fuel, fertilizer, and maritime transport costs, GPF warns that a prolonged suspension of navigation through the peak export period of August to December would significantly compound the crisis, with much resting on the revival of the now-dead grain deal [29].

## Houthi Strategic Autonomy and Iran's Proxy Network

In an analysis by Kamran Bokhari, GPF argued that the Houthis are not fighting Iran's war but pursuing their own independent calculus — a reading that challenges the conventional framing of the group as the most active member of Iran's regional proxy network [30]. GPF observes that despite being widely described as Iran's most active partner, the Houthis had avoided escalation even during the worst crisis the Iranian regime has faced since its inception, making clear that they did not consider the US-Israeli war against Iran important enough to risk retaliation. Their central objective is to consolidate power as Yemen's dominant political and military force, and every move they make serves that goal.

GPF contends that Iran supplies resources — military technology, intelligence, and messaging — that bolster Houthi capabilities, but does not exercise the command and control associated with a traditional proxy relationship. The Houthis retain considerable freedom to operate as they see fit, coordinating with Tehran when it suits their interests but pulling back when sustained escalation threatens their domestic standing or invites unacceptable retaliation. GPF identifies the memory of Operation Rough Rider — the 52-day U.S. campaign in 2025 comprising more than 1,000 strikes against Houthi targets — as a critical constraint on Houthi behavior, a lesson they do not wish to relive.

The analysis traces specific Houthi actions as instruments of political coercion rather than ideological solidarity with Iran: the July 13-14 missile and drone strikes on Saudi Arabia's Abha airport, launched after accusing the Saudis of attacking Sanaa International Airport while a Houthi delegation returning from Tehran attempted to land, followed by a July 21 announcement imposing a maritime blockade on Saudi Arabia. GPF reads these moves as carefully calibrated escalation designed to exploit Saudi Arabia's desire to safeguard its economic transformation plans — limited coercion that reminds Riyadh of Houthi capacity to impose economic and political pain without triggering a full return to war.

GPF's broader argument concerns the cohesion of Iran's proxy network as Tehran's position deteriorates. As Iran loses influence through successive military setbacks, economic pressure, and the weakening of allied groups, the Houthis' standing relative to the internationally recognized government in Aden declines, giving them every incentive to demonstrate coercive power independent of Iran. The Houthis have also capitalized on the Saudi-Emirati rift over Yemen's political future — Riyadh backing the Aden government while Abu Dhabi supports the Southern Transitional Council — which prevents a united anti-Houthi front and grants the movement room to consolidate control in northern Yemen. GPF concludes that as Iran's position deteriorates, the cohesion of its regional network will depend increasingly on the independent calculations of its members rather than direction from Tehran, a dynamic that limits Iran's ability to offset conventional military losses through its partners and explains why the regional balance of power is shifting against Iran despite sporadic "successes" in the Red Sea and the Gulf [30].

## Azerbaijan as Strategic Pivot

George Friedman (GPF) assesses [Azerbaijan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/azerbaijan.html) as a small but disproportionately significant state whose geography at the intersection of [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html), [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html), and [Turkey]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/turkey.html) makes it a natural arena for great-power competition. The 2023 Nagorno-Karabakh victory, achieved with Turkish support, cemented Azerbaijan's military credibility and consolidated the Turkey-Azerbaijan axis. The Trump Route for International Peace and Prosperity (TRIPP) — a US-mediated Armenia-Azerbaijan agreement connecting Azerbaijan to its Nakhchivan exclave along the Iranian border — elevates Azerbaijan from a terminal energy node to a transit hub linking the [Caspian Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/caspian.html) to Turkey and Europe. Friedman emphasizes the Iran-Azerbaijan border as a sensitive zone: Azeris are Iran's third-largest ethnic group at roughly 25% of the population, giving Azerbaijan potential influence over Iran's internal dynamics. Israel's deep partnership with Azerbaijan since independence adds a further layer, providing Israel with a strategic perch on Iran's northern border. Friedman frames the [South Caucasus]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/south-caucasus.html) as "in some ways the soft underbelly of Russia" — a region where Moscow's grip has weakened as the Ukraine war degrades its capacity to project power southward [32].

## Caspian War Convergence

Kamran Bokhari (GPF) identifies the [Caspian Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/caspian.html) as the basin where two geographically distant wars — the US-vs-Iran conflict centered on the Gulf and the Russia-vs-Ukraine conflict centered on the Black Sea — are converging. The dynamic is driven by the Iran-Russia military relationship: Russia relies on Iranian Shahed drones, drone-production expertise, artillery shells, and short-range ballistic missiles, receiving in return advanced drones and tactical intelligence. This arms-smuggling route traverses the Caspian, making the sea a logistics artery for one war and a potential target in the other. Bokhari documents the escalation sequence: Israel's March 2026 strike on Bandar Anzali opened the Caspian as a theater; Ukraine's July 26 strike on an Iranian cargo vessel linked the two wars directly. Iran considered retaliating against a Ukrainian Black Sea port before diplomacy de-escalated. Bokhari notes that the six trans-Caspian states have a shared incentive to keep the Caspian a commercial waterway through the Middle Corridor, but warns that if Tehran continues expanding the conflict while pressure mounts on Washington, the US could shift to attacking Iranian Caspian assets directly [33].

Victoria Herczegh assesses China's military reconstruction as a readiness paradox: loyalty, surveillance, and AI-enabled modernization may improve political control while degrading initiative, honest reporting, and decentralized adaptation in a fast-moving crisis. She judges that routine operations and modernization continue, but a move against Taiwan remains strategically unreasonable while the senior command structure is being rebuilt. [38]

## Pacific Order and China's SLBM Test

Ronan Wordsworth (GPF) analyzes [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html)'s July 6, 2026 JL-3 SLBM test — the first publicly acknowledged submarine-launched ballistic missile test into open international waters — as a demonstration of second-strike capability that simultaneously catalyzed Pacific Island backlash and Australian alliance-building. The missile traveled approximately 7,300km from a South China Sea launch point, landing in the South Pacific Nuclear Free Zone established by the 1985 Treaty of Rarotonga. Pacific Island leaders from Tuvalu, Vanuatu, Palau, and Solomon Islands condemned the test, invoking the region's Cold War nuclear testing legacy — but the 18-member Pacific Islands Forum failed to agree on a collective statement, revealing China's diplomatic clout. Wordsworth documents a de facto US-Australia division of labor: the US manages the Compacts of Free Association with Palau, the Marshall Islands, and Micronesia in the Northern Pacific, while [Australia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/australia.html) serves as day-to-day security partner across the South Pacific. Australia's emerging alliance architecture — the Ocean of Peace Alliance with Fiji (signed the same day as the JL-3 test), the Pukpuk Mutual Defense Treaty with PNG, and the Nakamal Agreement with Vanuatu — is supplemented by financial dominance: $1.5 billion in official development finance in 2024 (37% of the regional total) and $2.4 billion in new Pacific loan agreements since 2021, displacing China as the top bilateral lender. Chinese annual lending has declined to approximately $91 million since 2020, yet roughly 40% of Pacific infrastructure projects since 2008 have involved Chinese SOEs — a structural presence that reduced lending does not eliminate [34].

## Post-Iran-War MENA Architecture

Kamran Bokhari (GPF) argues that the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) is less about "defeating" Iran than about creating the conditions for US allies to take the reins of regional security. The US objective is to transition from regional hegemon to offshore actor with its finger on the scales — an offloading doctrine implemented before the war but accelerated by it. The defining feature of the emerging order is a new regional security architecture built around a core of [Turkey]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/turkey.html), [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html), [Pakistan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/pakistan.html), and [Egypt]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/egypt.html), with support from [Qatar]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/qatar.html), the [UAE]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/uae.html), [Jordan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/jordan.html), and [Azerbaijan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/azerbaijan.html). The GPF "Shifting Alliances" map illustrates this configuration [35].

The framework identifies the "blank canvas" problem: unlike Europe with NATO and the EU to provide institutional frameworks, the [Middle East]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html) lacks integration mechanisms and must construct them from scratch. The core challenge is aligning countries with their own interests and priorities while integrating them into a workable relationship with [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html) — the three most consequential actors (Turkey, Saudi Arabia, Pakistan) either lack formal diplomatic ties with Israel or, in Ankara's case, have seen relations deteriorate. Iran's proxy network collapse has created a strategic opening, but Iran continues to exploit the Israeli-Palestinian conflict to obstruct broader regional alignments. Bokhari notes a fundamental psychological shift among Arab governments: Iran attacking them at will is the "new normal" that will shape their calculations for years. The framework connects to [Layered Security]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/layered-security.html) — regional stakeholders must construct a self-sustaining balance of power as Washington reduces its direct military and financial exposure. See [Kamran Bokhari]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/kamran-bokhari.html) [35].

## Russia-EU Back-Channel Diplomacy

Andrew Ryvkin (GPF) argues that [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html)'s road back to the global stage runs through [Berlin]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/germany.html), not Washington. While media attention has focused on Russia-US negotiations, it is the [EU]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/organizations/eu.html) that shares a border with Russia, whose sanctions have hurt the Russian economy most, and that holds the decisive vote over frozen Russian assets, banking system integration, and European market access. Russia-China trade totaled $228 billion in 2025 — still less than Russia-EU trade of $282 billion in 2021 before the full-scale invasion [36].

The framework documents a sustained back-channel diplomacy between Germany and Russia. Since at least early 2024, meetings in Baku and Abu Dhabi have involved former German politicians (from Merkel's cabinet and former OSCE officials) and Kremlin-connected figures, formally discussing the revival of the Petersburg Dialogue (the German-Russian forum founded by Schroeder and Putin in 2001, terminated by Berlin in 2021). The Russian delegation includes Viktor Zubkov (former prime minister, Gazprom supervisory board chairman, and Putin's close friend from St. Petersburg), Valery Fadeyev (head of Russia's Human Rights Council and Putin adviser), and Alexey Gromyko (head of the Institute of Europe at the Russian Academy of Sciences). The framework notes that these figures carry more political weight inside Russia than media-friendly envoys like Kirill Dmitriev. Berlin has also maintained a channel through Roman Abramovich for narrowly defined humanitarian issues [36].

The framework argues that European decision-makers believe Russia remains structurally oriented toward Europe, and that maintaining Russia's subordinate relationship with China runs against Moscow's long-term interests. Putin has signaled interest in resuming gas exports to Germany, requiring only a political decision in Berlin. If a [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html) settlement is reached, these back channels are likely to expand as business leaders, bankers, and former officials become important in discussions over sanctions, frozen assets, reconstruction, and commercial ties. See [Andrew Ryvkin]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/andrew-ryvkin.html) and [Political Normalization]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/political-normalization.html) [36].
