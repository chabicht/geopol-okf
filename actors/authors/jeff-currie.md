# Background

Jeff Currie is a commodity and energy market analyst who built his career at Goldman Sachs, where he served as global head of commodities research for nearly three decades. He is currently Chief Strategy Officer at Altis Partners and serves as a Senior Advisor to the Carlyle Group. His expertise spans oil supply-demand dynamics, refining capacity, crack spreads, and the interaction between geopolitical disruptions and commodity price transmission. In the bundle, he appears both as the energy-specialist collaborator in [Robert Pape](robert-pape.html)'s August 2026 live briefing on the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) and as the guest on [Nate Hagens]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/nate-hagens.html)'s TGS 229 podcast, where he articulated comprehensive frameworks on the abundance illusion, the Grand Bargain, and the physical capital paradox [1][2].

# Key Frameworks

## Crude-Refining Double Bottleneck

Currie's central contribution to the August 2026 briefing is the identification of a double bottleneck in global energy markets: the crude-supply shock from the Hormuz closure is compounded by a parallel collapse in refining capacity, driven in part by Ukraine's strikes on Russian refineries. The relevant consumer product is gasoline and diesel, not crude itself; the crack spread — the margin between buying crude and selling refined products — reached $70 per barrel, the highest ever recorded. This framework explains why higher gasoline prices arrive even before crude buffers are fully exhausted, as refining constraints add a second layer of price pressure on top of the crude shortage.

## Refining Capacity Asymmetry Between China and the US

Currie identifies a structural asymmetry: China maintains a substantial refining surplus while the US is running at or above capacity. Over time, China can offer refining services and set geopolitical terms, positioning Beijing to benefit from the crisis regardless of how the Hormuz situation resolves. This insight connects the energy-market analysis to broader [Great Power Competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html) dynamics. Russia has now converted from the world's largest product and crude exporter to a net importer — importing jet fuel from Japan and diesel from India — deepening the refining-capacity reversal [1][2].

## The Abundance Illusion

Currie articulates a foundational framework for understanding Western energy policy since the 1970s. Carter's February 1977 "sweater speech" and April 1977 "energy transition" speech — in which he coined the term and called for investment in non-fossil fuels for security reasons — were the founding events. Carter "made a fatal mistake" by admitting scarcity; he "got killed" for it. Every president from George H. W. Bush onward learned the lesson: never admit scarcity, and instead suppress the signal through strategic reserve drawdowns and market interventions. Bush Sr. (the 1991 Gulf War as the first abundance-illusion presidency), Clinton, Bush Jr., Obama, Biden, and Trump all followed the same playbook. China, by contrast, heeded Carter's energy-transition advice for security reasons — investing in nuclear, solar, wind, batteries, and lithium not for environmental purposes but for energy security. The West pursued the abundance illusion while China built the alternative [2].

## The Grand Bargain

Currie provides the cleanest articulation of the architecture underpinning US dollar primacy: the Bretton Woods 1945 deal in which the US protects global sea lanes in exchange for trade flowing through New York and dollar usage. When the US emerged from WWII with 95% of global industrial production, the arrangement made sense. Eighty years later, the US is still performing the same role with a much smaller share of global output. The Grand Bargain is structurally obsolete. Failure to reopen the [Strait of Hormuz]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html) breaks the bargain — if the US cannot protect sea lanes and allies, capital begins pulling out of the United States. "That's gonna hit middle class America and change their lifestyles like they've never seen before." See [Monetary System Transition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/monetary-system-transition.html) [2].

## The Physical Capital Paradox

Commodities are the best-performing asset class of the decade — up over 200%, outpacing crypto at 157% — yet commodities remain dramatically under-owned at roughly 3% of the S&P. The paradox has a structural mechanism: at 3% S&P weight, energy doubling yields only a 6% portfolio return, while tech at ~40% of the S&P needs only a 12% rise to match. Dodd-Frank destroyed back-end curve liquidity by eliminating the proprietary risk-taking that once provided depth to commodity futures markets. Without that liquidity, the price signals that would normally trigger investment in physical capital are suppressed, ensuring the under-investment persists. The result: "huge potholes ahead" as the market loses the ability to smooth supply disruptions [2].

## The 3-2-1 Crack Spread Signal

"Crude is the noise; products are the signal." The 3-2-1 crack spread — weighting gasoline at three, diesel at two, and crude at one — reached $60 per barrel in mid-2026, a three-decade high. Diesel products are up 81% year-to-date against a broader index gain of 34%. The effective price of oil to consumers is not the crude price but the refined-product price: diesel at $160 per barrel effective against ~$100 Brent. The crack spread tells the real story of the crisis — the consumer-facing shortage is in refined products, not crude [2].

## Fiscal-Transfer Inflation

Currie argues that inflation is driven by fiscal transfers to the bottom two income quintiles — households with zero credit who live paycheck to paycheck — not by monetary policy alone. When governments inject fiscal transfers, these households spend immediately on commodities and goods, producing demand spikes that ripple through commodity prices. Every fiat-currency inflation regime follows this pattern: Venezuela, Italy in the 1980s, Latin America in the 1980s, Brazil in the 2000s, and the US in 2022. The mechanism is disguised in standard macro analysis, which treats inflation as a monetary phenomenon; Currie reframes it as a fiscal-transfer-to-commodity-demand pipeline. See [Inflation and Monetary Policy]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/inflation-and-monetary-policy.html) [2].

## The Munificent Seven

Currie coins the term "Munificent Seven" — "munificent means gifting lavishly" — for the hyperscaler technology companies whose valuations concentrate roughly 40% of the S&P. The concentration creates systemic risk: if these companies experience a drawdown comparable to what oil experienced in 2014, the S&P amplifies the instability. The real danger, however, is not to the companies themselves but to sovereigns: governments face the fiscal and employment consequences of a valuation collapse while the companies adapt. AI companies face a 2014-oil-style drawdown risk as the $800 billion annual buildout pushes commodity input costs higher [2].

## The AI Race Is an Energy Race

The competition between the US and China in AI is fundamentally an energy competition. China's bet on zero-marginal-cost electricity — renewables, nuclear, and battery storage — is a sunk-cost strategy: the capital is already spent, and the marginal cost of generation approaches zero. If China achieves this, "it's game over for the rest of the world." The transcontinental-railroad analogy applies: when the US connected the Pacific and Atlantic by rail, it was game over for competitors. China's electrification strategy operates on the same logic — dominant infrastructure that restructures the competitive landscape permanently [2].

## The New Joule Order

A security premium has replaced the green premium on energy. Electrification is no longer an environmental choice but the purchase of optionality — insulation from chokepoint disruption and fossil-fuel dependency. China is decades ahead of the West in this transition. Once the discussion moved from environmental to security, the transition will accelerate: peak oil demand may arrive sooner than expected because security-driven electrification turbocharges the shift. The New Joule Order frames energy not as a commodity market but as a security architecture [2].
