# Background

The United States remains the world's dominant superpower, yet its position rests on a foundation of extraordinary energy throughput. With roughly 4% of the global population, the US consumes approximately 16% of world energy — a structural reality that underpins both its military reach and its economic primacy. This energy advantage, however, is increasingly contested as rival powers close the gap and as the global system itself fractures [1].

The US-led international order is under simultaneous pressure from multiple theaters: an unresolved [Cold War 2]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/cold-war-2.html) with the DragonBear axis of [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) and [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html), a [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) that exposed deep military and diplomatic weaknesses, and an accelerating [AI military race]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/ai-military-race.html) where technological advantage has not yet translated into operational integration.

# Current Situation

## Strategic Posture: Military Decline & Overstretch

The [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) revealed serious deficiencies in American military planning and execution. The US failed to protect key facilities, could not achieve its strategic objectives, and demonstrated poor operational planning throughout the campaign. Critically, there was no rally-round-the-flag effect — American society remained locked in a partisan death match, denying the wartime president the domestic political consolidation that typically accompanies foreign military operations. [Phillips O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) documented that a CSIS report confirmed munitions depletion, and that the Trump administration's corruption has "percolated to the military" [2][8]. [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) made a personal decision to attack Iran that was not grounded in national interest; there was no meaningful political pressure compelling the strike. Despite occasional critical rhetoric, Trump has continued policies that effectively assist [Putin]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/putin.html), raising questions about the alignment of executive action with declared strategic priorities [5]. By May 2026, O'Brien reported Trump was "desperate to exit" the war but unable to, with no good military options [9]. The operational specifics of the degradation — aircraft losses, equipment damage, base destruction — are examined in the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) conflict file.

The war has also hollowed out the US naval presence in the [Indo-Pacific]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/indo-pacific.html). [Phillips P. O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) documents that the US Navy now has zero aircraft carriers deployed in the Western Pacific for the first time since 1943–44, after the George Washington was redeployed to replace the Lincoln in the Middle East. The carrier maintenance backlog is backed up for years; the Lincoln alone will require at least two years of refit. The cumulative effect is that the US has gone from dominant military power in the Western Pacific to counterweight to close to a non-factor in any war with [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) [62].

The war accelerated an existing trend of diplomatic decline. The US alienated European allies, [Japan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/japan.html), and [South Korea]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/south-korea.html). Gulf states increasingly view Washington as an unreliable security partner. The inability to maintain alliance cohesion during a major military campaign signals a structural weakening of the US-led coalition system. In Latin America, the US is attempting to reconstruct a hemispheric bloc through the Shield of the Americas framework, seeking to consolidate influence in its immediate neighborhood even as its global reach contracts [6].

## Strategic Posture: Retrenchment and Regional Recalibration

The Trump administration is publicly choosing to reduce American military exposure abroad — not ceding ground under compulsion, but ceding power by choice as the necessary implementation of a strategy that turns responsibility for regional defense over to regional powers. The approach is visible in both Trump's statements and policy papers such as the National Security Strategy, though the [Iran war]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) sits in tension with this stated posture. The US remains engaged economically and in terms of defense building and spending, even as military presence contracts [25].

In the [Indo-Pacific]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/indo-pacific.html), [Victoria Herczegh]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/geopolitical-futures.html) (GPF) documents the doctrinal label: Secretary of Defense Hegseth's 2026 Shangri-La Dialogue speech articulated a strategy of "flexible, practical realism" that seeks increased transactional burden-sharing from regional allies — including those that meet new defense spending standards and accelerate military transformation to take on more active roles in maintaining security. This stands in stark contrast to the traditional model in which the US acted as the sole security guarantor for the region. The Trump administration's reversion to the military designation "Pacific Command" (from "Indo-Pacific Command") is a performative narrowing of the regional posture. The result is an emerging regional security architecture in which principal allies — particularly Japan and Australia — assume more proactive leadership roles while the resulting network continues to rest on US influence but no longer depends on direct US leadership [25].

In the Middle East, [Andrew Davidson]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/geopolitical-futures.html) (GPF) frames the US as facing a sustained-campaign cost problem in the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html). The military balance strongly favors the United States, but achieving Washington's objectives — suppressing Iranian threats, protecting commercial traffic, defending regional bases, denying Iran de facto authority over an international waterway — would require a sustained campaign whose costs rise with time. The US can absorb those costs far longer than [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html), but a prolonged operation places growing demands on forces and munitions already committed across several regions. Further Iranian escalation would require the US to defend not only personnel and bases but also ports, shipping routes, and energy infrastructure throughout the Gulf — consuming air and missile defense interceptors, surveillance capacity, and naval assets at significant cost. Because the campaign also depends on continued Gulf access and cooperation, prolonged resource expenditure without restored passage or Iranian concessions becomes increasingly difficult to justify. The approaching US midterm elections sharpen this pressure, but the larger constraint is the cost of sustaining regional protection without a clear political result [26].

## Doctrine: The AI-Military Integration Gap

The United States holds a 3-6 month lead in AI model development, but trails [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) by years in military integration. The policy environment is characterized by a vacuum: Congress and the executive branch operate with friction, and no coherent national strategy has emerged for closing the integration gap. Beijing's coordinated, national-level approach contrasts sharply with Washington's fragmented response [3]. The Director of National Intelligence faces a set of strategic decisions: refocusing the ODNI, resourcing the intelligence community for AI and space domains, and adapting Cold War-era institutions to contemporary great-power competition [4].

[Adam Tooze]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/adam-tooze.html) frames the US AI boom as a world-transforming technology analogous to the atom bomb — potentially disrupting work, culture, and warfare on a scale that cannot be calculated. The key contrast is political economy: the mid-century transformative technologies (nuclear, rockets) were under state control (Oppenheimer, von Braun), whereas AI is driven by privately developed technology, private money, and the "unfettered egos of tech entrepreneurs" — the broligarchs of Gotham City. The BIS Annual Economic Report identifies the AI boom as endogenously generating a China-scale investment and credit boom, with the labor share of income potentially collapsing to 20% over the next forty years. Trillions of dollars are wagered on a vision of total disruption of familiar ways of living and working [18]. The [Cipher Brief]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/the-cipher-brief.html) underscores that the AI race will not be won by the best model but by the fastest military — a domain where China's integrated national approach currently holds the advantage [3].

[Phillips O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) provides a devastating empirical assessment of the Pentagon's AI-First doctrine. In January 2026, Defense Secretary Hegseth publicly released an order designating the US military an "AI-First" warfighting machine. CENTCOM commander Admiral Bradley Cooper echoed the boast at the Iran war's outset, claiming AI tools helped warfighters "sift through vast amounts of data in seconds so our leaders can cut through the noise and make smarter decisions faster than the enemy can react." Palantir CEO Alex Karp went further, asserting that without AI systems Russia would have won and Europe would face rampant terror attacks. Yet the US Iran campaign — which attacked over 13,000 targets and claimed 90% of Iran's weapons manufacturing was destroyed — became what O'Brien calls "one of the worst performances in the history of warfare": the Iranian military reconstituted rapidly, the US exhausted its advanced ordnance, and ultimately lost the war. If AI was central to the targeting packages, it was central to creating the most counterproductive strike campaign in military history. AI still struggles to analyze military and strategic systems, cannot process enough data, and makes fundamental errors such as targeting schools [58]. See [AI Military Race]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/ai-military-race.html).

## Doctrine: Military Mobility and Infrastructure Security

Retired Gen. Mike Minihan and Rear Adm. Mark Montgomery ([The Cipher Brief]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/the-cipher-brief.html)) argue that American military power is meaningless if it cannot move. Every war plan — from deterring aggression in [Europe]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/europe.html) to prevailing in the [Indo-Pacific]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/indo-pacific.html) — depends on the ability to maneuver people, equipment, fuel, information, and combat power faster than any adversary can react. Yet every deployment begins on commercial railroads, moves through civilian ports and airports, depends on privately owned communications networks, and increasingly relies on digital logistics systems that the Department of Defense does not own. This is America's strategic advantage and one of its greatest vulnerabilities [27].

[China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) does not need to destroy American combat power to gain an advantage — it simply needs to delay it. Every day equipment sits in a rail yard, every hour a port is offline, every aircraft waylaid by corrupted logistics data creates the friction an adversary seeks during the opening days of a conflict. For more than two years, US intelligence officials have warned that Chinese cyber operators are compromising hundreds of American transportation, communications, energy, and logistics systems, gaining persistent access so that Beijing can disrupt and destroy systems at the time of its choosing. [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html)'s cyberattacks against [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html)'s transportation, energy, and communications infrastructure reveal what this looks like in practice: modern wars are fought as much against the systems that sustain military power as against the military itself [27].

The policy prescriptions: harmonize cybersecurity standards across transportation sectors so operators spend more time improving security than satisfying overlapping regulations; provide cybersecurity grant programs for under-resourced transportation infrastructure operators (smaller ports, airports, and rail operators cannot reasonably defend themselves against nation-state adversaries without federal partnership); and begin exercising the way the US expects to fight — national, regional, and local exercises should assume degraded communications, cyberattacks against transportation systems, corrupted logistics data, and contested movement inside the United States. "Amateurs study tactics, professionals study logistics. The next war demands we take one step further. Victors study maneuver." If America cannot connect, it cannot maneuver; if it cannot move, it cannot fight [27]. This vulnerability connects directly to the broader [defense industrial resilience]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/defense-industrial-resilience.html) agenda — the binding constraint is no longer kinetic reach but the energy and digital substrate that sustains it.

## Domestic Dynamics: Institutional Degradation and Oligarchic Capture

The dismantling of American democratic institutions proceeds along judicial, personnel, financial, and symbolic axes simultaneously, each reinforcing the others. The [Slaughter SCOTUS ruling]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/slaughter-scotus-ruling.html) stripped regulatory agencies of their independence, allowing [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) to direct, gut, or repurpose any agency created by Congress — what [Paul Krugman]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/paul-krugman.html) frames as judicial enablement of oligarchic capture, with civil servants replaced by loyalists and regulatory outcomes determined by who enriches the president rather than by statutory mandate [7]. The personnel axis extends into the Department of Justice under Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche — Trump's former criminal defense attorney — who slipped during his confirmation hearing for elevation to attorney general, answering "I'm his lawyer" before correcting himself to "was his lawyer." [Heather Cox Richardson]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/heather-cox-richardson.html) documents that Blanche has openly used the DOJ to pursue Trump's political opponents, and a federal judge in Florida questioned whether he is fit to practice law after finding that the slush fund/immunity deal he signed off on with Trump and the Trump Organization had been manufactured to give cover to a deal they did not want reviewed by a judge. In Seattle, a panel of federal judges unanimously appointed a former judge as U.S. attorney to replace a Trump appointee whose 120-day interim position had expired; within an hour of taking the oath, he was fired by email [29].

The financial infrastructure of this capture operates through three mechanisms Krugman identifies: campaign finance (Citizens United unleashed a tsunami of billionaire money), corruption (under Trump 2.0 shifting from implicit revolving-door incentives to open cash, crypto, and sweetheart contracts), and information environment shaping (right-wing think tanks and captured media shifting the Overton Window). Corporate profits as a share of national income keep rising, reflecting increased monopoly power and the destruction of workers' rights — trends the Slaughter ruling will accelerate [7]. Corporate taxes fell from 35% in the 1960s to around 12% today, despite overwhelming and stable public opposition — Gallup polling over 20+ years shows a huge majority saying corporations pay too little. The 2017 Trump corporate tax cuts produced no surge in US investment, confirming no trickle-down — the decline was a massive indirect tax cut for wealthy stockholders [22]. Richardson traces the corruption's concrete form: Trump's $1.4 billion in cryptocurrency venture profits since taking office, his memecoin collapsing from $75.35 to $1.76 (97%), costing nearly a million followers $3.81 billion in collective losses while Trump took transaction fees up front. The Freedom 250 scheme diverted donations from the bipartisan America250 program to Trump's own organization [19]. This is the financial infrastructure of the broader [ideology and democracy]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/ideology-and-democracy.html) crisis.

The symbolic dimension of capture is documented by both [Anne Applebaum]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/anne-applebaum.html) and Richardson. Applebaum records the Rose Garden desecrated, the East Wing rebuilt as a personal monument, the Reflecting Pool redone incompetently with police compelled to enforce the president's fiction about vandalism, and the 250th Fourth of July celebration usurped by a privately funded Trump event — a pattern reflecting a governing logic in which the state exists to serve the ruler's clan [11]. Richardson extends the account through July 2026: the Treasury announced a commemorative $1 coin with Trump's likeness — a practice associated with living political leaders who try to make themselves indistinguishable from the government (Assad, Idi Amin, Saddam Hussein), made possible only by exploiting a loophole in the 1866 law barring living people from U.S. financial instruments. Trump is building a helipad on the South Lawn of the White House — normally very hard to permit because of zoning, airspace, and environmental review — without permission from Congress or any review panel, with Lockheed Martin (a major defense contractor) donating the construction. The Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool renovation he ordered without review is failing at the seams, which experts attributed to installation flaws; the White House denied missteps and had the Department of Justice arrest a 67-year-old cyclist for "vandalism" after he touched the detached lining. A Washington Post/Ipsos poll showed Trump's approval at 37%, with only 15% of Americans "strongly" approving and 66% saying groceries are unaffordable — the lowest strong-approval figure on record [29].

[Phillips O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) warns that the degradation reaches into the intelligence community: the Trump administration's plan to compile a master list of all foreign intelligence sources could be catastrophically destructive. The centralization of source identities in a single database creates an unprecedented counterintelligence liability — a breach or leak would compromise every foreign liaison relationship the US maintains. The plan serves no operational purpose other than to consolidate control over information that has historically been compartmented across agencies [14].

## Domestic Dynamics: Partisan Balkanization and Ideological Weaponization

The fragmentation of shared national identity has become both a strategic vulnerability and a scaffold for authoritarian consolidation. [O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) argues, using Gallup polling data, that America has balkanized into two partisan camps where loyalty to party now determines views on war and America's global role. Republicans and Democrats express satisfaction with the U.S. global position almost solely based on whether their party holds the presidency. Unlike during Vietnam or Kosovo, when partisanship was secondary to national identity, shared attachment to being American has eroded — a loss of shared national identity that compounds the military and diplomatic weaknesses exposed by the [Iran war]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) [15]. [Richardson]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/heather-cox-richardson.html) identifies the ideological weaponization that exploits this fragmentation: Trump's July 2026 Mt. Rushmore speech framed political opponents as "communists" — "the greatest threat to our country, including World War I, World War II, Pearl Harbor, or even 9/11" — a fabricated narrative constructed after the electoral success of Democratic Socialists in primary elections. Trump demanded the Senate end the filibuster and Congress pass the SAVE America Act: "We will not lose an election for a hundred years." On July 4, hundreds of masked white supremacists from Patriot Front marched in Washington, D.C.; the White House did not respond to queries about whether Trump condemns the march [19].

The ideological escalation intensified on July 17, 2026, when Trump, Secretary of State Rubio, and deputy White House chief of staff Stephen Miller threw the weight of the U.S. government behind the far-right conspiracy theory articulated in Jack Posobiec and Joshua Lisec's bestselling *Unhumans: The Secret History of Communist Revolutions (and How to Crush Them)* — blurbed by Donald Trump Jr. and J.D. Vance and introduced by Stephen Bannon. The book argues that anyone from communists to "progressives" places themselves "outside the category" of humanity and must be "unhumaned" first. Miller's State Department speech echoed the book's central framing almost verbatim, describing antifa demonstrators as people of whom "not one looks normal" and whose "outer appearance becomes a manifestation of their inner hatred." Bannon argued on his *War Room* podcast that the communist-threat narrative is "a predicate for immediate action" — a national security emergency executive order that "took all of SAVE America plus plus plus plus" [30]. Trump's prime-time speech the previous night tied American journalists to an alleged Chinese plot, claimed China acquired 220 million U.S. voter files (the White House's own documents showed such information is publicly available for purchase), and demanded Congress pass the SAVE America voter-suppression act. One of the documents Trump released showed Russia tried to influence the 2020 election to defeat Biden — contradicting his own denial of Russian interference. DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin then threatened state officials with criminal prosecution if they don't comply with Trump's demands to purge voter rolls through a federal digital tool that federal judges have blocked sixteen times for flagging recently naturalized citizens as noncitizens. The White House declared that "far-left extremism will be treated with the same seriousness and ferocity the world has long reserved for jihadist terrorism," despite a 2025 CSIS analysis showing left-wing political violence remains much lower than historical levels of right-wing and jihadist violence [30]. ## Domestic Dynamics: The Oligarchic Spiral and Media Capture

The hyper-wealthy have moved from passive influence to active capture of both the political system and the information environment. [Krugman]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/paul-krugman.html) argues that the political backlash against billionaires — what Tyler Cowen dismisses as "billionaire derangement syndrome" — is belated rather than excessive. The wealth of the Forbes 400 grew from $92 billion in 1982 to $6.6 trillion in 2025, dwarfing gains for the average American even adjusted for inflation. A New York Fed report documents that the share of national income going to workers is at an 80-year low. The hyper-wealthy are not merely "landmaxxing" with giant estates — they are "powermaxxing," seriously undermining American democracy as well as lowering the living standards of ordinary Americans. The combination of soaring billionaire wealth and the Roberts Supreme Court's Citizens United decision opened the floodgates: billionaires accounted for almost 20% of campaign spending in 2024, and that figure surely understates their influence. Some of what the Trump administration does reflects Trump's personal whims — which is why the Iran debacle happened — but a large part of federal policy is now "government of the billionaires, by the billionaires, for the billionaires" [31]. The specific actors embody the pattern: Larry Ellison's family has taken control of CBS, rapidly corrupting it into a right-wing mouthpiece, and is trying to take over CNN too. Elon Musk appears personally committed to white supremacy and right-wing extremism. Peter Thiel, who bought JD Vance his Ohio Senate seat, has called for a return to monarchy and now rants about the antichrist — "deranged billionaire syndrome." The predatory billionaires want low taxes, anti-competitive mergers approved, financial regulation eliminated, and healthcare stripped from ordinary people to fund tax cuts. The wealthiest 400 Americans pay an average tax rate of 24%, compared with 30% for the population at large and 45% for high-income Americans who derive income from earnings rather than asset ownership. Low taxes on the hyper-wealthy feed a downward spiral of oligarchy that has been taking place for decades — the real puzzle is why the backlash did not happen sooner [31].

This wealth-to-power pipeline operates most visibly through media consolidation. Krugman documents the pattern: the Ellison family acquired CBS (hiring Bari Weiss to reshape its orientation) and is attempting a Warner/CNN takeover; [Musk]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) seized Twitter/X and tilted its algorithm toward right-wing content; Zuckerberg is tilting Facebook; and Bezos shifted the *Washington Post*'s editorial policy hard right while eviscerating its news division. The objective is influence, not profit. A *New York Times* analysis found that 300 billionaires and their families provided 20% of all 2024 campaign contributions, and the purchase of politicians is now conducted brazenly through cash and crypto rather than through discreet indirect channels. The information space is being shaped to favor billionaire interests through zombie economic ideas (tax cuts self-funding), climate disinformation (100% of skeptical studies have fossil fuel backing), and algorithmic bias embedded in captured platforms. Krugman's conclusion: only reducing wealth concentration at the top offers a durable solution, because "if there are men big enough to own the government, they're going to own the government" [42]. The same day, Trump's appointees in the Department of Justice were reported to be overruling career attorneys in the antitrust division — pushing ahead without merger reviews designed to protect consumers from monopoly power, in what a former assistant attorney general called "unilateral surrender" [28].

The capture extends into AI policy and antitrust, where Krugman's interview with Lina Khan — who chaired the FTC under Biden and played an important role on the Mamdani transition team — extends the powermaxxing analysis. Khan frames the AI supply chain as a stack in which each layer (chips, hyperscalers, cloud infrastructure, models, apps and services) has economic properties that can easily tip toward monopoly through network effects. Allowing monopolization at one layer without rules such as common carriage or equal access can result in other layers becoming monopolized or distorted. The FTC had been investigating bottlenecks at the chip layer (Nvidia as gatekeeper) and the cloud layer (hyperscalers privileging their own AI products). The "interlocking directorates" pattern — Microsoft and OpenAI, Google and Amazon — raises questions about whether existing monopolists are gaining outsized control over competitively strategic decisions through opaque financial relationships [33]. Khan's February 2025 *Times* op-ed "Stop Worshiping the American Tech Giants" framed the choice starkly: paradigm-breaking innovations historically come from competitive markets, not from markets where gatekeepers decide who enters. The catalyst was DeepSeek — a Chinese AI firm whose model rivalled American sophistication with much greater efficiency. The conflict of interest is structural: the major cloud providers have their own cloud computing businesses, so they have incentives to favor AI models that are inefficient (consuming more compute) rather than efficient. The historical cautionary tale is Boeing — the late-1990s merger approval allowing Boeing to buy up its last US rival, McDonnell Douglas, led down a path where Boeing's planes started falling apart and people died. The Trump administration's AI policy embodies the national-champions risk: the CEOs of the major technology companies have made currying favor with the White House a top business priority, and there is a very serious risk of regulatory capture — rules favoring the biggest incumbents at the expense of startups and newer firms that may have better ideas. The empirical evidence on the Schumpeter-Arrow debate shows that monopolies can be good at incremental improvements but the breakthrough, paradigm-shifting innovations have come from outsiders and competitive markets. The fundamental breakthrough that made modern AI possible came from Google — which did nothing with it until people left and brought it to other places [33]. See [Tech Stack Diplomacy]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/tech-stack-diplomacy.html) and [AI Military Race]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/ai-military-race.html).

The international consequences of oligarchic capture are visible in declining global respect. Krugman documents the spectacular gap between Trump's boasts and the empirical record: Pew's Global Attitudes Survey shows that people in many countries now view [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) more positively than the US — a finding confirmed by overseas travel and by the behavior of governments. Around the world, including nations that used to be America's most loyal allies, governments are scrambling to reduce their dependence, economic and military, on an America that can't be trusted and has proved itself far weaker than anyone imagined. The "America is respected" lie serves two functions: it matters to Trump personally — he lives for external validation, and the delusion that the world is in awe of his prowess is key to his fragile sense of self-worth — and the boasts about international reputation are part of the case Trump is building for disrupting the midterm elections: since he is doing an incredible job, nobody should get in his way or place limits on his power. At this point none of what Trump says is really about persuasion — it is all about laying the groundwork for his attempt to destroy democracy [32]. ## Domestic Dynamics: Social Policy and the AI Backlash

The degradation of the American social safety net unfolds alongside an emerging political backlash against the AI industry — two dynamics that, together, reshape the domestic landscape. [Richardson]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/heather-cox-richardson.html) documents the stark contrast between the Democrats' 2021 American Rescue Plan — whose expanded child tax credit lifted 3.7 million children out of poverty before it expired — and the Republicans' 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), which extended over $4.5 trillion in tax breaks for the wealthy and corporations while cutting more than $1 trillion from social welfare programs. The OBBBA increased the child tax credit, but by less than an inflation adjustment would have; the benefits went mostly to the richest fifth of Americans, dropping to zero by the poorest fifth. Between the OBBBA's passage in July 2025 and March 2026, more than 4 million people lost access to the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program — at least a quarter of them children. Those losses will mount in 2027, after the midterm elections, when states must assume a much larger share of SNAP costs [28]. The Republican response to the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html)'s unpopularity has been a $95 billion budget framework — released July 15, 2026 — to provide another $73 billion in military funding for the war, a $12 billion bailout for farmers hurt by [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html)'s tariff wars, and $10 billion to enact aspects of the SAVE America voter-suppression measure that Trump has been unable to convince Congress to pass. Republicans are maneuvering around the Democrats through budget reconciliation, a process that needs no Democratic votes; even Republicans don't appear eager to throw more money at the Iran War before the midterms, given the Pentagon's opacity about costs and the White House's refusal to confer with Congress. Representative Warren Davidson called the plan "dead on arrival" [28].

Compounding these welfare cuts, a dramatic shift in American public opinion on AI data centers has emerged as a new political dynamic. According to Heatmap polling, Americans went from being evenly split on local data center construction (43% support / 42% oppose in August 2025) to overwhelmingly opposed (21% support / 71% oppose) by spring 2026 — a 50-point swing that may be the fastest opinion shift in modern American politics [24]. The backlash has a strong NIMBY core: the top concerns are water use and electricity prices, which unite Americans across party and age lines. When data center projects are actually canceled, the reason is typically noise. Among younger opponents, suspicion of AI itself drives opposition. The movement draws on deeper anxieties: a few tech executives perceived as laying claim to the country's future, communities feeling powerless against forces they cannot control, and the sense that decades of computing innovation have left the world shallower rather than richer [24]. The political implications are significant — the backlash is underwater among Republicans, independents, and Democrats. Democrats are already running against AI data centers in midterms, benefiting from voter instinct to reach for Democrats when an industry appears out of control. However, the coalition fractures when moving beyond local concerns: Democrats care about environmental impact; Republicans do not. If the data center were powered by renewable energy, Democrats would support it and Republicans would oppose it. Bernie Sanders has proposed a national moratorium, though his rationale (existential AI risks) differs from the NIMBY concerns driving most opposition [24].

The demographic foundation compounds these tensions. The US has been below the replacement fertility rate of 2.1 children per woman for nearly two decades (1.6 in 2024). [Mexico]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/latin-america.html), the top provider of immigrant workers, has been below replacement for about a decade (1.9 in 2024). [Africa]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/africa.html)'s working-age population is projected to rise from ~850 million today to over 1.5 billion by 2050, accounting for 85% of the global workforce increase — positioning Africa as a sought-after source of workers for shrinking developed economies [20].

Congressional resistance to the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) is growing. [Robert Pape]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/robert-pape.html) documents that the House voted 214–208 to require congressional approval for further military action against [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html), with four Republicans joining Democrats; the Senate rejected its parallel measure 49–47. In June, the Senate voted 50–48 to halt unauthorized US military action against Iran — the first such Senate approval during the conflict. Public opinion has turned sharply against the war: AP-NORC polling found 64% saying the war was not worth it, 72% identifying preventing oil and gas price rises as an important foreign-policy goal, and 40% naming gasoline prices as a major stress source (up from 27% pre-war). Quinnipiac found 60% opposed to US military action (the highest since the war began), 59% lacking confidence in [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html)'s Iran deal, and 45% saying the US is weaker. Reuters/Ipsos recorded [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html)'s approval at 33% — the lowest of his presidency — with only 20% supporting the war and 80% expecting it to last a long time [63]. ## Structural Foundations: Energy & the Dollar

[The Honest Sorcerer]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/the-honest-sorcerer.html) debunks the "energy independence" narrative: the US is a net crude oil importer. NGLs are not oil and cannot make diesel or jet fuel. US refineries need heavy crude from [Canada]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/canada.html), [Venezuela]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/latin-america.html), and [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html). During the Iran war, an armada of 121 empty tankers headed to the US to export refined fuels, draining the [SPR]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/spr-depletion-crisis.html) to approximately 349 million barrels — approaching 1983 lows. Post-peak shale is evident from declining rig counts [10]. Tooze identifies the US AI boom as a "K-shaped expansion" — one of four disrupters of the 2026 world economy. The boom is sucking in imports at a furious rate, but from [Taiwan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/taiwan.html) and Mexico, not from China. The US-China bilateral trade truce is holding; the bilateral deficit has narrowed dramatically. The US has effectively excluded the leading edge of China's export drive with 100% tariffs on Chinese EVs, making China Shock 2.0 largely a Europe problem. Trump is dismantling the IRA's energy transition subsidies even as the AI boom generates unprecedented asset price inflation [12].

Tooze argues that the US dollar system has undergone five distinct metamorphoses, each with its own political economy. The current regime — Dollar #5, the "profit dollar" — is sustained not by official reserve accumulation by foreign central banks (as in Bretton Woods 2.0, ~2000-2015) but by private capital inflows attracted by the dominance of US financial markets. America's unequal, K-shaped economy is the "honeypot of global capitalism." Since 2015, Beijing has imposed tight capital controls trapping $50-60 trillion; America's deficits are financed by inflows from Europe, South Korea, Taiwan, and Japan — not China. The dollar is no longer a "reserve currency" in the traditional sense but a promise of liquidity and a vehicle for unfettered capital accumulation. See [Monetary System Transition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/monetary-system-transition.html) [23]. The implication for US power: the current dollar regime depends on the attractiveness of US private assets and the institutional stability that underwrites them. If Trump's institutional degradation undermines that attractiveness, the "profit dollar" could metamorphose again — but not into a post-dollar regime, rather another mutation of the dollar system. The dedollarization trend visible in reserve data captures the shift from Dollar #4 to #5 (official reserves declining as private inflows take over) rather than a flight from the dollar per se [23].

# Analysis

The United States is experiencing a compounding crisis: military overstretch, diplomatic isolation, domestic political dysfunction, and a technology race where its lead is narrow and poorly leveraged. The [global system rupture]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-rupture.html) is not merely happening to the US — it is being accelerated by American decisions that diverge from strategic logic. The [energy and resource]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/energy-and-resources.html) foundation of US power remains robust in absolute terms but is diminishing relative to rivals, particularly China, which uses nearly twice as much energy and houses four times the population.

Tooze identifies two of his four disrupters as American: Trump's trade policy rampage and US fiscal incontinence. The AI boom — the third disrupter — is centered in the US but its benefits are K-shaped, enriching a tiny elite while the institutional fabric degrades. The fourth disrupter, China's economic gear-shift, interacts with the other three to produce a world economy in which "two powerful forces are at work in the same world, at the same time" but are "to a degree separated and disconnected" [12][13].

## Military Overstretch and the Iran War Legacy

The [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) has become a strategic trap whose costs compound across every dimension — operational, material, institutional, and domestic. O'Brien documents two extraordinary outbursts from the US administration in mid-July 2026 that exemplify the strategic cost of the "consequence-free world" the US has chosen to inhabit. Trump's July 11 tweet threatening to "completely decimate and destroy all areas of Iran" — "PRAISE BE TO ALLAH" — should Iran attempt to assassinate him is juxtaposed against Trump's own celebration of the assassination of Khamenei at the start of the war. The US pursued and legitimized a policy of leader assassination; the same policy now makes Trump a legitimate target for Iran. In parallel, Undersecretary of State Elbridge Colby — who for years has been lambasting Europeans for not doing more to defend themselves and leading the call for withdrawal of forces from Europe — posted a tweet thread insisting that a "collective middle powers strategy" is a distraction and that "access to the American DIB is a privilege, not a right." The thread insults Europeans as too weak to act independently while simultaneously threatening and pleading with them not to do so. O'Brien reads the thread as panic: it is finally dawning on the administration that actions have consequences — its own Europe-withdrawal policies have produced the strategic autonomy it now pleads with Europeans not to pursue — a self-destroying case. See [Donald Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) and [Layered Security]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/layered-security.html) [34].

The operational trap is sharpened by the political clock. [Robert Pape]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/robert-pape.html) argues (July 20, 2026) that America is unlikely to reopen the [Strait of Hormuz]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html) before the November midterm elections. He draws a critical distinction between tactical and strategic victory: tactical success measures military performance — launchers destroyed, attacks declined — while strategic success requires the political objective of restoring normal commercial shipping. The judge of strategic victory is not the Pentagon but the market: shipping companies, insurers, and commodity traders must conclude that transiting Hormuz is routine again. Only 50–100 drones or missiles are needed to swarm ships, and [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) possesses tens of thousands; a single successful strike can reverse months of restored confidence. For the United States, the suppression problem is a force-allocation trap: guaranteeing passage requires persistent coverage of a long waterway against dispersed, cheap launch systems, so even high attrition rates do not free scarce air-defense, intelligence, and strike capacity for other theaters [36]. The detailed geography, inventories, and operational requirements are kept in [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html). The ground option creates the same institutional problem at larger scale: a nominally limited landing would redirect US effort from reopening shipping to protecting and reinforcing a vulnerable force ashore, turning a narrow objective into an expanding commitment [37].

The escalation dynamic compounds with Iran's nuclear hardening. In Breaking Analysis #7 (July 21, 2026), Pape identifies a new strategic problem: [Israeli]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html) intelligence reports that Iran has moved thousands of uranium-enrichment centrifuges into tunnels beneath Pickaxe Mountain near Natanz, buried 300–450 feet under solid rock — beyond the reach of existing conventional bunker-busting munitions. The facility's depth creates what Pape calls the Ground Power Dilemma. The US and Israeli bombing campaign achieved tactical successes throughout the war, but Iran responded by moving enrichment deeper underground. This is the classic Escalation Trap mechanics: tactical success that deepens strategic entrapment. Airpower did not solve the nuclear problem; it transformed it. As Iran's nuclear assets become harder to destroy, airstrikes yield diminishing strategic returns while pressure for more dangerous military options grows. If Washington believes Pickaxe Mountain has made Iran's nuclear program invulnerable, that belief alone creates pressure for broader military action regardless of what inspectors might discover — the choice narrows to two unpalatable options: tolerate a hardened nuclear program, or accept far greater risks of operations beyond an air campaign — including ground operations or nuclear options. Pape draws on the 2003 Iraq WMD precedent to identify a fear-driven escalation logic: leaders escalate based on what they believe adversaries are capable of, not what later proves true. Uncertainty favors escalation — the absence of verifiable information generates its own coercive momentum. The political clock linkage intensifies the trap: if the US cannot reopen Hormuz before the November midterms, pressure for broader escalation including attacks on deeply buried nuclear facilities will grow [45]. See [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) and [Escalation Theory Critique]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/escalation-theory-critique.html).

The material cost compounds across both military and domestic dimensions. [O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) documents a crisis of munitions depletion as the war enters its fifth month of renewed operations: THAAD interceptor stocks, already low before the latest round of strikes, were 53–80% expended by the April ceasefire. Patriot PAC-3/MSE stocks were 45–61% expended over the same period. An anonymous US official told O'Brien: "We do not have enough to safely sustain operations, and I don't think the White House is aware of that." The Pentagon is panicking, seeking billions from Congress — but Congress remains inert [44]. [Richardson]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/heather-cox-richardson.html) documents the Pentagon's parallel fiscal crisis: the Navy and Air Force are running out of operational funds by end of July, diverting equipment and maintenance money and canceling exercises, while urgently seeking a $67 billion supplemental. Sen. Ossoff's confrontation of Defense Secretary Hegseth — pressing whether his earlier claims that Iran's missile threat was "destroyed" and "combat ineffective" were truthful, and receiving only deflection — revealed the gap between declared military success and operational reality [41]. The War Powers Act has not been invoked 144 days into the war, no press briefings are being held, and CNN reported on the Pentagon actively suppressing press coverage [44]. The China implications are severe: combined Patriot and THAAD inventories stand at approximately 1,500 interceptors. With the standard two-interceptors-per-target doctrine, that yields perhaps 1,000 successful intercepts — against a [Chinese]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) missile arsenal estimated at roughly 3,500. O'Brien now assesses that the US might not win even the opening battles of a China war after the Iran-era depletion. US bases in Guam and the Marianas could be devastated at the start of a Pacific conflict. A "short war scenario" — in which the US runs out of systems and suffers such high losses it cannot continue — has become plausible. The war of choice against Iran is degrading readiness for the primary great-power competitor in real time [44]. See [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html), [Taiwan Standoff]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/taiwan-standoff.html), and [Great Power Competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html).

This military depletion unfolds against the degradation of the American social safety net. Under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, 440,000 Arizonans lost SNAP benefits — the deepest food-assistance cuts in US history — with expanded work rules, costs shifted to states, and Arizona laying off a third of caseworkers. Agriculture Secretary Rollins claimed fraud, but error rates reflect classification mistakes, not fraud. [RFK Jr.]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) announced withholding over $1 billion in Medicaid funding from California and Minnesota on unproven fraud claims: California had already lost $1.3 billion since May, now facing $867 million more; Minnesota had $243 million withheld since February, with $200 million more added. The National Park Service is being systematically dismantled under Interior Secretary Burgum — staff slashed, funds starved, political narrative imposed on historical interpretation. TSA began reverting to pre-9/11 private security screening at Des Moines and Tampa — the very model that 9/11 demonstrated was vulnerable [41]. A counterpoint emerged in Sen. Andy Kim's MediKids proposal — Medicaid-for-all-children from birth to age 26, addressing 4.4 million uninsured and 23 million underinsured children — endorsed by medical associations [41].

The cultural and institutional dimension of this overstretch is equally damaging. O'Brien argues that the US military is culturally preparing for failure by celebrating the qualities that do not win wars while neglecting those that do. Wars are won by the smartest, most adaptable, best-equipped soldiers who believe they are not being sacrificed for nothing. The historical record is unambiguous: the bravest soldiers of World War II — the Japanese — lost. Italian soldiers were brave but underequipped. US and British soldiers, who had the highest survival rates, prevailed through superior logistics, technology, and adaptability, not superior valor. Yet the Department of War under Secretary Hegseth is stressing all the wrong things — romantic sacrifice narratives instead of education, technological sophistication, and thoughtfulness. Hegseth has long emphasized hyper-masculine bravery as a military virtue, citing E.B. Sledge's Pacific campaign accounts on the 2024 Kelly podcast and in speeches to generals. The most emblematic manifestation is Hegseth's announcement of testosterone screening for soldiers — what O'Brien calls the "High-T Department of War." The policy equates warfighting capacity with testosterone levels, despite the medical reality that testosterone boosting is linked to infertility. The institutional prioritization of testosterone and sacrifice rhetoric over the logistics, technology, and education that actually win wars is not merely a cultural eccentricity — it is a component of the broader strategic incoherence that has produced the Iran debacle, the $1.5 trillion budget without a strategy, and the AI-military integration gap [43]. See [Revolution in Military Affairs]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/revolution-in-military-affairs.html) and [Defense Industrial Resilience]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/defense-industrial-resilience.html).

## The Defense Budget and Acquisition Paradox

The Trump administration's FY 2027 defense request — roughly $1.15 trillion in baseline spending plus $350 billion in reconciliation and a $67 billion supplemental, combining toward a $1.5 trillion headline — was assessed by a Brookings Institution panel in July 2026 with a devastating consensus: the budget was constructed backward. Todd Harrison (AEI) argued that the number was set first — an arbitrary 5%-of-GDP target declared on Truth Social — and the Pentagon was then told to fill it at the last minute. The tell is the absence of hard tradeoffs: the request buys fourth-generation F-15s, fifth-generation F-35s, and sixth-generation fighters simultaneously, a sure sign that no prioritization occurred. Harrison flagged a $54 billion line item in the Defense Autonomous Warfare Group (DAWG) — 243 times the prior year's appropriation, exceeding the entire Marine Corps budget — with almost no program detail: no quantities, no specific drone types, no articulated concept of use. The battleship program drew equal scorn: a clean-sheet design starting in FY 2027, lead-ship procurement in FY 2028, and full-rate production at one per year by FY 2031 — a timeline Harrison said does not pass the laugh test, likely setting up the Navy to spend billions chasing a presidential whim before canceling it. He likened the need for congressional budget process reform to the early 1970s, when the system last required fundamental restructuring [38].

Mara Karlin (SAIS) highlighted the strategic incoherence: the Trump National Defense Strategy breaks with decades of bipartisan threat consensus by prioritizing the Western Hemisphere and alleged narco-terrorists, circumscribing China to the first island chain, downgrading Europe, and barely mentioning the Middle East — even as the administration launched the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html). The paradox is stark: if the strategy genuinely centers on the Western Hemisphere and a narrow China focus, it could justify a quite small defense budget — not $1.5 trillion at World War levels. The number is not merited by the strategy, and the strategy is not merited by the number. David Wessel (Brookings) framed the fiscal dimension: the administration is proposing record defense spending against an unsustainable fiscal trajectory while avoiding the tradeoffs — spending cuts elsewhere or tax increases — that budgeting demands. Equally troubling, Wessel argued, is the erosion of public trust in the military. The nonpartisan character of the armed forces — the foundation of democratic civilian control — is being degraded by Secretary Hegseth's personnel decisions, which are politicizing the officer corps in ways that compound the institutional damage documented throughout this concept. See [Defense Industrial Resilience]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/defense-industrial-resilience.html) and [Donald Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) [38].

[Aurelien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/aurelien.html) argues that the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) has lost the ability to determine how crises unfold, clinging to outdated concepts and massively overestimating Western norms. The US political culture claims credit for outcomes it had little influence over — Washington's system is so large and complex that the rest of the world has only a walk-on part in its internal struggles. It is career suicide in Washington to suggest that anyone else has agency, especially the locals. This consensual hallucination is attractive until it stubs its toe on reality [40]. The F-47 air superiority program demonstrates failure to learn from [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html) and [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html). An air-superiority aircraft implies an environment where other aircraft contest that superiority. If opponents are increasingly moving to missiles to dominate airspace, then the era of the manned fighter may be ending. The enormous inertia of the defense system continues moving in the direction of what is already known — the F-47 proceeds as though the lessons of the current conflicts have not happened. The West no longer knows what it wants military forces for, or how it would employ them. "Defending against Russia" is a slogan, not a mission — the result of uncritically reviving tired clichés from the political folklore of the 1930s to the 1980s, because that is all its leaders know. The West follows fashions in military thinking (drones are the latest) but shows no sign of willingness to undertake fundamental reviews of its strategy. See [Revolution in Military Affairs]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/revolution-in-military-affairs.html) [40].

Against this backdrop of strategic incoherence, Jacob Green argues that the "Make-or-Buy line" in DoD software acquisition has shifted fundamentally with the arrival of agentic AI. The secretary of defense's November 2025 commercial-first acquisition reform directive assumed a world where building custom software was slow and expensive, making commercial purchase the default. Green's empirical demonstration overturns that premise: using an accredited agentic AI development environment, he built a 60,000-page legal-regulatory knowledge base wrapped in a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server — 9,500 lines of code — in under 20 hours of focused work at roughly $1,700 in model inference costs. A traditional COCOMO estimate for the same deliverable ran 6,000–7,000 engineering hours (12–13 months with a four-person team) at $840,000–$1.2 million through a comparable Army small business prototype award. Agentic AI is turning elite coding into a ubiquitous commodity delivered at previously unfathomable speed for pennies on the dollar [39]. Green's prescription: procure cloud infrastructure, agentic harnesses, and model access commercially; then empower existing DoD development organizations — Army Software Factory, Science and Technology Centers, program offices — to build mission-specific applications with full government data ownership and no recurring licensing. The financial logic is stark: $178 billion in venture funding flowed to top-tier AI model companies in Q1 2026 alone — over three times the $58.5 billion DoD requested for the entire FY 2027 AI and CJADC2 portfolio. The department cannot match industry's model development cadence and should not try; it should buy access to the best models and redirect its resources toward building applications on top of them [39].

Green identifies three architectural enablers. First, AI gateway services provide a single model-agnostic endpoint, allowing users to select the best model for each task while consolidating monitoring, compliance, and pricing — a deliberate hedge against the volatile cadence of model capability growth. Second, the Model Context Protocol — an open-source standard adopted industry-wide including by major data platform vendors — enables plug-and-play data access: DoD can build MCP servers around its authoritative data sources, allowing any compliant agentic application to retrieve data in real time without costly system-to-system integrations. This realizes the long-sought Data Mesh vision, where data lives in its functional domain system and is accessible as a product through agent-to-agent interactions — divesting the costly and duplicative back ends of monolithic platforms (the $2.3 billion earmarked for Maven Smart System and Joint Fires Network in FY 2027 alone illustrates the recurring licensing burden). Third, pre-accredited development and production environments with streamlined onboarding would let internal development teams deliver value on day one rather than after a 6-to-18-month authorization wait, compressing timelines more than any vendor-side reform [39]. Green also proposes introducing internal market forces: reimbursable funding models and a central system where program offices submit work requests that DoD development organizations bid on, incentivizing efficient value creation through cross-service competition without mandated personnel reductions. Open-source tools — Google's Gemma, Hermes Agent, Open Code — offer additional capability with no licensing costs, subject to the same code-scanning and red-teaming scrutiny applied to any software. The broader strategic context connects directly to the [AI Military Race]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/ai-military-race.html): the US lead in AI model development is real but narrow, and Green's framework suggests that the integration gap with [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) could be closed not by building better models but by building better applications faster and cheaper than the current acquisition system permits. See [AI Military Race]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/ai-military-race.html) and [Defense Industrial Resilience]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/defense-industrial-resilience.html) [39].

## Economic Vulnerabilities: The AI Bubble and Tech-Stock Risk

The AI boom that Tooze frames as a world-transforming, K-shaped expansion [12][18] rests on foundations that may be far shakier than the trillions wagered on it suggest. Ed Zitron (*Where's Your Ed At*) argues that the US stock market and, to a significant extent, the global economy rest on the continued solvency of OpenAI — a company with no viable business model that accounts for $748 billion of the remaining performance obligations of Microsoft, Amazon, and Oracle. The "AI bubble" is not the product of return on investment but of a cult-like psychosis that infected some of the most powerful and wealthy individuals and institutions, producing the greatest capital misallocation in history. Hyperscalers (Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Meta) sank more than a trillion dollars into AI capex without ever showing a single dollar of profit, justifying literally anyone investing in AI data centers under the logic that "the largest companies in the world couldn't be wrong" — even though the reason they were doing so was to expand capacity for OpenAI and Anthropic, whom the hyperscalers themselves incubated. Zitron's thesis: if OpenAI collapses — which he considers an inevitability given its loss-laden economics, doomed subscriptions, pathetic advertising revenue, and the absence of measurable AI ROI — it will be the "Lehman Brothers of the AI bubble," seizing up AI data center debt, pulling back compute demand, and calling into question the rationality of investing in any AI startup. The Bank of England governor Andrew Bailey warned that the bursting of the AI bubble would have effects significant enough to change British monetary policy [35].

The downstream consumer effect Zitron documents is a "memory crisis": Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron (the DRAM triopoly controlling 90%+ of the world's memory) diverted manufacturing supply from consumer RAM to high-bandwidth memory for AI GPUs, jacking up the price of laptops, smartphones, consoles, and consumer electronics. Micron posted 84.9% gross margins in Q3 2026. SK Hynix's chairman warned the shortage would last until 2030; a Bank of America report said SK Hynix may add only a sixth of its planned capacity by 2028. The price of consumer electronics will be inflated for the foreseeable future, even if the AI bubble bursts. See [AI Military Race]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/ai-military-race.html) and [Tech Stack Diplomacy]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/tech-stack-diplomacy.html) [35].

## Strategic Posture in the Indo-Pacific

The US-Japan alliance is adapting to a new form of sustained short-of-war pressure from [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) and [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) that falls below the threshold of armed conflict but steadily erodes Japan's security environment. Aizawa identifies the December 2025 joint strategic bomber flight as a turning point: timed to China's political interests, with a coercive route extending toward Tokyo, and involving nuclear-capable platforms. The alliance has responded with bilateral exercises — two B-52s with six JASDF fighters in December 2025, and four B-52s with eleven JASDF fighters in February 2026 — but Aizawa argues that these represent deterrence responses to what is increasingly a compellence problem. The boundary between deterrence and compellence has blurred: China can now apply military pressure with Russian support without crossing the armed conflict threshold, and the alliance's current framework — built around Flexible Deterrent Options — does not adequately address this gray zone. Aizawa calls for shared threat perceptions, reaffirmed common interests, and examination of the full range of response approaches including compellence [46].

The [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) has directly undermined US force posture in the [Indo-Pacific]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/indo-pacific.html), creating tensions within the alliance. Ueki documents how the 31st Marine Expeditionary Unit and amphibious warship USS Tripoli were deployed from US Forces Japan to the Middle East, and Marine Commandant General Eric Smith testified that the gap in the Marine Corps' ability to deter adversaries in the Indo-Pacific remains unfilled — prompting public and legislative concern in [Japan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/japan.html). US munitions depletion from the Iran war — approximately half of key munitions used in 39 days — has cascading effects on allied readiness. Tomahawk deliveries to Japan have been delayed by up to four years, impeding Japan's plans to rapidly strengthen its counterstrike capability. The structural problem is an offense-defense cost imbalance: 1,900 to 3,000 interceptors consumed in less than two weeks of the Iran war against a production capacity of 600 Patriot PAC-3 per year, while [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) dominates drone production through companies like DJI (over 70 percent market share). The Hegseth-Koizumi Operation Supercharge agreement in May 2026 — accelerating codevelopment and coproduction of SM-3 Block IIA and AMRAAM missiles — represents a structural shift toward allied industrial-base cooperation, but the production timelines mean near-term gaps will persist [47]. See [Japan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/japan.html), [Taiwan Standoff]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/taiwan-standoff.html), and [Defense Industrial Resilience]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/defense-industrial-resilience.html).

## The Four-Pillar Erosion Framework

[Paul Krugman]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/paul-krugman.html) argues that [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) has "shattered American power" with astonishing speed, dismantling four residual US advantages that persisted until roughly 2024. First, **science and cutting-edge technology leadership** — Trump officials orchestrated attacks on scientists, driving talent toward Canada, Europe, and [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html). Second, **military technological dominance** — Secretary Hegseth's "pro-testosterone, anti-intellectual leadership" has driven the military's reputation into a nose-dive; a CSIS December 2025 report documented that the Chinese supply chain underpins modern drone warfare. Third, **dollar weaponization** — Iran's ability to sell oil rested on payment in yuan; ships paying Hormuz passage also paid in yuan or cryptocurrency, meaning the dollar's coercive power has been "greatly reduced." Fourth, **the alliance system** — Trump torpedoed alliances on day one, demanding Canada become the 51st state and Denmark hand over Greenland. All four pillars were squandered without adversary action; the erosion is self-inflicted [48].

The Pew favorability collapse quantifies the damage. In 12 countries surveyed, [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html)'s favorability surpassed the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) between 2023 and 2026. The sharpest swings occurred in core allies: Germany (US favorability fell from 57% to 27%, while China's rose from 19% to 33%), Sweden (55% to 19% vs. 12% to 27%), and Australia (52% to 24% vs. 13% to 31%). The pattern is not a Chinese charm offensive but American self-destruction — the crossover is driven by the collapse of US standing rather than the rise of Chinese popularity. On manufacturing, China overtook the US in 2010; the US and EU combined still produce more, but that alliance advantage is fading. The cumulative assessment: "When future historians write about this tale of self-immolation, they may well do so in Mandarin" [48]. See [Great Power Competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html) and [Sanctions and Financial Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/sanctions-and-financial-warfare.html).

## Munitions Depletion and the Window of Vulnerability

[Mark F. Cancian]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/mark-f-cancian.html) and [Chris H. Park]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/chris-h-park.html) (CSIS) provide the systematic structural analysis underlying the munitions depletion that [O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) documented operationally. The US has expended approximately half of its initial inventory for three key munitions — Patriot, THAAD, and PrSM — and about one-third for the rest (JASSM, SM-2/3/6, and Tomahawk). The remaining stocks suffice for any plausible scenario in the war against [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html), but the risk lies in potential future wars, particularly with [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) — but potentially also [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) or North Korea. A [multiyear rebuild](https://www.csis.org/analysis/rebuilding-us-missile-inventory-multiyear-project) now looms [49].

The depletion did not begin with the Iran war. The post-Cold War strategic shift from great-power competition to two limited regional conflicts (notional adversaries: Iraq and North Korea) reduced munitions procurement substantially. Expectations began changing only in 2014 (Crimea) and became pressing in 2022 (full-scale Ukraine invasion). The industrial base was restructured for efficient peacetime production, not surge. In 1993, Deputy Secretary of Defense William Perry gathered defense executives for the "Last Supper," telling them to merge or diversify. The number of aerospace and defense prime contractors decreased from 51 to 5; the whole structure got smaller. Surge capability was regarded as unnecessary and wasteful, leaving very little spare capacity when DOD decided to surge production in 2022 [49].

Munitions compete poorly in the Pentagon's budget process. Platforms (aircraft, ships, armored vehicles) have the advantage of operating for 30 years, being highly visible, and contributing to deterrence. Munitions go into a bunker and only come out if there is a war or their service life expires. Thus munitions have often served as bill payers for higher-priority needs despite recognition of their importance [49].

The consumption pattern across recent Middle East wars compounds the problem. The Biden-era [Houthi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houthi_movement) campaign (Operation Poseidon Archer) used at least 135 Tomahawk missiles and hundreds of SM-2/3/6 interceptors. The [Twelve Day War]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/twelve-day-war.html) expended 150 THAAD interceptors and 24 Tomahawks. Additional strikes included 16 Tomahawks against ISIS sites in [Nigeria]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/nigeria.html) on Christmas Day 2025 and several dozen JASSMs in the Venezuela raid of January 2026. The immediate problem is not money, but time: munitions take two to four years from contract to delivery. Current deliveries reflect funding before FY 2024. Until production equals demand — several years in the future — a window of vulnerability persists [49]. See [Defense Industrial Resilience]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/defense-industrial-resilience.html) and [Twelve Day War]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/twelve-day-war.html).

## Nuclear Testing Posture and the Stockpile Stewardship Program

[Philipp Rombach]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/philipp-rombach.html) (SWP) documents the domestic US nuclear testing debate alongside the international compliance crisis. [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) announced on October 30, 2025 that nuclear testing would resume, citing Russian and Chinese testing activities. Secretary of Energy Chris Wright quickly rolled back the announcement, but the question has remained whether the United States will soon resume testing in the Nevada desert. The Heritage Foundation's Project 2025 report had called for the immediate resumption of nuclear testing, arguing that reliance solely on computer-based methods — many dependent on Cold War-era data — is inadequate given aging plutonium pits and the ongoing modernization program [50].

The timeline for a return to testing is set out in Presidential Decision Directive 15 (PDD-15), issued by President Clinton. The National Nuclear Security Administration (NNSA) could conduct a basic test with minimal diagnostic instrumentation under relaxed environmental requirements within 6–10 months. A fully instrumented test would require 24–36 months. Tests for development of new nuclear weapon systems would require up to five years [50].

Since the testing moratorium began in 1992, the United States has conducted 34 subcritical tests without a self-sustaining chain reaction at the Nevada National Security Site, and plans to increase to three tests per year by the end of the decade. Under the NNSA's Stockpile Stewardship and Management Program, data from subcritical tests validate science-based stockpile stewardship using the most powerful supercomputers at Los Alamos and Livermore, complemented by advanced X-ray and laser facilities including the Dual-Axis Radiographic Hydrodynamic Test Facility (DARHT) and the National Ignition Facility (NIF). The program receives $10–16 billion annually from the Department of Energy. The US adheres to a zero-yield standard: nuclear explosions producing a self-sustaining, supercritical chain reaction are prohibited regardless of yield, while subcritical hydrodynamic tests remain permissible [50].

Rombach's central strategic argument is that US resumption of underground nuclear testing would benefit [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) and [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) more than Washington. The United States has the most extensive nuclear testing history (1,054 tests) and the world's most advanced science-based stockpile stewardship capabilities; it has the most to lose from the erosion of international testing norms. China, with only 45 historical tests using inferior diagnostic instrumentation, would benefit the most from a full resumption. The US has not paid its 2026 CTBTO contributions, undermining the International Monitoring System that detected the alleged Chinese test. See [Nuclear Proliferation and Arms Control]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/nuclear-proliferation-and-arms-control.html) and [China Alleged Nuclear Test]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/china-alleged-june-2020-underground-nuclear-test.html) [50].

## Gray-Zone Organizational Deficit

[The Cipher Brief]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/the-cipher-brief.html) argues that the United States lacks the whole-of-government structure needed for gray-zone competition — the realm of coercion and subversion below armed conflict but outside legitimate statecraft. The US is not organized as a nation to devise gray-zone strategies. The State Department is focused on developing relationships. The National Security Council is hollowed out. Due to issues with legal authorities, neither the Department of War nor the intelligence community can take the lead in implementing whole-of-government strategies. The US government has been arguing over the definition and leadership of gray-zone and information operations for years, leaving a structural deficit [51].

Autocracies have a structural advantage in the gray zone. Non-democratic states are not limited by domestic law and regulation in the same way; their centralized systems allow them to marshal whole-of-state and whole-of-society resources. The US lags behind its autocratic adversaries in the information domain because adversaries have ingrained gray-zone tactics into their doctrines. In democratic societies, the covert nature of gray-zone activities is controversial, and legal and military systems are geared toward seeing conflicts through the binary lens of war and peace with little consideration for the space in between [51].

The historical record shows that gray-zone activities are a longstanding form of statecraft — Cold War "active measures," the Trojan Horse, and Athenian proxy operations against Spartan Helots all fit the pattern. The Cipher Brief's prescription: publicly put adversaries on notice, appoint a seasoned NSC professional to coordinate integrating USG gray-zone activities against specific adversaries, and use trained DoD and IC planners working with the rest of government to develop government-wide plans implemented at the White House. The current NSC Director for Cognitive Advantage is a step, but cognitive advantage is only one part of gray-zone activity. See [Endless Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/endless-warfare.html) and [Cognitive Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/cognitive-warfare.html) [51].

## Service-Reliability Drag from Sustained Operations Tempo

Retired Air Force Lt. Gen. [S. Clinton Hinote]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/s-clinton-hinote.html) and Gen. [James Slife]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/james-slife.html), speaking at a CSIS event on July 23, 2026, identified a delayed cumulative effect on military readiness from the heavy operations tempo of the [Iran war]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html). Slife stated that the pace of operations over the last year has probably exceeded the Air Force and the Navy's ability to maintain levels of readiness going forward, warning that "there is always a delayed impact to these kinds of heavy operations" and that the next three-to-five years will require close attention [52].

Hinote highlighted the F-15E rescue mission — the recovery of the weapon-system officer shot down over Iran — as a complex multi-service operation requiring tremendous integration across multiple types of forces in a very difficult time frame. He suggested it may stand as one of the best rescue operations ever conducted, and that it may drive a decade of reform analogous to the reforms that followed the troubled April 1980 Iran rescue attempt (Eagle Claw). The contrast is notable: Eagle Claw was "so bad that it led to reforms for the next decade in the United States military"; the F-15E rescue may prove equally consequential in driving institutional adaptation — but in the opposite direction, as a model of success rather than failure [52].

The broader strategic assessment from both officers reinforced the existing analysis of the Iran war's strategic drift. Hinote framed the gap between military and strategic success: the US is accomplishing military objectives — destroying the Iranian Navy, Air Force, air defenses, missile and drone production — but how that translates into strategic success "has been the weakness in this entire operation." The Strait of Hormuz dilemma is structural: the joint force can prevent 98% of attacks on shipping, but the last 2% that it cannot stop is what ship insurers cannot accept — and as long as Iran has the will, it will have some capability. Slife noted that US and Israeli forces have pursued very different target sets, reflecting different national aims: Israel's "mowing the grass" theory versus the US coercion campaign seeking an open Strait and abandoned nuclear aspirations — aims that may have worked against each other [52]. See [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html).

## The Bessent "Deal Imminent" Pattern and the 2028/2029 Exit Standard

On August 5, 2026, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told CNBC's Squawk Box that a deal to open the Strait of Hormuz was possible "today or tomorrow." Global markets rallied (S&P +1.8 percent, Nasdaq +2.6 percent) and oil fell over 5 percent. [Robert Pape]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/robert-pape.html) reframes the announcement not as a breakthrough but as a recurring pattern: US administration promises of imminent peace, market reaction, and the war continuing. The negotiating parties are arguing over risk, not shipping — the rumored 7 percent cargo-value fee mirrors today's wartime insurance premium, not a benign Malacca-style transit fee. Pape's prescriptive test for a genuine breakthrough is bilateral public confirmation by both Washington and Tehran, which as of writing had not occurred [53].

Pape's August 10 essay crystallizes the framework for judging US policy: not whether [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) can defeat [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html), but whether by January 2029 he has left behind a regional system more stable than the one the US entered in 2026. The standard is a regional balance in which Iran has enough power and incentive to accept balance rather than continue expanding its leverage, and in which [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html), the [UAE]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/uae.html), [Turkey]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/turkey.html), [Pakistan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/pakistan.html), and other regional powers have enough capability to constrain Iran without requiring permanent American dominance. Trump will reject this advice because it sounds like accepting strategic defeat — but that is precisely the psychological trap. The choice is not between victory and withdrawal but between continuing to escalate without a credible theory of political victory and using remaining American leverage to construct something that can last [54]. See [Escalation Theory Critique]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/escalation-theory-critique.html).

## Asymmetric Economic Exposure and Third-Market Resilience

Kiel Institute simulations of a full Hormuz closure show the US losing just −0.07 percent of welfare under the short-run scenario — barely registering economically compared to developing-country losses 10-20 times larger. The US is structurally insulated from the energy chokepoint's direct trade-channel effects, though the model excludes speculative and financial-market dynamics, hoarding, and SPR releases [55].

A complementary Kiel study on third-market competition finds that the US maintained or improved its position relative to other non-Chinese exporters despite China's export expansion. The US excess-loss rate of 67.7 percent is moderate, and the weighted market-share change of only −0.07pp means the US is a net "winner" in the non-Chinese segment — adapting more successfully than [Germany]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/germany.html), [Japan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/japan.html), or the [United Kingdom]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-kingdom.html). Trump 1.0-era protectionism (100 percent tariffs on Chinese EVs) redirected the leading edge of China's export drive toward Europe, shielding the US market from the displacement hitting European manufacturers [56]. See [China Shock and Deindustrialization]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/china-shock-and-deindustrialization.html).

## FIMA Repo Activation and the Exchange Stabilization Fund

The August 3, 2026 joint yen intervention with [Japan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/japan.html) deployed two previously unused peacetime instruments: the Fed's FIMA (Foreign and International Monetary Authorities) repo facility was activated, allowing Japan to borrow dollars against its Treasury portfolio rather than selling it, and Washington sold euros from the Exchange Stabilization Fund. The intervention was motivated as much by protecting the US bond market — where 30-year yields already exceeded 5 percent — as by defending Japan's currency. [Velina Tchakarova]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/velina-tchakarova.html) frames this as a threshold moment: exchange-rate intervention is now formally part of alliance economic security, breaking a 28-year tradition of yen purchases [57]. See [Monetary System Transition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/monetary-system-transition.html).

## War Financing and the Fiscal Ceiling

O'Brien argues that the question of how to pay for the [Iran war]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) — the central question of war — is being ignored. The US is attempting to fight having already accrued a national debt exceeding 120% of GDP, approximately the level at the end of World War II (121.7%). Unlike 1945, when the US committed to reducing the debt (falling to 56% of GDP by 1960), the debt is rising sharply toward a projected 140% of GDP by 2040. Debt service is projected to reach $2.1 trillion annually by 2036, even before accounting for additional war costs. The era of sub-3% 10-year Treasury rates that prevailed from 2011 to 2023 has ended, giving the US a "double whammy" it has never faced: record debt alongside relatively high interest rates [59].

The Pentagon's claimed Iran war cost of $37.5 billion is almost certainly a fraction of the real cost, as the Defense Department simultaneously requested a $67.1 billion supplemental on top of a $150 billion supplemental received the prior year. O'Brien estimates the $37.5 billion figure covers only munitions expended, with the real cost being multiples higher — a deliberate low-balling to minimize the appearance of failure [59]. Meanwhile, Democrats have begun blocking further defense appropriations, and the war's unpopularity among Democratic and Independent voters creates a structural disincentive for continued funding through the midterm elections and potentially beyond [59].

The FIMA repo activation's underlying motivation was self-interest: the Trump administration was desperate to prevent [Japan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/japan.html) from selling US Treasury bonds to defend the yen, which would have pushed up US borrowing costs. The intervention was not beneficence but a worried act of self-preservation in a fragile bond market [59].

## Doctrinal Reset vs. Replenishment

O'Brien argues that framing the US military's problem as one of stock replenishment is fundamentally wrong. The US has spent approximately $9 trillion on defense since 2016, yet the force is degraded — in its worst post-1945 shape. The problem is not merely depleted stocks but doctrinal and cultural damage: the military was structured for short wars and glamorous missions, not sustained high-intensity conflict. Even at pre-Iran-war Patriot and THAAD levels plus a 33% cushion, the US would have had roughly 3,000 Patriot and 600 THAAD interceptors — sufficient against [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) for "a few months (maybe a few weeks)" before stocks dropped below current depleted levels. The Chinese air force operates 3,000+ combat aircraft with improving quality, and Chinese missile stocks number in the many thousands. China's industrial capacity — with 70-80% of global drone manufacturing — compounds the problem; Chinese military drone exports already run 20 times higher than US exports [60].

Simply replenishing stocks would recreate a military that could not fight a war of real length. What is needed is a complete reconstruction of the US understanding of war — the mix of systems, the industrial base, and the doctrinal culture. The cultural damage compounds the material deficit: toadies and sycophants are promoted while good officers keep their heads down, making the military an intellectually terrified institution [60].

Two factors temper the assessment. First, the fundamentals of US power — technology, economy, entrepreneurship, and the capacity to recreate an industrial base — remain intact; this is not Rome in 409 AD. Second, the Iran war has stripped away military complacency: the true weakness of America's position can no longer be hidden. US allies in [Europe]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/europe.html) need to assume they are on their own against [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html), and Indo-Pacific allies must assume the US will be a stunted version of itself for years [60]. See [Defense Industrial Resilience]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/defense-industrial-resilience.html).

## Pacific Military Decline

[Phillips P. O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) argues that the "American Age" in the Western Pacific is over with no prospect of resuscitation. Even a post-2028 administration cannot rebuild a decayed military in under a decade or restore the allied trust that has been squandered. The US foreign policy establishment, in O'Brien's assessment, has effectively become a lobbying group for the [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) administration, refusing to acknowledge what is unfolding in the [Indo-Pacific]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/indo-pacific.html) — a willful blindness that compounds the material decline by preventing any corrective action. ## Domestic Political Cost of the Iran War

[Robert Pape]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/robert-pape.html) argues that a prolonged, costly war with American casualties and no convincing endgame could push Democrats and possibly Republicans to support congressional restrictions on troop deployments, war funding, and military assistance to [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html). The decisive possibility is a bipartisan congressional revolt producing veto-proof majorities — rendering the war politically unsustainable and establishing a precedent that binds future presidents. This would mark the first time Congress has constrained a sitting president's war powers since the Vietnam era, with structural consequences for executive authority in foreign policy.
