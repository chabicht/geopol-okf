# Background

Russia is a revisionist power seeking to overturn the post-Cold War order. Its presidency under [Putin]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/putin.html) has been defined from day one by the Chechen conflict — the second Chechen war was effectively Putin's presidential campaign. The war in [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html) has become the central strategic commitment, consuming manpower and equipment at rates that force trade-offs across all other theaters of operation. Russia anchors the DragonBear axis alongside [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html), coordinating pressure on the Western coalition as part of the broader [Cold War 2]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/cold-war-2.html).

# Current Situation

## The Ukraine War: Costs and Trajectory

The [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html) continues to strain Russian resources. Putin has rejected ceasefire proposals, with demands disconnected from battlefield realities. Ukraine's long-range strikes are reaching Moscow and St. Petersburg, attacking Russian war production infrastructure — not just refineries. The FP-5 Flamingo missile has struck the Titan-Barrikady weapons complex in Volgograd, demonstrating that Ukrainian reach extends deep into Russian industrial territory [6]. In May 2026, Ukraine conducted a major [drone strike on Moscow]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/moscow-drone-strike.html), demonstrating that Putin can no longer protect the capital from war consequences. [Phillips O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) compares the strike to Japan's loss of the Marianas in WWII — the moment when the propaganda narrative collapsed because the war reached the homeland. [CSIS]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/csis.html) notes that Putin dramatically scaled back his annual military parade in May 2026 — a stunning acknowledgement of concerns about Ukrainian long-range strikes and increasing battlefield challenges. Crimea has come under withering attack, forcing Russia to close summer camps and beaches, disrupting fuel supplies, and causing electricity outages [37]. Russia's cumulative casualties exceed 1 million [6].

[CSIS]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/csis.html) data documents approximately 1.4 million battlefield casualties and 450,000 fatalities between February 2022 and June 2026. Russia's monthly casualty rates of 30,000-34,000 in 2026 likely exceed recruitment of ~27,000/month. Russia's territorial control shrank in spring 2026 — a net loss of ~400 sq km in April-May, the first monthly net losses since August 2024. Russian advance rates of 50-90 meters/day are among the slowest in any war over the last century [12]. The Russia-Ukraine casualty ratio has likely risen to nearly 8:1 in the first half of 2026, up from between 2:1 and 3:1 for much of the war — driven in large part by Ukraine's use of drones, including AI-enabled drones, as part of an aggressive air interdiction campaign. According to some estimates, over 90 percent of Russian casualties are from drone attacks rather than human-to-human engagements. The kill zone extends approximately 20–40 kilometers across the front line, saturated with drones that make massed personnel and armor movements extremely dangerous [37]. The [Kiel Institute]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/kiel-institute.html) concludes that Russia's macro stance is "not sustainable": the Q1 2026 budget deficit exceeded the full-year target in three months, the sovereign wealth fund's liquid assets have shrunk to 1.8% of GDP, and war capacity is directly coupled to hydrocarbon export income. Oil and gas revenue fell to its lowest level since 2020. Putin is raising VAT from 20% to 22% and cannot borrow abroad [13][14].

[The Cipher Brief]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/the-cipher-brief.html) argues that a Russian defeat — military exhaustion bleeding into political rupture within the next two years — has moved from a fringe scenario to one that serious governments must insure against. The preparation agenda includes securing ~1,800 strategic warheads, pre-negotiating recognition of borders if the Federation fragments, keeping weapons scientists employed, and defining what "victory" means for Ukraine [16]. Yet [George Friedman]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/geopolitical-futures.html) (GPF) argues that Russia does not have one hand tied behind its back — it is missing a hand. The Russian military has performed poorly in Ukraine, and its ability to mount another attack on a European nation is extremely limited. [Poland]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/europe.html) has developed a substantial, well-armed, and well-trained military — an attack in the Baltics or on Poland would almost certainly trigger a Polish response. The reason Putin has not expanded the war is that any attack on any western-oriented country will likely evolve into a Polish and possibly a US response [20]. Yet despite historically poor performance — casualties, fatalities, and average rates of advance among the worst of any major power in any war since World War II — Russia continues to fight. So long as Putin remains willing to pay the steep price, Russia can tap into a deep manpower pool and a war economy that, while strained, has not yet buckled. The bitter irony, [Seth G. Jones]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/seth-g-jones.html) and [Riley McCabe]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/riley-mccabe.html) argue, is that despite Russia's battlefield challenges and economic vulnerabilities, the United States and Europe have failed to fully wield economic or military pressure [37].

## Economic Strain and Logistics

Despite five years of war and non-stop sanctions, the Russian economy has avoided collapse — but isolation, rising inflation, tight central bank policies, insufficient import substitution, and ongoing Ukrainian attacks have taken a toll. At an economic meeting on April 15, Putin announced that GDP had declined for the second consecutive month and demanded that economic officials urgently restart growth. The Ministry of Economic Development laid out the economy's challenges: a strong ruble, high interest rates, labor shortages, budget constraints, and exhausted reserves [17]. Moscow had hoped to independently build its own technology sector, but brain drain and systemic issues have prevented it. By early 2026, domestic computer-aided design software systems accounted for less than a third of the market; pirated foreign software accounted for the rest. Capital never stopped leaving the country, now going to places like Dubai, Hong Kong, and Tel Aviv. The leader of the Communist Party of Russia warned the State Duma on April 22 that the country would be at risk of revolution if authorities did not halt the economic collapse. Despite these challenges, the defense industry continues to fuel production, and the central bank notes no sharp rise in unemployment or decline in household income [17].

Ukraine's long-range strike campaign has taken approximately 38-42% of Russia's oil refining capacity offline. Approximately 78 of 83 federal subjects have experienced gasoline shortages, with 48 imposing purchasing limits. Russia has banned gasoline and jet fuel exports and is rationing fuel to its own citizens — a petrostate that cannot keep its own drivers in gasoline [14][15]. Zolotova (GPF) reports that after drone attacks in the latter half of June 2026, gasoline production fell by 25 percent year on year. Authorities in several Russian regions announced restrictions on the sale of motor fuel — limits ranging from 20 liters per person in Crimea to 40 liters of gasoline at highway gas stations in Omsk [19]. The Kremlin's fuel imports from other states are further depleting coffers and reducing funds available for the war, and ISW assesses that intensifying gasoline shortages will likely compound broader inflationary pressures and complicate the Kremlin's efforts to lower the key interest rate [15]. The structural consequence is that Russia may have to choose between supplying the domestic market and maintaining petroleum-product exports, affecting prices in a market already exposed to Middle East instability [19].

Russian oil exports surged to 4.11 million barrels per day, the highest since January, filling the gap left by the Hormuz crisis. Sanctions waivers enabled this export surge, illustrating the contradiction between Western declaratory policy and actual energy market behavior. RUSI documents that the US has not adopted a single designation against the Russian shadow fleet since January 2025. The US focus on Iran undermined sanctions on Russia: general licences allowing Russian oil sales during the Hormuz crisis kneecapped G7 price cap efforts just as they were biting (Urals fell below $50/barrel by December 2025). Nearly 30% of Russian crude in April 2026 was transported on G7 tankers. The price cap is exhausted — RUSI recommends replacing it with a full maritime services ban [28]. ISW assesses that Ukraine's strikes are depriving Moscow of the opportunity to benefit from rising global oil prices driven by the Middle East war — a missed revenue stream at precisely the moment when energy markets would otherwise reward Russian exporters. ISW further notes that Russian crude oil exports rose in June 2026 compared to June 2025, as refinery strikes left more crude available for shipment — but this crude-export revenue only partially offsets the lost refined-product income [15].

The shadow fleet — the clandestine network of oil tankers that has become the lifeline of the Russian economy — operates at considerable scale. [Julian Pawlak]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/julian-pawlak.html) (SWP), writing in the German Reservistenverband magazine *Loyal*, documents that approximately three tankers per day transit the Baltic Sea from Primorsk and Ust-Luga to buyers in [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) and India, carrying nearly half of Russia's total oil exports. Together with Novorossiysk on the Black Sea, these three ports handle almost 60 percent of Russia's seaborne oil exports [38]. European boarding actions have increased — more than a dozen shadow fleet vessels have been seized or controlled since January 2026, including French special forces boarding the "Deyna" from a helicopter in March, the Swedish coast guard stopping the "Jin Hui" in May, and British elite units storming the "Smyrtos" in a nighttime operation in June. Yet these actions hit only approximately 2 percent of transits — roughly 10 ships stopped out of approximately 1,000 annual transits through the Baltic and North Sea. International maritime law guarantees the right of innocent passage, and ships can only be stopped with concrete evidence of violations. Many states, including [Germany]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/germany.html), remain hesitant due to the legal and financial consequences: court proceedings have ended in expensive failures, seized ships become ecological time bombs moored off the coast, and foreign crews must be supplied for months [38]. Ukraine's drone campaign has been far more effective. Ukrainian surface and subsurface drones have damaged or disabled 50–60 oil tankers — 4–5 percent of the total shadow fleet of approximately 1,300 tankers, but a significantly higher 25–35 percent loss rate for tankers serving Black Sea routes. The KSE Institute calculates that drone attacks on Ust-Luga and Primorsk alone cost Russia approximately $1.76 billion in oil revenue in March 2026, as tanker departures from Ust-Luga fell from 16–17 per week to just two. Pawlak calls for a more coordinated European approach — a joint line on handling the shadow fleet, using legal hooks such as false flagging — noting that each country still largely pursues its own strategy [38]. See [Shadow Fleet Boarding Wave]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/shadow-fleet-dawn-raids-2026.html) and [Sanctions and Financial Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/sanctions-and-financial-warfare.html).

Despite sanctions, Russian seaport cargo traffic actually increased due to trade reorientation to the Asia-Pacific, Middle East, Africa, and Latin America. Cargo turnover increased by 18% in the Far East, 6% in the Baltic Sea, and 3% in the Azov-Black Sea basin over the past seven years. However, eastern ports are smaller with shoddier infrastructure. In 2025, Russian Railways entered full-blown crisis amid a slowing economy, falling demand, debt, and sanctions, requesting emergency government assistance of 200 billion rubles ($2.8 billion) to cover operating expenses. The Kremlin has emphasized "transport sovereignty" for sustainable access to friendly markets, but foreign lines not controlled by Russian principals still transported 45% of container turnover in the Far East, 75% in the Baltic basin, and 80% in the Azov-Black Sea basin in 2025 [18].

## Military Force and Posture

Greg Whisler and Michael Kofman (War on the Rocks) document the most significant Russian military expansion in decades. Russia began the war with one of its smallest armies in over 100 years — a force of battalion tactical groups optimized for short, local contingencies, short on infantry, with no reserve system. The wartime expansion grew the deployed force to over 700,000 personnel through partial mobilization (300,000 in September 2022) and contract soldier recruitment (~1.3 million over the war). By 2023, the General Staff created two new combined-arms armies, two motorized rifle divisions, and 27 new maneuver brigades and regiments. The force reached over 800 maneuver battalions — roughly four times the invasion force [23]. By 2025, however, force growth had largely halted as monthly recruitment (~35,000) was offset by similar losses. The cracks in the force generation model — rising regional recruitment bonuses becoming fiscally untenable, casualties exceeding recruitment — suggest Moscow has passed its high-water mark for offensive capacity. Much of the expanded force is likely to persist: the Leningrad Military District, re-established opposite Finland and the Baltic states, will host new corps and divisions with expanded precision strike capabilities — a permanent force structure shift on [NATO's]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/organizations/nato.html) borders [23]. Notably, Moscow sustained this expansion primarily through contracted volunteers rather than mass mobilization — the first large-scale, extended war Russia has fought this way. However, the paradox of mass: as Russia fielded more forces, the value of that mass diminished. Ukrainian adaptation in [drone warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/drone-warfare.html), combined with mines, prepared defenses, and artillery, negated traditional manpower advantages [23].

Ukraine's drone strikes are reaching deep into Siberia — a drone threat was declared for the first time ever in Novosibirsk and Omsk, with Ukrainian drones venturing 2,500 kilometers beyond the border to attack the Omsk oil refinery, the largest in Russia [21]. Russia possesses high-quality air defense systems (S-500, A-235 Nudol, Pantsir-SMD), but the challenge is the sheer number needed to protect its vast territory [21]. Approximately 80% of the population lives west of the Urals, and just to cover the line of contact with Ukraine, Russia would need as many as 500 Pantsir units — which it does not have [21]. Ukraine's ability to strike Moscow City shortly after Victory Day and St. Petersburg during SPIEF indicates that Russia lacks sufficient air defenses even for critically important rear areas [15]. Airport closures occur almost daily due to drone threats — cancellations in 2026 were up 4.3-fold year on year [21].

Air defense shortcomings are the structural vulnerability underlying the fuel crisis. Putin acknowledged on June 28 that [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) needs to better coordinate air defenses at all levels, expressing confidence in Russia's ability to boost production — though it is far from clear that Russia can do so quickly [15]. Reports suggest Russia may be experiencing shortages of air defense missiles, possibly because Western sanctions limit access to key components, and the Russian defense-industrial base is unlikely to quickly and sufficiently increase air defense system production in line with Putin's directives [15]. Russia may be moving air defenses from other areas to protect priority sites, but available systems are unlikely to cover all critical infrastructure [15]. The Kremlin failed to address its air defense deficiencies in late 2025 and early 2026 despite the 2025 shortage crisis and continued strikes [15]. The bureaucracy that Russia built to exercise control over its sprawling territory is a liability when reacting swiftly to external threats — not until late May 2026 did Russia's Defense Ministry authorize private companies to acquire anti-aircraft artillery systems and electronic warfare stations for their own defense [21]. Russian efforts to recruit rear-area air defenders will compete with manpower needs for frontline operations and the newly established Unmanned Systems Forces, adding further pressure on an already declining recruitment base [15].

RUSI argues that a Sino-American crisis or war would represent Russia's "last window" to revise the European order. However, Russia's best options might be ensuring a Chinese victory in the Pacific rather than directly attacking Europe. The Russian Pacific Fleet has received roughly half of Russia's newest Yasen-class submarines; submarines allocated to the Northern Fleet can be reallocated east via the northern sea route. Russia has enough key capabilities — submarines and missile launchers — to potentially swing an even balance between the US and China [27]. On the nuclear posture front, [Philipp Rombach]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/philipp-rombach.html) (SWP) documents that Russia revoked its ratification of the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (CTBT) in 2023, in the context of the war in Ukraine, to put itself on an equal footing with the United States (which signed but never ratified the CTBT). Russia declined the Biden administration's September 2023 invitation to send observers to the Nevada National Security Site — a transparency and confidence-building measure — alongside China. Parallel construction activity has been documented at the Russian nuclear test site on the Arctic archipelago of Novaya Zemlya. Although the CTBT has not formally entered into force, it is largely observed in practice; Russia's de-ratification nonetheless signals an erosion of the international norm against nuclear testing, compounded by the [US]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) failure to pay its 2026 CTBTO contributions, which further weakens the International Monitoring System [40]. See [Nuclear Proliferation and Arms Control]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/nuclear-proliferation-and-arms-control.html).

## Internal Stability and Succession

[Kadyrov]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/kadyrov.html) is rumored to be terminally ill, with no clear successor. Destabilization of Chechnya threatens Putin's original claim to having solved the Chechen problem — the foundational legitimacy narrative of his presidency [1]. Kadyrov also serves as the Kremlin's messenger to the Islamic world, leveraging his social media presence, elite interactions, and "holy jihad" narrative against the West to achieve mass media amplification across Indonesia, Saudi Arabia, and the Global South. His reported failing health threatens to create a strategic communications gap. Several potential successors exist — including Kadyrov's sons, Adam Delimkhanov, Magomed Daudov, and Apti Alaudinov — but none combine his unique blend of militaristic persona, Islamic credentials, and entertainment value [11].

Russia is conducting a systematic [resettlement campaign]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/russia-resettlement-campaign.html) to repopulate occupied [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html) with Russian citizens through weaponized property law, preferential mortgages, and demographic engineering. With 75% of apartments in occupied Mariupol purchased by Russian immigrants and plans to resettle 114,000 Russians by 2045, the campaign aims to engineer a demographic reality that entrenches occupation governance and complicates any future reintegration into Ukraine. The campaign violates the Fourth Geneva Convention's prohibition on transferring occupier populations into occupied territory [10].

[The Cipher Brief]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/the-cipher-brief.html) argues that repeated predictions of Vladimir Putin's imminent collapse have failed. Three years after Yevgeny Prigozhin's Wagner mutiny, the Russian security state has demonstrated remarkable durability. Putin's system rests on multiple overlapping centers of coercive power — the FSB (domestic intelligence), Rosgvardia (National Guard), and FSO (Federal Protective Service) — deliberately structured to counterbalance one another. The key analytical distinction is between long-term structural vulnerabilities and near-term political collapse — those are not the same thing. Prigozhin was not marching to overthrow Putin; he targeted Defense Minister Shoigu and Chief of the General Staff Gerasimov. Elite infighting should not be mistaken for imminent regime collapse. Sustained Western resolve and continued support for Ukraine are required — the security state will not collapse under its own weight [22].

RUSI (David Roche) develops a Leadership Vulnerability Framework scoring authoritarian regimes across three weighted variables: Economic System Stress (ESS, 35%), Elite Fragmentation Risk (EFR, 45%), and Trigger Index (TI, 20%). Applied to Russia in mid-2026, the framework places the country in the "High Stress and Increased Vulnerability" category — not a collapse zone, but a historically significant leadership-vulnerability zone. ESS is estimated at approximately 7.8 on a ten-point scale, driven by an inflation gap (8), a real wage gap (7), a Financial Reality Gap measuring divergence between reported banking health and observable strain (8), and a defence burden (8). EFR stands at approximately 6.9, composed of succession uncertainty (5.5), patronage compression (7.5), and military sustainability pressure (8.0). The TI remains materially below historical transition levels at 3.5, though potential triggers include conscription, economic collapse, military defeat, loss of Crimea, and bad election results. Over the past year, ESS rose from approximately 6.5 to 7.8 and EFR from 5.8 to 6.9, while TI increased only modestly from 3.0 to 3.5 — deterioration is structural rather than event-driven [41].

The framework's conclusion is counterintuitive: weakness does not make Putin less dangerous but more. A cornered Putin with nothing to offer his people except a draining economy and a war longer than the First World War and more murderous than the Soviet-Afghanistan campaign may become more isolated, more divorced from reality, and more unpredictable. The temptation is to extend the war through a step-change in grey-zone escalation against Europe, hoping to undermine European support for Ukraine without triggering NATO Article 5. Russia's daily losses are running at 1,300–1,400 killed and wounded, with recruitment hovering around 1,000 per day against a 2026 target of 409,000. Conscription — which would violate Putin's social contract not to let the "Special Operation" hurt the urban elite — looms as a coming flashpoint. The succession outlook is bleak: a military putsch is unlikely, a popular revolt even less so, and a Kremlin elite coup is as likely to produce a more extremist leader as a less extreme one [41].

[Phillips O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) (WU198) documents a stark asymmetry in alliance quality: Russia's allies — Iran (drone technology, Shahed/Geran), China (massive oil purchases, critical component supplies), and North Korea (artillery shells, front-line soldiers, and now ballistic missile forces) — provide military support with no strings attached, allowing Russia to fight the war it wants to fight. North Korea is deploying a ballistic missile strike force near Voronezh with 120 missiles and six launchers, with Zelensky claiming 30,000–50,000 additional North Korean troops may follow. In exchange, Russia helps Iranian targeting of US forces and provides technological help to improve North Korea's missile forces. Ukraine's allies, by contrast, have constantly tried to limit what Ukraine receives and how it uses what it has — transforming the war into a longer, bloodier affair than necessary. The US has used its leverage over Ukraine (through rationed Patriot supplies) to force a halt to Ukraine's effective Black Sea oil-interdiction campaign at Novorossiysk, a major favor to Putin [42].

GPF (GeoEconomicLens August 2026) reports that Russian fuel shortages are gradually easing in Moscow and some regions, with queues at gas stations shortening and sales limits relaxing. Ukrainian drones continue to attack Russian territory daily, expanding their geographic reach and frequency. The Caspian Sea has emerged as a leader in growth among all Russian sea basins in the first half of 2026, forming part of the International North-South Transport Corridor linking Russia to Iran and India [43].

## DragonBear and Strategic Relationships

Moscow fundamentally misjudged [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html)'s America First as isolationist and accommodating of Russia's sphere of influence. The [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) demonstrated the opposite: the U.S. is aggressive and unrestrained when it chooses to act. Russia now realizes that multipolarity requires building a security architecture — centered on nuclear deterrence and Eurasian partnerships — that can withstand prolonged American pressure, rather than expecting a bilateral deal with Washington to recognize its interests [9]. The Iran ceasefire itself represented a DragonBear course correction: Russia and China green-lit the MoU because uncontrolled escalation risked damaging their own foundations [4][5].

A Kremlin-approved programme is transferring Russia's Ukraine-war combat lessons to the Chinese military. The agreement was signed July 2, 2026 by a Russian major general and a Chinese senior colonel. The subject matter is precisely what one would expect from more than four years of high-intensity war: drone warfare, anti-ship missile exposure, and the tactical realities of a contested modern battlefield. Russia holds combat experience accumulated at enormous cost; [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) holds industrial scale, technological weight, and simulators but has not fought a war in decades. A Russia degraded by war, increasingly dependent on Beijing economically and industrially, becomes the weaker partner — yet in becoming weaker it transfers hard-won expertise upward to a rising China that can absorb and industrialise those lessons at scale [25].

As Russian nuclear saber-rattling accelerated through 2026, with prominent Russian voices openly urging the Kremlin to reach for nuclear weapons after Ukrainian strikes, Beijing reportedly delivered Moscow an ultimatum-form warning that there can be no thought of using nuclear weapons in Ukraine. The DragonBear coordinates across every domain except the one where China's global standing and strategic interest diverge absolutely from Russia's escalation logic. Beijing enables the war machine and simultaneously polices its nuclear threshold. The red line runs through Beijing, not Moscow — coordination and constraint are exercised by the same hand [25].

RUSI traces the collapse of the "spirit of Anchorage" — the vague term Russian media adopted after the August 2025 Trump-Putin summit in Alaska. The term had no formal written agreement behind it; each side came away with its own interpretation. By October 2025, Lavrov was noting the US had changed its rhetoric under European pressure. By June 2026, Lavrov pronounced the spirit dead. See [Spirit of Anchorage 2025]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/spirit-of-anchorage-2025.html) [26]. Russia oscillates between hard doctrines and abstract, occasionally religiously-themed frameworks about its "mission," "fate," or "destiny" — making international agreements difficult to negotiate. The "spirit of Anchorage" exemplifies this pattern: Russia creates an ambiguous verbal framework, assigns it whatever meaning suits the current propaganda need, and blames the other party for "betraying" a commitment that was never made [26].

RUSI identifies three separate but parallel conversations Russia maintains: the Ukraine war, the bilateral US relationship, and potential future war with [NATO]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/organizations/nato.html). On 6 July 2026, Peskov stated the "special operation" had morphed into a "war" due to Western intervention — [Lukashenka]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/lukashenka.html) echoed this the next day, something he had historically avoided. The timing on the eve of the NATO Ankara summit was deliberate [26]. Russian, Ukrainian, and American negotiators held their first formal trilateral peace negotiations in Abu Dhabi in January 2026. The cautious optimism from Ukrainian officials and Russian state media suggests Moscow may be prepared to explore pathways toward ending the conflict — but the damage to Russia's influence along its southern flank is already done [8].

## Asymmetric and Hybrid Operations

Russia is conducting systematic [cognitive warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/cognitive-warfare.html) against the West. The Social Design Agency has orchestrated operations including islamophobic pig head attacks on Paris mosques, vandalism campaigns, and election interference. These are not isolated incidents but coordinated efforts to destabilize Western societies from within [3]. Russia's networks constitute a core instrument of its [Endless Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/endless-warfare.html) strategy against the U.S. and the West. These networks fall into two categories [24]: **Institutional networks** — the GRU, FSB, and specialized units responsible for assassinations, sabotage, influence operations, and cyber-attacks conducting cognitive warfare and political coercion across [Europe]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/europe.html). **Ambiguous networks** — "ghost fleets" that have generated billions in revenue despite Western sanctions, shell and front companies, criminal organizations, and illicit financial systems that enable sanctions evasion and sustain operations. These networks are resilient, adaptive, and self-recovering, having been developed over decades [24].

Russia's reputation as a regime guarantor has been damaged by failures in Syria and Venezuela. In [Africa]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/africa.html), the transition from Wagner to the [Africa Corps]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/organizations/wagner-africa-corps.html) has produced a more bureaucratic, less flexible instrument — one vulnerable to wider strategic constraints as resources are prioritized for the Ukraine front. [Turkey]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/turkey.html) sees an opening in Mali as Russian security support shows its limits [2].

[CSIS]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/csis.html) (Shah and Sherman) document that Russia-Iran cyber cooperation has been repeatedly misread and overstated. Russia's cyber ecosystem is expansive — state intelligence agencies (FSB), state-created front companies, state-coerced cybercriminals, private sector contractors, and patriotic hackers form a complex web. Russia and Iran's strategic partnership is driven by self-interest and shared Western adversaries. In cyberspace, their history of cooperation includes a 2001 treaty establishing a framework for interstate cooperation, a 2021 agreement on cyber and information security, Russian private sector provision of digital surveillance and censorship software to Iran, and a 2025 Comprehensive Strategic Partnership Treaty [29]. However, several mitigating factors limit the depth of cyber cooperation: hacktivist coordination does not equate to state-to-state collaboration; Russian cyber actor Turla commandeered Iranian cyber espionage infrastructure to launch its own operations — a "false-flag" that likely deepened distrust; each state worries about the other conducting cyber espionage on its infrastructure; and the partnership is characterized by "mutual suspicion, ideological differences, and competition." The evidence does not substantiate claims of closely coordinated cyber operations. Drone technology and satellite imagery sharing have demonstrated far greater strategic impact [29].

## Southern Flank and CSTO

Russia's grip on its southern frontier — from the Black Sea through the South Caucasus to [Central Asia]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/central-asia.html) — has eroded significantly, accelerated by the 2022 invasion of Ukraine. The 2020 Nagorno-Karabakh War exposed Russia's inability to manage its traditional sphere; Turkey's decisive support for [Azerbaijan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/azerbaijan.html) overturned decades of Russian-managed equilibrium. The US has exploited the opening through the Trump Route corridor connecting Azerbaijan to Nakhchivan through Armenian territory, giving Washington a foothold on Russia's southern periphery [8]. Central Asian states are deepening ties with the US (C5+1 summits in 2023 and 2025), pursuing multi-vector foreign policies, and reviving national languages at the expense of Russian. The Middle Corridor — a China-to-Europe trade route bypassing Russia — is reducing the region's dependence on Russian transit. Russia's strategic bandwidth remains monopolized by the western flank (Ukraine), leaving the south increasingly open to Chinese, Turkish, and American influence [8].

Russia is attempting to revitalize the Collective Security Treaty Organization (CSTO) — the post-Soviet military alliance comprising Armenia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, and Belarus. Putin's plan serves two objectives: standardizing Central Asian militaries on Russian equipment (reversing the mid-2000s diversification toward Western and Chinese systems), and creating export markets for Russian military products that the war economy cannot absorb domestically. In December 2025, the CSTO practiced crisis-localization exercises, and Kazakhstan is pushing to expand deployment authorities [7]. However, the CSTO remains hampered by Armenia's frozen membership, divergent threat perceptions, and a history of failures — it did not assist Kyrgyzstan in 2010, abstained from the Nagorno-Karabakh War, and saw member states refuse to support Russia's position on Crimea. See [Layered Security]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/layered-security.html) [7].

## Putin's Fuel Dilemma

[RUSI]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/rusi.html) (Jim McLean) analyses the structural trade-off that Ukraine's deep-strike campaign against Russian energy infrastructure has forced on the Kremlin: every step taken to shield consumers from fuel shortages comes at the expense of export revenues and budget stability [30]. After weeks of mounting evidence, Putin publicly acknowledged on 28 June 2026 that Ukrainian attacks on energy infrastructure were creating "problems" and a "certain shortage" of fuel — the first such admission — even as he insisted the situation was "not critical" [30]. The scope of the crisis is broad and worsening. Two-thirds of Russian federal entities have reported gasoline or diesel restrictions, with the number of affected federal subjects steadily increasing since mid-June and occupied Crimea experiencing shortages as early as late May [30][15]. The precursor crisis was already severe: at least 57 of Russia's 83 regions experienced gasoline shortages by mid-October 2025 [15]. Shrinking supply has driven surging demand, rising prices, and long lines at gas stations across the country, prompting regional governments and gas station companies to impose sales restrictions beginning in early June [15]. ISW has not yet observed restrictions in Ingushetia, Kalmykia, Chechnya, Chukotka, or Nenets Autonomous Okrug, though shortages will likely spread there [15]. Retail fuel prices rose 3% in a single week, gasoline production is down roughly a quarter since late May, and jet fuel prices at Russian airports are up 17% since early June [30]. Diesel production fell by one million metric tons in April 2026 and a further 600,000 metric tons in May, and some Russian regions have restricted diesel sales alongside gasoline [15].

Putin announced on June 28 that Russia is tapping gasoline reserves, leveraging small and medium refineries to supplement large-facility output, shortening routine maintenance cycles, and postponing scheduled maintenance [15]. The Duma approved amendments permitting lower-quality, below-standard fuel to enter the market, and regional officials proposed invoking price caps at gas stations in Russia and occupied Crimea — measures that would shift the cost burden from consumers to suppliers, further constraining supply as stations struggle with profitability [15]. The Kremlin has extended the jet fuel export ban through end-November and is considering a diesel ban; it is also negotiating to import approximately 50,000 tonnes of gasoline from Kazakhstan [30]. Many of these mitigation efforts address symptoms rather than root causes and are effective only in the short term; while gasoline demand will naturally decline in the fall, seasonal relief will not eliminate the primary driver — Ukraine's strike campaign [15]. Consumer demand will likely keep rising despite these measures, driven by widespread panic and seasonal summertime spikes [15].

The fiscal dimension is decisive. Export restrictions prevent refiners from selling into higher-priced foreign markets, reducing foreign-currency earnings while limiting companies' ability to recover costs at home [30]. The fuel dampener — the federal subsidy to refiners supplying the domestic market instead of exporting — rose from approximately $220 million in Q1 2026 to more than $2.5 billion in May alone [30]. A diesel export ban would forgo an estimated $2.9 billion in monthly gasoil export revenues, equivalent to 14% of Russia's total oil export earnings that month [30]. The Kremlin's fuel imports from other states are further depleting coffers and reducing funds available for the war [15]. Russian officials have consistently downplayed the crisis: Deputy Prime Minister Novak claimed the situation is "challenging but manageable," asserting sufficient reserves and describing the disruption as merely "restructuring fuel market logistics," while Peskov attributed the shortages to global oil price irregularities, insisting they are not unique to Russia [15]. Yet the dilemma narrows the Kremlin's room for manoeuvre: the longer Putin pursues maximalist aims in Ukraine, the more detached he becomes from realities at home. Many Russians endorsed the imperial project in principle, but the fear of being directly affected — through Ukrainian strikes or mounting economic hardship — is steadily eroding enthusiasm for the war. See [Putin]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/putin.html), [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html), and [Energy and Resources]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/energy-and-resources.html) [30].

# Key Dynamics

- **The Ukraine war is consuming Russia at unsustainable rates.** Casualties exceed 1.4 million with the casualty ratio reaching 8:1, over 90 percent from drone attacks, monthly losses outpace recruitment, and territorial gains have reversed for the first time since 2024. [6][12][37]
- **A compounding fuel crisis is spreading.** Ukraine's deep-strike campaign has degraded refining capacity, spread shortages to most federal subjects, and forced the Kremlin to choose between domestic supply and export revenue. [14][15][19][30]
- **Air defense gaps span the country.** Russia's vast territory enables Ukrainian strikes from Moscow to Siberia, a structural vulnerability production delays and bureaucratic inertia cannot quickly close. [15][21]
- **Russia is the dependent partner in the DragonBear axis.** China polices the nuclear threshold and absorbs combat lessons while constraining Moscow's strategic autonomy. [25][35]
- **The southern flank is eroding.** The CSTO is hampered by frozen memberships and past failures, Central Asian states are diversifying westward, and the Middle Corridor bypasses Russian transit. [7][8]
- **The security state has proven durable.** Structural vulnerabilities, succession risk in Chechnya, and elite infighting should not be mistaken for imminent regime collapse. [1][22]
- **Putin is entering a grey zone of increasing vulnerability.** RUSI's framework scores Russia at ESS 7.8, EFR 6.9, TI 3.5 — historically significant stress without imminent collapse, making Putin more unpredictable and dangerous. [41]
- **Russia's allies are more helpful than Ukraine's.** Iran, China, and North Korea provide no-strings-attached military support, while the US uses Patriot leverage to constrain Ukraine's effective campaigns. [42]
- **Cognitive warfare remains an effective instrument of power.** Hybrid networks coordinate sabotage, influence operations, and sanctions evasion across the West. [3][24]
- **The shadow fleet is an economic lifeline under growing pressure.** ~3 tankers/day transit the Baltic carrying ~half of Russia's oil exports, but European boarding actions hit only ~2% of transits while Ukraine's drone campaign has damaged 50–60 tankers and cost $1.76B in lost revenue. [38]

# Analysis

Russia's strategic position is paradoxical: deeply committed in Ukraine, facing succession risk in Chechnya, losing ground in Africa, yet successfully exploiting Western energy dependencies and conducting effective cognitive warfare. The DragonBear axis provides critical support, but Russia's ability to sustain multiple theaters is increasingly constrained by the prioritization demands of the Ukraine war.

### The Attrition Strategy and Missile Primacy

[Aurelien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/aurelien.html) identifies Russia's approach in [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html) as classical attrition warfare — the same paradigm that decided the First World War, where superior numbers, access to raw materials, and productive capacity ultimately wore down the Central Powers. Russia's strategy targets Ukraine's ability to actually deploy forces by attacking fuel depots, transport links, and infrastructure. If forces cannot reach the front, they are irrelevant regardless of their nominal strength [31].

Russia retained immense stocks of missiles and artillery from its Soviet-inherited tradition of indirect-fire primacy, while the West neglected conventional offensive missiles in favor of manned aircraft. Russian missile salvoes can overwhelm Western defenses — individual missiles can perhaps be countered reliably, but salvoes cannot. Only one missile needs to reach its target. The West's reaction to Russian missile capability has been to ignore it and propose anti-missile systems rather than develop comparable offensive capability. Russia will decide when the Ukraine conflict stops, and the post-war situation will likely feature the intimidatory use of military capability for political purposes [31]. See [Revolution in Military Affairs]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/revolution-in-military-affairs.html) and [Defense Industrial Resilience]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/defense-industrial-resilience.html).

### The Hormuz Windfall and Military Training for China

[Velina Tchakarova]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/velina-tchakarova.html)'s Day 144 diagnostic identifies a direct cross-domain linkage in which Russia's Hormuz windfall — the surge in oil export revenues generated by the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html)'s disruption of Gulf supply — is financing the war in [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html). The Iran war displaced roughly 10 mbpd of Gulf supply, and Russia's oil exports surged to fill the gap, generating revenues that directly sustain the war economy. This creates a structural feedback loop: the more the US-Iran war disrupts Gulf supply, the more Russia earns, and the more resources it can dedicate to the Ukraine front [32].

Simultaneously, the diagnostic notes Russian military training for [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) as an S3 (US-China and DragonBear) domain indicator — the Kremlin-approved programme transferring Ukraine-war combat lessons to the PLA continues to operate. The combination of the Hormuz windfall financing the Ukraine war and military training for China represents the DragonBear axis operating at full simultaneity: Russia monetizes a crisis generated by Iran's confrontation with the US, and simultaneously transfers the combat experience gained in Ukraine upward to China's rising military. See [DragonBear]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html) [32].

### Japan Reengagement and Russia's Overplayed Hand

[RUSI]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/rusi.html) (James Brown) documents a recent trend of Japanese political and business reengagement with [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) — LDP politicians attending Russian embassy events, a METI-led business delegation to Moscow, and the resumption of high-level diplomatic contacts — that Moscow has failed to exploit effectively. Russia's increasingly close relationship with [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) and [North Korea]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html), including joint aviation and naval patrols intended as a show of force against [Japan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/japan.html), limits the prospects for rapprochement. A *New York Times* report in July 2026 that Putin has turned Japan into a "den of spies" since 2022 will further undermine public trust in Russia. Russian diplomats have also overplayed their hand: when Japan's ambassador proposed a foreign ministers' meeting, Russian Foreign Ministry spokeswoman Zakharova responded not with enthusiasm but with a list of arduous demands — including the lifting of sanctions and ending support for [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html) — preconditions that effectively foreclose the diplomatic opening. A wiser Russian policy would exploit every opportunity for reengagement to widen divisions between Japan and its European partners; instead, Moscow is narrowing its own options [33].

### Military Coordination with China in Asia and the CRINK Framework

Russia's role in enabling Chinese military pressure against [Japan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/japan.html) has acquired operational significance beyond symbolic joint patrols. Aizawa identifies the December 2025 joint strategic bomber flight as a case where Russia served as a supportive partner in an operation timed to China's political interests — pressuring Japan following Takaichi's Taiwan statement. The flight route, extending over 600 miles past the Southwest Islands toward Tokyo, demonstrated a more coercive pattern than previous iterations. Russian Tu-95 nuclear-capable bombers flew alongside Chinese H-6N bombers, enabling China to project combined nuclear-capable reach toward the Japanese capital. Russia's participation allows China to apply military pressure with the backing of a partner that possesses combat experience and nuclear expertise — a division of labor consistent with the DragonBear's coordination architecture [34].

Ueki documents that the emerging Russia-North Korea military relationship — North Korean troops deployed to support Russia's war in Ukraine — has not developed into trilateral cooperation with China. China opposed a China-Russia-North Korea trilateral exercise proposed by Defense Minister Shoigu in July 2023, and Beijing dismissed the significance of the Putin-Kim Comprehensive Strategic Partnership Treaty signed in June 2024. Russia's cooperation with each CRINK partner remains bilateral, and China's preference for bilateral relationships limits the potential for a formal trilateral military architecture. Russia's dependence on [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) has increased through the Ukraine war — China's exports to Russia surged 40 percent from 2022 to 2023 — but the reverse is not true: China's exports to Russia amount to only about 20 percent of its exports to the United States. The asymmetry means Russia is the dependent partner in the relationship, constraining Moscow's ability to shape the DragonBear's strategic direction [35]. See [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) and [Japan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/japan.html).

### Arctic Militarization and the White Water Zone

Russia's Arctic posture is anchored in a Soviet-era legacy: during the Cold War, the Arctic functioned as a forward strategic space for naval operations, nuclear deterrence, and early warning systems. This infrastructure collapsed in the post-1991 disorder, but under Putin the Kremlin has systematically reactivated and modernized it since the early 2000s — rebuilding bases, expanding icebreaker fleets, and restoring air defense coverage across the northern flank. The result is a strategic reality that existing Western maritime frameworks, organized around the blue/green/brown water taxonomy, cannot adequately capture. Adrians (SWP) proposes "white water zone" as a fourth maritime category to conceptualize the cold-water operational environment that Russia has already militarized in practice [36].

Russian Arctic strategy is framed within a "world ocean" concept that organizes maritime space hierarchically into near seas and far seas. The Arctic occupies a privileged position as consolidated sovereign domain — an integrated strategic space rather than a peripheral theater. Crucially, much of the competition in the Arctic unfolds below the threshold of conventional confrontation: fishing rights disputes, non-military maritime forces enforcing jurisdictional claims, and constabulary pressures that do not trigger Article 5 responses but steadily reshape the operational reality. This grey-zone posture allows Russia to consolidate control incrementally while Western alliance structures debate whether the Arctic constitutes a dedicated operational environment or merely a secondary concern [36]. See [Arctic]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/arctic.html) and [NATO]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/organizations/nato.html).

## Back-Channel Diplomacy and the Normalization Path

[Andrew Ryvkin]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/andrew-ryvkin.html) (GPF) argues that Moscow's road back to the global stage runs through Berlin, not Washington. The media focus on US-Russia negotiations obscures the decisive role of the EU: it is the EU that shares a border with Russia, EU sanctions that have hurt the Russian economy the most, and Europe that is [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html)'s key ally. Russia's eastward turn toward China — trade totaling $228 billion in 2025 — is driven by necessity rather than strategy. By comparison, Russia's trade with the EU in 2021 equaled $282 billion. The EU controls the future of Russia's frozen assets, integration into the global banking system, and access to European markets. Moscow understands that if it ever wants to regain access to those assets, it needs a clear channel to the EU and, specifically, to [Germany]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/germany.html) [39].

Since at least early 2024, Germany has held a series of back-channel meetings in Baku and Abu Dhabi involving former German politicians and Kremlin-connected figures, formally devoted to discussing the possible revival of the Petersburg Dialogue. The Russian delegation includes Viktor Zubkov (former prime minister and chairman of Gazprom's supervisory board — a longtime Putin confidant from St. Petersburg), Valery Fadeyev (head of Russia's Human Rights Council and Putin adviser), and Alexey Gromyko (head of the Institute of Europe at the Russian Academy of Sciences, which advises the Kremlin on EU policy). These figures carry significantly more political weight inside Russia than media-friendly envoys like Kirill Dmitriev. [Roman Abramovich]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/roman-abramovich.html) has been identified as another channel, valued for keeping lines of communication open and helping with narrowly defined humanitarian issues [39].

The structural constraint on diplomacy is the absence of an anti-war faction within the Russian elite. Openly taking an anti-war position would guarantee expulsion from the elite. Russia's security services — the FSB and SVR — remain firmly pro-war and deeply anti-Western, willing to engage only on narrow issues such as prisoner exchanges. Putin has signaled unwillingness to compromise on territory and remains committed to continuing the war at least to the point of capturing the Donbas. Yet both sides understand that the war will eventually end, and that stable lines of communication will be needed to restore at least some degree of normalcy. If a settlement is reached, these back channels are likely to expand rather than disappear, with business leaders, bankers, and former officials becoming increasingly important in discussions over sanctions, frozen assets, reconstruction, and commercial ties [39]. See [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html).
