# Background

China is the world's most populous nation and its largest energy consumer, using almost twice as much energy as the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) while housing four times as many people. Its manufacturing base is the most advanced and high-tech on the planet, giving it structural advantages in the conversion of economic capacity into military power. The US perceives China as its primary strategic threat; the status quo of US dominance with roughly half the economy generates persistent friction that defines the [great power competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html) of the current era [1].

China anchors the DragonBear axis with [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html), coordinating pressure on the Western coalition across multiple theaters simultaneously.

# Current Situation

## Doctrine: AI-Military Integration & PLA Modernization

Beijing aims to displace the United States as the global AI leader by 2030. Unlike the fragmented American approach, China pursues a coordinated national-level strategy in which civilian AI firms are folded directly into PLA modernization. Alibaba and Baidu have been added to the Pentagon's Chinese Military Companies list; DeepSeek is part of PLA modernization efforts. While the US holds a 3-6 month lead in AI models, China is years ahead in military integration — the dimension that ultimately matters for the [AI military race]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/ai-military-race.html). [The Cipher Brief]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/the-cipher-brief.html) frames the contest as one that will not be won by the best model but by the fastest military [2].

The PLA's doctrinal apparatus is evolving in parallel. The 2020 revision of the PLA's "Science of Military Strategy" textbook reveals new emphases on wartime political work, "intelligentization" (AI-driven warfare), multi-domain integrated joint operations, strategic reconnaissance, and maritime maneuver operations in far seas. New chapters cover joint logistics and a revised People's Armed Police section including coast guard roles. The text is not a fundamental rethinking but an incremental update reflecting Xi's military reforms [11].

However, Western analysts face a growing blind spot in understanding Chinese military doctrine as the PLA restricts open-source information and its key doctrinal texts become outdated, lacking coverage of AI, autonomous systems, and post-2016 reforms. The PLA's pre-existing investments in AI-enabled swarm technology and sophisticated reconnaissance drones are skewing the lessons it draws from [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html) and Middle East conflicts, where low-cost attritable FPV drones dominate. Xi's purges and institutional rigidity discourage adaptation, leading the PLA to downplay the central role of cheap, disposable systems while continuing to develop expensive, survivable platforms — creating a potential asymmetric advantage for [Taiwan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/taiwan.html) [12][13].

## Doctrine: Ideological Turn Toward Maoism

As Beijing grapples with economic slowdown, social unrest, and ideological uncertainty, the CCP is undergoing a significant turn toward ideological mobilization that echoes elements of Maoism. Cai Qi, the CCP ideology chief, has called on officials to strengthen "mainstream ideology that is self-confident, self-reliant and united" and to "focus on the economy" — an ideological rather than technocratic directive. Publicity Department leader Li Shulei has urged improvements in "ideological and political work" and the development of "a Chinese system of philosophy and social sciences with independent thinking" — rejecting foreign frameworks in favor of party-led knowledge production [8].

The ideological push has two goals: domestically, to shore up confidence and suppress narratives that could undermine the party's claim to competent governance; internationally, to "tell China's stories" in a controlled, positive way. Ideology is intended to function as a substitute for rapid economic improvement — the portrayal of economic problems as crises of confidence mirrors Mao's tendency to politicize material difficulties [8].

Xi's revival of the "Fengqiao experience" — a Mao-era method (1963) for containing and resolving conflicts at the grassroots level — exemplifies the turn. Rural protests over land seizures, inadequate compensation, religious demolitions, and cremation mandates reflect the financial desperation of debt-burdened local governments. The return of migrant workers to rural areas has inflamed tensions as people bring urban expectations into environments that cannot satisfy them [8].

The parallels with Mao are structural: emphasis on ideology, centrality of the party, significance of unity under a strong leader, distrust of unchecked bureaucratic autonomy. The key difference is that Xi leads through a highly institutionalized party-state that prioritizes order and administrative control, whereas Mao ruled through mass mobilization and class struggle. The likely outcome is a "mixed" system in which ideological tightening coexists with pragmatic economic management — not Maoism restored, but ideology as a stabilizing force during structural economic stress [8].

## Strategic Culture: Endless Warfare and Infrastructure Pre-Positioning

China's networks constitute a core instrument of its [Endless Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/endless-warfare.html) strategy against the United States. Cyber operators probe and penetrate U.S. critical infrastructure to sustain access, collect information, and provide a disruption capability during a crisis; influence operations and technology transfer directly enhance military capabilities. The CCP's United Front Work Department reflects centralized national direction and a long-term horizon — in contrast to America's fragmented approach. These are not isolated activities but integrated elements of a persistent strategy to erode U.S. power and influence over time [18].

The specific operational expression is systematic pre-positioning inside American commercial infrastructure. Retired Gen. Mike Minihan and Rear Adm. Mark Montgomery ([The Cipher Brief]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/the-cipher-brief.html)) report that for more than two years, top US intelligence officials have warned that Chinese cyber operators are compromising hundreds of American transportation, communications, energy, and logistics systems — gaining persistent access so that Beijing can disrupt and destroy systems at the time of its choosing. The operational logic is delay rather than destruction: China does not need to destroy American combat power to gain an advantage, it simply needs to slow it. Every day equipment sits in a rail yard, every hour a port is offline, every aircraft waylaid by corrupted logistics data or disrupted communications creates the friction an adversary seeks during the opening days of a conflict. Ports rely on automated cranes and digital cargo management; railroads depend on computerized dispatching and signaling; airports operate through integrated flight planning, air traffic management, and logistics software; fuel distribution, maintenance scheduling, cargo visibility, and command and control all depend on data moving securely across interconnected networks. The intrusion into these systems means the battlespace is being shaped before any shot is fired [22]. See [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) for the policy response agenda and [Defense Industrial Resilience]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/defense-industrial-resilience.html) for the broader infrastructure-security frame.

## Political Economy: Structural Decay, Export Dependence, and Financial Repression

China's growth engine has undergone a structural break. The policy tools that once generated rapid expansion — massive credit injection, infrastructure investment, property development — no longer function. This is not a crisis or collapse scenario but a steady decay of the state's capacity to influence economic outcomes through fiscal and financial systems [29][30]. The origins trace to the unprecedented credit bubble of 2008-2017, when China's banks added credit equivalent to roughly one-third of global GDP — $27 trillion in new bank assets — a pace no other country has matched in the last century [30]. Property was the primary beneficiary of this cheap credit; the property market collapse is the proximate cause of the current slowdown, while the exhaustion of the post-2008 credit bubble is the ultimate cause [30]. During the boom, credit flows enabled political objectives; the absence of credit now constrains them [30]. After decades of unproductive investment and accumulated bad debt, Beijing has lost the domestic demand generation mechanism, leaving the economy entirely export-dependent [29][30].

The banking system and fiscal position compound the constraint. The $72 trillion banking system needs restructuring, but doing so would require accepting slower growth; Beijing has chosen instead to roll over bad loans indefinitely and continue funding nonproductive companies [30]. Bank lending growth has fallen to 5.3%, less than a third of boom-year rates, with 58% of new loans issued at or below the 3% prime rate — credit flowing to state-owned enterprises and local governments rather than tech firms or households [30]. The fiscal position is equally constrained: tax revenues stand at 15.4% of GDP, below every OECD member, while fiscal deficits run at approximately 9-10% of GDP (~$2 trillion per year), leaving extremely limited bandwidth for stimulus [30].

The export surge is partly a symptom of domestic decay. Weak domestic demand — not industrial policy alone — drives excess production abroad at compressed prices: as property construction waned, the industrial ecosystem lost its domestic customers but kept producing with credit, pushing the surplus outward [30]. The rest of the economy has cooled since 2022 even as exports climbed [30]. China achieved a $1.2 trillion trade surplus and $3.8 trillion in exports (+5.5% year-over-year) despite the Trump trade war, but this export strength is a direct result of the domestic slowdown, not a sign of industrial vitality [30]. [Adam Tooze]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/adam-tooze.html) frames this as "China Shock 2.0" — distinct from the early-2000s "China Shock 1.0" which was a low-tech, low-wage cost story felt primarily in the US. China Shock 2.0 is above all about [Europe]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/europe.html) and about China's progress into advanced industrial sectors, above all vehicles. The EU-China trade deficit has roughly doubled since COVID. The surge is concentrated in green energy goods (in demand for Europe's energy transition) and chemicals (where European production has been hit by high gas prices). Contrary to the price-dumping thesis, unit values of Chinese exports are rising in line with Japan and South Korea — the surge is a deliberate industrialization push backed by policy, not exchange-rate manipulation. CSIS estimates that Chinese EV industry subsidies totaled $231 billion from 2009 to 2023 — large, but not outsized compared to the European Green Deal or Biden's IRA, and German automakers paid out €31 billion in dividends in 2023 alone rather than reinvesting [9]. The surge is most visible in electric vehicles: in 2025, China alone sold more than 13 million electric cars (55% of new car sales in the Chinese market), accounted for nearly three-quarters of global electric car production, and saw EV exports double to more than 2.5 million units — overtaking [Japan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/japan.html) as the global auto sales leader. China was responsible for more than 80% of global battery cell production, with battery prices at roughly $84/kWh compared to as much as 55% higher in North America and Europe. This dominance extends across the entire supply chain: mineral refining, battery manufacturing, vehicle assembly, charging infrastructure, and government coordination [16]. See [EV Competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/ev-competition.html).

The export model is sustained by financial repression. The real exchange rate — combining the nominal exchange rate with relative prices — has been depreciating since 2021: prices in the West have risen while China hovers on the edge of deflation; with the nominal rate pegged by capital controls and intervention at an undervalued level, Chinese goods become even more competitive. Instead of driving yuan appreciation, trade surplus proceeds are absorbed into official and quasi-official reserve accumulation [9][10]. The other side of this exchange rate management is a system of capital controls that wall in tens of trillions in Chinese wealth. An estimated $800 billion to $1 trillion left China in 2025. Beijing responded with a crackdown: offshore brokerages (Longbridge, Tiger Brokers, Futu) fined $330 million; red-chip IPO structures restricted; H-share listing proceeds forced back to the mainland; individual mainlanders barred from buying US stocks through Hong Kong accounts. Chinese citizens can transfer only $50,000 per year abroad. The system constitutes what Tooze calls "financial repression" — a core component of China's political economy. Beijing tolerates wealth creation but does not grant its bourgeoisie unlimited free disposal of its wealth. China's wealthiest investors are effectively barred from participating in the US AI boom — the greatest run-up in asset prices in history. China's stock market lags woefully behind the world. If the capital account were liberalized, the likely result would not be yuan appreciation but capital flight and crash — a scenario akin to 2015-2016, when over a trillion dollars drained and Beijing imposed stricter controls [10].

The exchange-rate dimension is now politicized. ECB President Christine Lagarde's call for global leaders to discuss the undervaluation of the Chinese yuan reflects a wider geoeconomic shift. China's rising trade surplus, weak domestic demand, and strong industrial output are increasing pressure on advanced economies. A weaker yuan makes Chinese exports more competitive, but the larger issue is the interaction between exchange rates, industrial subsidies, excess manufacturing capacity, and global trade imbalances. The structural change is the gradual politicization of trade and currency relations — exchange rates are becoming part of the wider contest over industrial power [16]. See [Monetary System Transition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/monetary-system-transition.html).

The diplomatic posture has shifted accordingly. During its rise, China offered growing market access as an incentive; now it can only threaten to withhold goods [30]. Beijing is alarmed by global de-risking and has taken retaliatory actions: April decrees against "discriminatory" foreign laws and June controls on outbound direct investment, establishing legal basis for punishing countries that restrict Chinese investments [30]. Beijing has threatened countermeasures against Europe's Industrial Accelerator Act and is tightening dual-use controls against Europe's defense buildup — betting it can stop the Western trade response long enough to assemble new chokepoints [30]. Yet Beijing is pursuing targeted controls on outbound investments rather than broad-based export controls that would limit its own growth, recognizing the constraint [30]. China is already the world's largest exporter; diversifying to genuinely new markets is extremely difficult when the West accounts for most of the world's final consumer demand growth, and the scope for further market penetration is narrowing [30]. Beijing appears willing to accept weaker consumption to preserve manufacturing leadership and reduce foreign technology dependence — a trade-off at the heart of the US-China rivalry [29].

## The "Big One" and the 2026 Surplus Surge

By mid-2026 the surplus had returned to magnitudes not seen since the 2006-08 peak. Brad Setser's data — relayed by [Tooze]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/adam-tooze.html) — show China's raw trade surplus at roughly 7% of GDP, only a bit below its 2006-08 levels, while the export volume index rose about 60% from end-2018 to April 2026 against an import volume index that rose only about 10%. The asymmetry generated an unprecedented divergence in the trade balance — a divergence that macroeconomic aggregates alone cannot fully explain [31].

The 2020s surge is qualitatively different from the 2000s export wave. The first shock was driven by China's integration into Western supply chains on Western terms — private foreign investment linking China's labor force into globalization as the West had designed it. The current surge reflects deliberate post-WTO learning: Beijing resolved already in the early 2000s that China should not remain at the bottom of the supply chain and has spent the subsequent twenty years climbing the value chains under national industrial policy, from regional to city-level implementation, to engage the world economy from a position of technological leadership. The shock is industrial policy running at world scale, not the inexorable outgrowth of an entrenched savings glut [31].

Trump 1.0 protectionism (post-2017) now shields the United States against this new shock, redirecting the surplus toward Europe. The US's 100% tariffs on Chinese EVs and the broader US-China bilateral deficit narrowing mean the leading edge of China's export drive hits Europe rather than America. The protective response is the Polanyian backlash to Shock 1.0, belatedly institutionalized as policy [31]. See [China Shock and Deindustrialization]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/china-shock-and-deindustrialization.html) for the full sequenced treatment.

[Tooze]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/adam-tooze.html) proposes the seismology metaphor to capture the difference: Shock 1.0 was a "foreshock" and the 2020s event may be the main event — a "Big One" rather than a sequel. The 1.0/2.0 numbering understates a complete transformation of industrial division of labour at world scale, not a repetition with marginal differences [31].

## Structural Limits

[The Honest Sorcerer]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/the-honest-sorcerer.html) argues that China's economic miracle has ended. Despite still growing, something is fundamentally wrong with the model, which has more in common with Western capitalist economies than most admit: ballooning debt levels, slowing growth, falling birth rates, saturated markets, environmental degradation, and resource depletion. China is not a communist country — it is a state-led capitalist economy suffering from the same ills as its American counterpart [6]. It is impossible to grow forever on a finite planet, and China's energy-intensive, export-driven model faces the same thermodynamic constraints as any other industrial civilization.

China peaked at 18.5% of global GDP in 2021 and has since declined to 16.7% officially — closer to 15% when measurement adjustments are applied. The [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html), by contrast, has risen from 24% to approximately 26% over the same period [29][30]. Rhodium Group estimates that 2022 actual growth was likely negative rather than the officially claimed 3%, and that 2022-2025 real growth averaged approximately 1.5-2.0% rather than the claimed 4.6% — official data has become unreliable [30]. In dollar terms, China's cumulative expansion since 2021 amounts to only about 2% [30]. The consequences of weakening export growth extend beyond headline numbers: deflation, rising corporate debt, weaker exchange rate, capital outflows, financial instability, and the potential for a lost decade analogous to [Japan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/japan.html) in the 1990s [30]. Economic pressure means China will struggle to modernize its military and sustain technological innovation as fiscal resources trend lower [30].

## Relationships: Taiwan Posture

China is executing a systematic [Taiwan standoff]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/taiwan-standoff.html) campaign — a multi-domain grey-zone effort that pairs coercion with co-optation. [Velina Tchakarova]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/velina-tchakarova.html) describes the campaign as military encirclement, coast guard harassment, economic inducement, and united-front messaging working in concert to erode Taiwan's defensive will [3]. Beijing normalizes presence around [Taiwan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/taiwan.html) and extends encirclement east of the island while pursuing co-optation through the Straits Forum and economic inducements. The operational specifics — vessel deployments, Scarborough Shoal construction, carrier operations, and the dual-track coercive/engagement pattern — are tracked in the [Taiwan standoff]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/taiwan-standoff.html) conflict file.

The February 2026 [CMC purge]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/cmc-purge-2026.html) shapes China's Taiwan posture: Xi Jinping removed Zhang Youxia and Liu Zhenli — the only CMC members with combat experience — driven by disagreement over the Taiwan timeline (Xi insists on a 2027 invasion capability, while Zhang anticipated full joint operational capability closer to 2035). The purge damages command cohesion, reduces the likelihood of a near-term invasion, and increases the risk of miscalculation by new commanders eager to demonstrate loyalty [7]. See [CMC Purge of 2026]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/cmc-purge-2026.html).

## Relationships: Central Asia & Bangladesh

China is the largest investor in [Central Asia]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/central-asia.html), with 90% of its FDI concentrated in [Kazakhstan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/kazakhstan.html), [Uzbekistan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/uzbekistan.html), and Turkmenistan. The investment pattern is shifting from infrastructure projects to manufacturing relocation and mineral extraction — a transition that deepens structural dependency rather than merely building roads and pipelines [4].

China is extending its influence into [Bangladesh]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/bangladesh.html) through $9.2 billion in proposed investments across infrastructure, logistics, manufacturing, and energy. Prime Minister Tarique Rahman's visit to Beijing produced 13 MOUs and renewed momentum behind the Teesta River project — a long-delayed initiative that [India]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/india.html) and Bangladesh had been unable to agree on. The expansion alarms India but faces limits: Myanmar's instability, Bangladesh's desire for strategic autonomy, and Beijing's strained budget [14].

## Relationships: Asian Containment & NATO-IP4

[George Friedman]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/geopolitical-futures.html) (GPF) identifies an emerging Asian coalition — [India]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/india.html), [Japan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/japan.html), [Australia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/australia.html), [Indonesia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/indonesia.html), New Zealand, and [Vietnam]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/vietnam.html) — as a potentially more significant containment architecture than the Quad. The coalition threatens China on land from India and Vietnam and, in theory, imposes a naval barrier to China's access to the oceans. Indonesia's participation could further limit China's maritime access to Europe and the [Middle East]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html), making China dependent on a trans-Pacific route through the Panama Canal or around South America. The economic dimension of this coalition creates a powerful bloc that could serve as an alternative source of imports for the US, giving Washington more economic leverage over China. China's economy produces more products than it can domestically consume and must export massive amounts to maintain economic health — its largest customer remains the US. The massive economic power of the nations in this potential coalition could redirect trade flows away from China [17].

China also confronts the NATO-IP4 architecture. [RUSI]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/rusi.html) traces how China's backing of [Putin]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/putin.html)'s 2022 escalation catalyzed NATO-IP4 mutual attraction, validating security "indivisibility" between the Euro-Atlantic and Indo-Pacific theatres. China remains unhappy with NATO's Asian partnerships — as Odgaard notes, NATO's outreach reinforces Beijing's "concerns about encirclement and the extension of transatlantic security structures into Asia." The PRC stigmatises "block formation" that runs counter to its interest in separating Asian allies from America, from Europe, and from each other [20].

However, the second Trump administration's NDS treats Euro-Atlantic/Indo-Pacific separation as desirable, and its "constructive strategic stability" détente with China conflicts with "ganging up" via NATO-IP4. This suggests US interests may be diverging from those of NATO's Asian and European partners — creating an opening for China to exploit. The "Europeanisation" of NATO may further complicate Asian engagement: [France]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/france.html) is wary of constraints on China diplomacy, and Spain is pulling toward Beijing. Even if Washington loses interest, the IP4 values access to NATO as a platform for accessing lessons from Ukraine [20].

## A Narrowing Window in Southeast Asia

[Victoria Herczegh]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/geopolitical-futures.html) (GPF) argues that a brief Chinese opening in Southeast Asia has narrowed decisively in 2026. At the height of the Middle East energy crisis, Beijing offered dialogue and economic assistance to vulnerable Southeast Asian states — particularly the [Philippines]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/philippines.html), whose dependence on Gulf energy imports created acute economic pressure. For a brief period, Manila appeared willing to reopen discussions on joint energy development and broader cooperation with Beijing, provided it remained consistent with international law and progress toward a South China Sea Code of Conduct [21].

That opportunity narrowed as [Japan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/japan.html) simultaneously offered assistance in expanding regional energy resilience, as [ASEAN]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/organizations/asean.html) members accelerated their own economic cooperation, and — most importantly — as the rapid acceleration of security cooperation among regional actors fundamentally changed the strategic environment. Instead of moving closer to China in response to perceived US retrenchment, prominent ASEAN states diversified their security relationships with Japan, [Australia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/australia.html), and one another. China's initiatives through the Comprehensive Strategic Partnership, its "3+3" dialogue mechanism with [Vietnam]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/vietnam.html), and its brief engagement with the Philippines have remained comparatively limited in practical strategic effect — overtaken by the more dynamic expansion of the emerging regional security network [21].

The economic-security divergence is the crucial structural finding. Economically, Beijing remains indispensable for ASEAN: trade, investment, supply chains, and infrastructure links give China considerable regional influence that no external actor can easily replace. Security dynamics, however, increasingly follow a different trajectory. As Japan and Australia assume more proactive leadership roles and key ASEAN states deepen cooperation among themselves, Beijing has less room to maneuver. The economic and security spheres are diverging, and the divergence is being driven by ASEAN states' own choices rather than by external pressure [21].

China's growing wariness is visible in its military posture. Beijing has focused on reinforcing relationships within its traditional strategic network — enhancing military coordination with [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) and engaging more with North Korea. It is increasingly criticizing US regional allies, especially Japan. China's rare test-firing of a ballistic missile from a nuclear submarine into the Pacific Ocean in July 2026 is clearly a statement meant to warn neighboring countries. Beijing increasingly faces an interconnected coalition of regional partners intent on preserving stability through cooperation — and if it chooses to intensify maritime power projection, that network is likely to become even more cohesive [21].

## Nuclear Modernization and the Pacific Order

China's nuclear modernization program runs on two tracks that converge on the Pacific: the warhead side and the delivery side. On the warhead side, [Philipp Rombach]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/philipp-rombach.html) (SWP) traces an arsenal expanding from roughly 300 to 600 warheads, on a trajectory toward an estimated 1,500 by 2035 — a buildup that positions China as a third nuclear superpower. But China's testing record is thin: only 45 detonations (against 1,054 for the US and 715 for the Soviet Union), conducted with comparatively rudimentary diagnostic instrumentation. This leaves Beijing with lower confidence in arsenal safety, security, and reliability than its rivals possess, and a corresponding drive to acquire additional test data. Since 2019, US compliance reports have stated that China is probably conducting secret underground nuclear tests at the Lop Nur site; in February 2026 the United States presented new evidence at the UN concerning an alleged June 2020 test. Rombach judges the most plausible purpose as weapons physics and warhead design — validating computer simulations and developing new sub-kiloton tactical warhead designs that would give Beijing additional nuclear response options, especially in a [Taiwan standoff]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/taiwan-standoff.html) contingency — while also advancing warhead miniaturization, improved maneuverability, and survivability, consistent with China's objectives of expanding nuclear capabilities in the western Pacific and achieving regional hegemony [34]. See [China Alleged Nuclear Test]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/china-alleged-june-2020-underground-nuclear-test.html), [Nuclear Proliferation and Arms Control]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/nuclear-proliferation-and-arms-control.html), and [JL-3 SLBM Test]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/jl-3-slbm-test-2026-07-06.html).

The delivery track surfaced on July 6, 2026, when a Chinese nuclear-powered submarine in the South China Sea launched a ballistic missile — assessed as a JL-3 submarine-launched ballistic missile with a reported range near 10,000km — on a 7,300km arc that terminated inside the South Pacific Nuclear Free Zone, the area established by the 1985 Treaty of Rarotonga. This was China's first publicly acknowledged SLBM test into open international waters, and it demonstrated a survivable second-strike nuclear deterrent: the capacity to launch a retaliatory nuclear strike from a protected submarine position after absorbing a first strike. The JL-3 test thus operationalizes the warhead-side modernization — a submarine-based second strike requires miniaturized, maneuverable, survivable warheads of exactly the kind the alleged Lop Nur testing program is designed to validate — and it makes the program's maturity visible to the region [32]. See [China JL-3 SLBM Test]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/jl-3-slbm-test-2026-07-06.html).

The test's landing zone made it a diplomatic event as much as a military one. Ronan Wordsworth ([GPF]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/geopolitical-futures.html)) reads the test as both a milestone and a setback. Leaders from Tuvalu, Vanuatu, Palau, and Solomon Islands condemned the launch, drawing on the region's Cold War nuclear-testing legacy — the US conducted roughly 100 Pacific tests, France nearly twice that — and the lingering displacement and contamination that produced the Rarotonga framework. Yet the 18-member Pacific Islands Forum could not agree on a collective statement, exposing China's capacity to fracture island consensus through diplomatic engagement and aid. On the same day, [Australia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/australia.html) and Fiji signed the [Ocean of Peace Alliance]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/ocean-of-peace-alliance-2026-07-06.html), a mutual defense treaty — suggesting that Beijing's military signaling is catalyzing the very containment architecture it seeks to prevent [32].

That coalition architecture is already visible in a de facto US-Australia division of labor across the Pacific. Washington retains the high-end deterrence role through Compacts of Free Association with Palau, the Marshall Islands, and Micronesia in the northern Pacific; Canberra functions as the everyday security partner across the south. Australia has overtaken China as the region's leading bilateral lender, disbursing $1.5 billion in official development finance during 2024 — 37 percent of the regional total — and committing $2.4 billion in new Pacific loan agreements since 2021. Chinese annual lending has fallen to roughly $91 million since 2020, down sharply from its 2010s peak, with a pivot toward smaller grants. But approximately 40 percent of [Pacific Islands]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/pacific-islands.html) infrastructure projects since 2008 have involved Chinese state-owned enterprises — a structural footprint that diminished lending does not erase. Chinese warships have circumnavigated Australia and conducted live-fire drills in the Tasman Strait, underscoring that reduced financing does not equate to reduced strategic ambition [32].

## Relationships: DragonBear Posture

July 2026 demonstrated the DragonBear's coordination across multiple layers. [Velina Tchakarova]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/velina-tchakarova.html) frames the axis's signature as simultaneity — coordination across all systemically relevant domains (maritime, informational, technological, strategic, experiential) without the formal architecture of alliance [19]. The division of labour is the logic in its purest form: China holds industrial scale and technological weight but has not fought a war in decades; [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) supplies combat experience accumulated at enormous cost. A Russia degraded by war becomes the weaker partner, yet in becoming weaker it transfers hard-won expertise upward to a rising China that can industrialise those lessons at scale [19]. The operational specifics of Joint Sea-2026 and the Kremlin-approved lesson-transfer programme are tracked in the [Cold War 2]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/cold-war-2.html) conflict file.

The one hard boundary is nuclear. As Russian nuclear saber-rattling accelerated through 2026, Beijing delivered Moscow an ultimatum-form warning that there can be no thought of using nuclear weapons in Ukraine. The DragonBear coordinates across every domain except the one where China's global standing and strategic interest diverge absolutely from Russia's escalation logic. Beijing enables the war machine and simultaneously polices its nuclear threshold — the red line runs through Beijing, not Moscow [19].

China also profits from American distraction. Joe Kent, in conversation with [Robert Pape]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/robert-pape.html), asserts that China is "exponentially benefiting" from the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html), positioning itself as a more appealing partner to Middle Eastern states by offering deals instead of regime change. The war erodes US security guarantees and the petrodollar system, demonstrating US limitations to the world and bolstering China's position in [great power competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html), including regarding [Taiwan]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/taiwan-standoff.html) [15]. The Iran ceasefire itself represented a DragonBear course correction: Russia and China green-lit the MoU because uncontrolled escalation risked a [Global System Rupture]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-rupture.html) that would damage their own foundations [5].

# Key Dynamics

- **AI-military integration is China's asymmetric edge.** China lags the US in AI models by months but leads in military integration — the dimension that matters for the AI military race. [2]
- **The growth engine has broken.** The credit-fueled expansion model is exhausted; the economy is export-dependent with no domestic demand mechanism, and fiscal bandwidth for stimulus is near zero. [29][30]
- **Financial repression and bifurcation sustain the export surge.** Capital controls and an undervalued yuan keep Chinese goods competitive; only Chinese-flagged yuan tankers transited Hormuz during the March 2026 closure, while exports shifted to complex capital goods ($471bn→$3.5tn) displacing advanced economies. [9][10][36][37]
- **Strategic culture weaponizes networks and infrastructure.** Cyber pre-positioning in U.S. transportation, energy, and logistics systems is designed to delay, not destroy — shaping the battlespace before conflict. [18][22]
- **The DragonBear axis provides military lesson-transfer without formal alliance, while the CMC purge degrades Taiwan invasion readiness.** Russia's combat experience flows upward to China's industrial scale, coordinated across all domains except nuclear. Xi's removal of combat-experienced CMC members over a 2027-vs-2035 timeline dispute reduces near-term invasion likelihood but raises miscalculation risk. [7][19]
- **An Asian containment coalition is forming.** India, Japan, Australia, Indonesia, Vietnam, and the NATO-IP4 architecture are tightening security ties, diverging from China economically and militarily. [17][20][21]
- **Nuclear arsenal expansion and alleged secret testing mark a new phase in great power arms competition.** US compliance reports since 2019 allege China is conducting secret underground tests at Lop Nur; the arsenal grew from 300 to 600 warheads, projected 1,500 by 2035. [34]
- **The 2026 surplus surge may be the "Big One."** China's raw trade surplus is back to ~7% of GDP with exports +60% vs imports +10% since end-2018, redirected to Europe because Trump 1.0-era protectionism now shields the US — Tooze proposes the 2020s event is qualitatively different from Shock 1.0, not a sequel. [31]

# Analysis

China's strategic position is defined by coordinated action across domains where the US struggles to maintain coherence: AI military integration, maritime grey-zone operations, and resource investment in Central Asia. The DragonBear axis provides a force multiplier, allowing Beijing and Moscow to apply synchronized pressure on the Western coalition. However, China's growth model faces inherent limits that constrain its long-term trajectory.

The DragonBear coordination architecture does not require a formal alliance to reshape the balance of power. The division of labour — China as economic engine, Russia as combat-experienced military partner — is the logic in its purest form. A Russia degraded by war becomes the weaker partner, yet in becoming weaker it transfers expertise upward to a rising China that can industrialise those lessons at scale. The West may face a more capable and assertive PLA as a result [19].

Tooze identifies the "gear-shift in Chinese economic policy" as one of four disrupters of the 2026 world economy — alongside Trump's trade rampage, US fiscal incontinence, and the AI boom. China Shock 2.0 is the export-facing manifestation of this gear-shift. The mercantilist export model is sustained by financial repression: capital controls that wall in wealth, prevent yuan appreciation, and keep Chinese goods artificially competitive. The system's internal tension — between the regime's need for control and the elite's desire to participate in global asset markets — is a structural vulnerability [9][10].

### The Decaying Engine: Structural Limits on Chinese Power

The structural decay of China's growth engine reshapes the competitive landscape. Policy tools that once generated rapid expansion are no longer functional — steady decay, not crisis, is the trajectory [29][30]. The $27 trillion credit bubble of 2008-2017 created an illusion of inexorable rise; its exhaustion has exposed an economy with no domestic demand generation mechanism, entirely dependent on exporting excess production at compressed prices [30]. The $72 trillion banking system, fiscal deficits approaching 10% of GDP, and declining credit flows to productive sectors all signal a state that has lost the levers of economic management [30].

The export surge is the symptom, not the strategy. Weak domestic demand — not industrial policy alone — drives the $1.2 trillion trade surplus [30]. Beijing's shift from carrots to sticks, from market access offers to retaliation threats, reflects diminished capacity rather than strategic confidence [30]. China is already the world's largest exporter; diversifying to genuinely new markets is extremely difficult when the West accounts for most global final consumer demand growth [30]. The threats are now contingent, time-limited, and more deterrable than inexorable — the capacity to execute long-term plans has been impaired by the same fiscal and financial decay that fuels the export surge [30].

The deeper question is whether China can rebalance toward consumption without sacrificing the industrial strategy that underpins its geopolitical ambitions [29]. Beijing appears willing to accept weaker consumption to preserve manufacturing leadership and reduce foreign technology dependence — a trade-off that defines the US-China rivalry [29]. The two geopolitical ecosystems reflect complementary asymmetries: the US-led coalition holds financial primacy, tech innovation, and alliance networks; the DragonBear holds industrial capacity, strategic resources, and military adaptation [29]. China's challenge is less about absolute economic size than about converting manufacturing and technological capacity into sustained productivity-driven growth [29].

### National Total War and Systems Confrontation

Wang & Beauchamp-Mustafaga trace the evolution of China's strategic thinking from comprehensive national power in the 1990s — a systems-engineering approach to integrating and building national strength — through systems confrontation after observing US information-enabled operations in the 1991 Gulf War and the 1999 Kosovo War, to the formal strategy of "national total war" that emerged from combining people's war with systems confrontation during the COVID pandemic. National total war is not total war in the classical sense of unconstrained violence; it describes whole-of-nation mobilization to build civilian capabilities in peacetime that party leaders can weaponize during a conflict. This requires non-military institutions — media, financial systems — to coordinate with the military at a national level and deliver effects, going beyond military-civil fusion modernization and procurement [23].

According to officials in the Academy of Military Science, national total war is already embedded in Xi Jinping's Strong Military Thought and is a critical element of China's military strategic guideline — the highest-level political and military guidance. The strategy prepares the PLA for "future great power wars" in which "confrontation unfolds across all domains and dimensions through national total war." Minister of State Security Chen Yixin has called on all party members to convert "systemic advantages into combat effectiveness," and in 2025 China published official policy directing members to "fight well the national total war" [23]. The 15th Five-Year Plan, issued in early 2026, makes developing the necessary strategic capabilities a priority, providing a benchmark for evaluating whether the broader military adopts operational concepts aligned with this strategy [23].

The operational expression of national total war against [Taiwan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/taiwan.html) is the "all-domain megacity incapacitation warfare" concept, published in the March 2025 edition of *Military Art* — the CMC-overseen internal military journal. The concept integrates military firepower with cognitive effects across three lines of effort — blockade, precision strike, and cognitive attack — with the goal of engineering cognitive submission rather than physical destruction. China's planners are also considering how national total war against the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) would unfold, with researchers in *China Military Science* describing how cognitive effects can "fracture the hegemonic system" [23]. See [Cognitive Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/cognitive-warfare.html) and [Taiwan Standoff]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/taiwan-standoff.html).

## US Munitions Depletion and China's Missile Advantage

[Phillips O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) assesses that the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) has created a structural missile advantage for China. US THAAD interceptor stocks were 53–80% expended by the April ceasefire, and Patriot PAC-3/MSE stocks were 45–61% expended. Combined remaining inventories stand at approximately 1,500 interceptors; with the standard two-per-target doctrine, that yields perhaps 1,000 successful intercepts against China's estimated 3,500 missiles. US bases in Guam and the Marianas — the forward staging architecture for any Pacific conflict — could be devastated at the start. THAAD deliveries are suspended from August 2023 through April 2027, and production is only expanding from 96 to 400 per year. Patriot production runs at approximately 600 per year. Neither rate can replenish the Iran-era drawdown before a potential Taiwan contingency. O'Brien now doubts the US could win even the opening battles of a China war — a revision of his earlier assessments driven by the munitions depletion data [24]. See [Taiwan Standoff]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/taiwan-standoff.html) and [Defense Industrial Resilience]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/defense-industrial-resilience.html).

## DragonBear Military Training and Freedom-of-Navigation Rhetoric

[Velina Tchakarova]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/velina-tchakarova.html)'s Day 144 diagnostic identifies multiple S3 (US-China and DragonBear) domain indicators involving China. Russian military training for the PLA continues — the Kremlin-approved programme transferring Ukraine-war combat lessons to the Chinese military operates as a core DragonBear coordination mechanism. Chinese Coast Guard (CCG) patrols east of [Taiwan]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/taiwan-standoff.html) persist as a gray-zone pressure vector. The diagnostic also notes Iranian transit fees as a revenue stream flowing through the DragonBear architecture, linking the S1 domain (US-Iran War) to S3 through financial pathways [25].

The S3→S7 normative pairing identified in the diagnostic is particularly consequential for China. With both the US and [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) monetizing the Strait of Hormuz — the US through its declared routing regime and toll proposals, Iran through its control of transit — freedom of navigation becomes available for rhetorical capture by the party least committed to enforcing it. The US-China freedom-of-navigation exchange over the Strait creates a normative competition in which China can position itself as the defender of open maritime corridors without bearing the operational costs of enforcement. This rhetorical capture is structurally advantageous: China can invoke freedom of navigation in the Strait of Hormuz while simultaneously conducting CCG patrols east of Taiwan, using one theater's rhetoric to normalize the other's coercion [25].

## China-Russia Military Coordination and the CRINK Question

China's use of [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) as a military pressure multiplier against [Japan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/japan.html) represents a qualitative evolution in the DragonBear's Indo-Pacific posture. Aizawa identifies the December 2025 joint strategic bomber flight as a case where Chinese interests drove the timing and coercive character of the operation: the flight was calibrated to pressure Japan following Takaichi's "survival-threatening situation" statement on Taiwan, with a route extending over 600 miles past the Southwest Islands toward Tokyo. The participation of nuclear-capable H-6N bombers marked a vertical escalation from conventional to nuclear-capable signaling. China can now apply military pressure on Japan with Russian support at times that serve Beijing's political interests — a capability that did not previously exist in operational form [26].

Ueki challenges the framing of CRINK (China, Russia, Iran, North Korea) as a strategic coalition. China's cooperation with each CRINK country remains strictly bilateral; no CRINK military exercise has occurred, and China has not conducted bilateral military exercises with either North Korea or Iran. China opposed a China-Russia-North Korea trilateral exercise proposed by Russian Defense Minister Shoigu in July 2023, and Beijing dismissed the significance of the Putin-Kim Comprehensive Strategic Partnership Treaty. China's support for Russia in Ukraine involves dual-use technology but no military assets — a position of "pro-Russian neutrality" that stops short of overt military assistance. Similarly, China's position on Iran involves indirect support (satellite information, diplomatic advocacy) but remains uncertain and non-declaratory. Ueki argues that China's ambivalence stems from competing strategic objectives: CRINK countries are partners in strategic competition against the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html), but China also seeks recognition as a global power and champion of the Global South — a status incompatible with membership in an "axis of upheaval." The economic data reinforces this asymmetry: China's exports to the US far outweigh exports to Russia (approximately 20 percent of US-level exports to Russia), and Russia's dependence on China has increased, not the reverse [27].

Ueki notes that China has been actively strengthening relations with non-CRINK countries — including ASEAN, [Japan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/japan.html), France, the UK, Germany, and Canada — throughout late 2025 and early 2026. This diplomatic diversification underscores that Beijing's most consequential relationships remain with advanced economies, not with the CRINK periphery. China sees Taiwan as a domestic problem and may prefer not to involve other CRINK countries in a Taiwan contingency, as their involvement would delegitimize China's claim that the issue is internal. Treating CRINK as a unified coalition risks driving the four countries toward closer coordination — the opposite of the intended effect [27]. See [Taiwan Standoff]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/taiwan-standoff.html) and [Great Power Competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html).

## Export Restrictions and the EU-Africa Diversification Response

Kaupenjohann (SWP) documents how China's export restriction strategies are actively catalyzing the very diversification partnerships that threaten its midstream leverage. The 2025 heavy rare earth export restrictions sent immediate industrial stress through global supply chains, demonstrating in practice what had previously been a theoretical risk: concentrated processing capacity can be weaponized at any moment. This follows a broader pattern — the OECD has documented that raw material export restrictions worldwide have quadrupled since 2009, with China's measures representing the most strategically consequential instance [28].

The copper smelting sector reveals a different dimension of China's market power. Chinese smelting capacity has expanded faster than global concentrate supply, driving treatment and refining charges (TC/RCs) into negative territory in 2025 — smelters literally paying miners for concentrate to maintain throughput and defend market share. This overcapacity creates a formidable competitive moat: any new entrant, including prospective [Africa]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/africa.html)n smelters backed by European partnerships, must compete with entrenched Chinese operations running at negative margins and benefiting from accumulated economies of scale [28].

Paradoxically, China's exercise of these leverage points — rare earth export restrictions, smelting overcapacity, and the general pattern of raw material export controls — is driving the strategic response it should logically seek to prevent. The [EU]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/organizations/eu.html) Global Gateway, the Critical Raw Materials Act, and [Germany]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/germany.html)'s Raw Materials Fund are all policy responses to the demonstrated risk of Chinese supply chain weaponization. Africa's approximately 30% share of global critical mineral reserves makes the continent the natural diversification partner, and African industrial policy ambitions — export restrictions on unprocessed minerals, local-content requirements — align strategically with European diversification needs. The "partnership among equals" framing reflects an economic imperative on both sides: the status quo of Chinese supply concentration has become too risky to sustain [28].

## How China Won the Iran War: Non-Combatant Victory Through US Self-Destruction

[Paul Krugman]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/paul-krugman.html) argues that China "won" the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) without being a direct combatant — the war accelerated every trend eroding US power while China collected the geopolitical dividends. The thesis rests on four converging dynamics. First, the Chinese supply chain underpins modern drone warfare — a CSIS December 2025 report confirmed that the components sustaining the Iran war's drone campaigns trace back to Chinese manufacturing. Second, yuan-based sanctions evasion: Iran's ability to sell oil rested on payment in yuan, and ships paying Hormuz passage also paid in yuan or cryptocurrency, functionally neutralizing dollar weaponization. Third, the alliance collapse: Trump's demands that Canada become the 51st state and Denmark hand over Greenland destroyed the coalition architecture that underwrote US power. Fourth, the Pew favorability data: in 12 countries, [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html)'s favorability surpassed the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) between 2023 and 2026 — including Germany (57→27 US vs. 19→33 China), Sweden (55→19 vs. 12→27), and Australia (52→24 vs. 13→31) [33].

On manufacturing, China overtook the US in output in 2010; the US and EU combined still produce more, but that alliance advantage is fading. The non-combatant victory is structural rather than tactical — China did not need to fight; it needed only to watch the US destroy the foundations of its own power. The closing assessment: "When future historians write about this tale of self-immolation, they may well do so in Mandarin" [33]. See [Great Power Competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html) and [Sanctions and Financial Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/sanctions-and-financial-warfare.html).

## The Yuan-Denominated Hormuz Exception

During the March 2026 Hormuz closure, only Chinese-flagged tankers carrying yuan-denominated cargo were permitted sporadic passage through the Strait — a fact documented by Kiel Institute ship-tracking data. This exception is the clearest operational evidence that a parallel transaction system bypassing the petrodollar is functioning alongside the dollar-based order. The yuan cargo exception represents a structural fact about bifurcation in real time: Iran's chokepoint control creates a sanctioned-oil transaction channel that privileges Chinese-flagged shipping and yuan settlement, giving Beijing discounted Iranian oil and a strategic buffer that insulated China from the Hormuz energy shock [36]. See [Global System Bifurcation]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-bifurcation.html) and [Monetary System Transition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/monetary-system-transition.html).

## Export Growth, Complexity Shift, and Third-Market Displacement

A Kiel Institute study quantifies the scale and structural transformation of China's export expansion. Goods exports grew more than seven-fold, from $471 billion in 2002 to $3.5 trillion in 2024, lifting China's share of world exports from 7.6 percent to 16 percent. Over the same period, China's comparative advantage broadened from approximately 1,800 product categories to over 2,500 — and the composition shifted decisively toward capital and intermediate goods (consumption goods fell from 52 percent of the export basket in 2002 to 28 percent in 2024, while capital goods rose from 14 percent to 27 percent and intermediate goods from 34 percent to 45 percent). The Product Complexity Index profile shows China's complexity rising markedly, while [Germany]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/germany.html), [Japan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/japan.html), and the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) remained essentially flat. China's market-share gains in third markets fall almost entirely on advanced economies, particularly in complex manufacturing sectors — vehicles, machinery, chemicals, and electronics — confirming that the "Shock 2.0" is a deliberate industrialization push into the high-complexity space where advanced-economy competitiveness was previously unchallenged [37]. See [China Shock and Deindustrialization]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/china-shock-and-deindustrialization.html).

## AI as Collapse Amplifier: The Thermodynamic Counter-Argument

[The Honest Sorcerer]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/the-honest-sorcerer.html) extends the civilizational collapse framework to the AI techno-savior narrative. AI/robotics and space colonization — proposals to save primacy and ultimately modernity — increase complexity, energy, and material use. In effect, these non-solutions lock civilization more firmly into the techno-industrial progress trap and accelerate collapse by intensifying resource draw-down and ecological destruction. Automating and electrifying everything would take far more energy than this civilization could harvest from its dwindling resource base. This is a thermodynamic counter-argument to the "AI as productivity rescue" framing that underpins both US and Chinese economic strategy [35].

China is not exempt from this logic. Both the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) and China are expressions of the same growth imperative running into the same thermodynamic limits. China's energy-intensive, export-driven model — already documented as facing structural decay — faces the identical constraint: it is impossible to grow forever on a finite planet, and adding more technological complexity (AI, automation, electrification) raises the energy cost of energy rather than lowering it. The progress trap operates identically regardless of political system: each additional investment in complexity yields smaller relative benefits until the cost of maintaining the system outstrips the returns. China's pursuit of AI-driven military modernization and industrial upgrading — the core of its [great power competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html) strategy — is thus subject to the same diminishing marginal returns that constrain the US, though China's lower per-capita energy consumption provides more headroom in the near term [35]. See [Civilizational Collapse and Progress Traps]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/civilizational-collapse-and-progress-traps.html) and [Great Power Competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html).
