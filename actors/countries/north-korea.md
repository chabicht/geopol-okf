# Background

North Korea (DPRK) is a single-party authoritarian state governed by the Workers' Party of Korea under the leadership of Kim Jong Un. The political system offers no meaningful pluralism: elections feature single-candidate ballots, Freedom House rates political rights at 0 out of 40, and an extensive network of political prison camps sustains regime control through coercion. The state's ideological foundation — *Juche* (self-reliance) and *Songun* (military-first politics) — has evolved under Kim Jong Un into a posture that places nuclear weapons at the center of national identity and survival, a shift now codified in the constitutional amendments of May 2026 [1].

# Current Situation

## Nuclear Command and Doctrine

The May 2026 constitutional amendments granted explicit nuclear command authority to the chairman of the State Affairs Commission (SAC) for the first time. A new Article 89 permits the SAC chairman to delegate nuclear command authority to a designated nuclear command organization, establishing a legal framework for decentralized nuclear command and control (NC2). This decentralization is a deliberate counter-decapitation measure: the US and [South Korea]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/south-korea.html) have developed operational concepts — "Kill Chain" preemptive strike and "Korea Massive Punishment and Retaliation" (KMPR) — that target North Korean leadership in a crisis. By dispersing launch authority, Pyongyang aims to ensure that a decapitation strike cannot neutralize the nuclear arsenal [1].

The dispersion-survival logic carries an inherent tradeoff. Dispersed nuclear forces may survive a first strike but risk being cut off from central command, creating pre-delegation dilemmas and the danger of unauthorized or accidental use. The constitutional delegation provision is a necessary but insufficient step: it addresses the legal authority to fire under degraded communications but does not resolve the technical and doctrinal challenges of ensuring reliable positive control across a distributed nuclear force [1].

Denuclearization has become increasingly impossible. Nuclear weapons are now more essential to North Korea's security than at any previous point, embedded in the constitution itself. This constitutional entrenchment may embolden Pyongyang to take provocative actions, calculating that its deterrent shields it from decisive retaliation. The amendments signal that nuclear capability is not a bargaining chip but a permanent feature of the state's identity [1].

## Inter-Korean Relations

The amendments redefine North Korea's borders in Article 2: the DPRK now formally borders [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) and [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) to the north and the Republic of Korea to the south. All references to reunification have been deleted from the constitution's preamble, severing the legal and ideological basis for treating South Korea as a partner in a shared national project [1].

The "hostile two states" policy has been codified at the constitutional level. North Korea has abandoned the term "Nam-Joseon" (South Joseon) in favor of "Republic of Korea," treating the ROK as a separate and adversarial foreign state rather than a wayward part of the Korean nation. Inter-Korean organizations have been abolished since January 2024, dismantling the institutional infrastructure that once facilitated dialogue. Border militarization has intensified: Kim Jong Un ordered the expansion of long-range artillery along the southern border with Seoul as the primary target, and inter-Korean railways and roads have been demolished. These physical and institutional barriers undermine efforts by South Korean President Lee Jae Myung to de-escalate tensions [1].

Abandoning unification also serves a domestic control function. By framing South Korea as a hostile foreign power, Pyongyang invalidates ROK demands for denuclearization as illegitimate external interference. The regime seeks to deny information penetration from the South — treating cultural and informational inflows as acts of hostility rather than exchanges between co-nationals [1].

## Defense Industrial Policy

Article 60 of the amended constitution mandates the development of defense science and technology, calling for a "Juche-oriented, modernized, scientific" defense industry. This constitutional provision signals determination to resist any external pressure to halt military modernization — the regime is embedding weapons development in the highest law of the land. Article 61 establishes a "military-first atmosphere" and directs the state to prepare the population for "resistance warfare," effectively constitutionalizing civilian mobilization for national defense [1].

Kim Jong Un has directed the development of artificial intelligence and space technology for national defense applications, as articulated at the 9th Workers' Party Congress. These directives place advanced dual-use technologies within the military-first framework, expanding the scope of North Korea's defense industrial ambition beyond traditional munitions and missile production into emerging domains [1].

## Russia-Ukraine War Involvement

North Korea is no longer merely an ammunition supplier to [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) but has become directly engaged in the [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html). North Korean troops have participated in combat operations in the Kursk region of Russia alongside Russian forces against [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html), with additional deployments anticipated. The scale of potential involvement remains uncertain: unconfirmed reports suggest deployments could reach 30,000–50,000 personnel, though these figures have not been verified. Such forces could serve in reserve, engineering, logistics, missile operations, or training capacities rather than purely front-line combat [2].

# Key Dynamics

- **Nuclear weapons constitutionalized as permanent identity.** The May 2026 amendments embed nuclear centrality in the constitution itself, making denuclearization negotiation effectively impossible. [1]
- **Decentralized nuclear command is a counter-decapitation measure.** New Article 89 permits delegating launch authority to survive US/ROK "Kill Chain" preemptive strikes, but creates pre-delegation and accidental-use risks. [1]
- **"Hostile two states" codified at constitutional level.** Reunification references deleted, ROK treated as an adversarial foreign state, inter-Korean institutions abolished, and border militarization intensified. [1]
- **Abandoning unification serves domestic control.** Framing South Korea as a hostile foreign power invalidates ROK denuclearization demands and justifies blocking cultural and informational inflows. [1]
- **Kim Jong Un elevated as "head of state."** The SAC chairman's constitutional powers were expanded over the legislature and redefined as head of state, distancing Kim from the predecessor personality cult. [1]
- **Welfare promises dropped to acknowledge economic failure.** Constitutional commitments to tax abolition, free medical care, and no unemployment were removed — a pragmatic retreat from socialist doctrine the state cannot deliver. [1]

# Analysis

## Constitutional Power Structure

The May 2026 amendments restructured the constitutional hierarchy of state organs. The section on the SAC chairman was moved ahead of the Supreme People's Assembly (SPA), signaling a formal elevation of executive authority over the legislature. The SAC chairman's powers were expanded to include the ability to suspend, appoint, and dismiss SPA leadership during recesses, veto laws passed by the SPA, and receive foreign credentials — functions that consolidate diplomatic and legislative prerogatives in a single office. Simultaneously, the SPA's authority to summon the SAC chairman was removed, eliminating a procedural check [1].

The SAC chairman was redefined as the "head of state representing the DPRK," replacing the previous designation of "supreme leader." This is not merely a terminological shift: it creates a constitutional title that functions in international diplomatic settings, enabling Kim Jong Un to engage in summit diplomacy and receive credentials without relying on the legacy frameworks of his predecessors [1].

## Regime Legitimation

North Korea's legitimation strategy is shifting from personality cult to "democratic" process legitimation. The "head of state" title positions Kim Jong Un as a constitutionally embedded executive rather than a charismatic heir, a framing designed for international audiences and domestic elites who may be tiring of dynastic mysticism. Most references to Kim Il Sung and Kim Jong Il have been removed from the constitution's preamble: Kim Il Sung's name appears only once, compared to 21 times in the 2023 constitution. Kim Jong Un has also scaled down state celebrations of predecessor anniversaries, further distancing his rule from the cult apparatus [1].

The "people-first" principle is replacing the cult of personality as the main propaganda theme. This rhetorical pivot does not democratize the regime but provides an alternative basis for legitimacy that does not depend on the supernatural attributes of the Kim lineage. The amendments also eliminate doctrinal contradictions created by the "hostile two states" policy, which had clashed with predecessor-era constitutional demands for unification — removing those demands resolves the ideological incoherence [1].

## Economic Reality

The amendments omitted references to tax abolition, the claim of "no unemployment," and "universal free medical care" — provisions that had been constitutional promises the state could no longer credibly deliver. The reduction of constitutional welfare responsibilities acknowledges the gap between socialist doctrine and material reality. The scale of failure is documented: 72 percent of defectors surveyed between 2016 and 2022 reported never receiving state food rations; the Sinuiju greenhouse showcase project is non-operational; North Korea ranks 149th out of 172 countries on the Social Progress Index [1].

The revision may enable future taxation — removing the constitutional prohibition on taxes opens fiscal space the state desperately needs. It may also signal laxer regulation of the *Jangmadang* (informal market) economy, tacitly accepting the markets that have sustained the population since the 1990s famine. A complete abandonment of socialist distribution is unlikely, but the constitutional retreat from maximalist welfare promises marks a pragmatic adjustment to economic reality [1].

## Strategic Posture Synthesis

Taken together, the May 2026 constitutional amendments codify three interlocking shifts. First, they entrench the centrality of nuclear weapons to North Korea's identity and security, making denuclearization negotiation effectively impossible. Second, they sever the legal and ideological basis for engagement with [South Korea]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/south-korea.html), replacing unification aspirations with a permanently adversarial two-state framework. Third, they free Kim Jong Un from the constitutional and ideological constraints of his predecessors' legacies, allowing him to govern through a "head of state" framework that suits diplomatic engagement and domestic consolidation alike. These shifts position North Korea as a more assertive actor within the [Cold War 2]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/cold-war-2.html) landscape, aligned with [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) and [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) in the broader [Great Power Competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html) while pursuing an independent strategic agenda centered on regime survival and nuclear recognition [1].

## Mutual Benefits of North Korean–Russian Military Cooperation

The deepening North Korean involvement in the [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html) serves both parties' strategic interests. For North Korea, the conflict provides unprecedented combat experience with UAVs, electronic warfare, and modern combined-arms operations — capabilities relevant to the [Drone Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/drone-warfare.html) domain and the broader [Revolution in Military Affairs]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/revolution-in-military-affairs.html) that cannot be acquired through training alone. For Russia, North Korean personnel provide manpower and force depth at a time when Russian conventional forces are under sustained pressure. [Velina Tchakarova]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/velina-tchakarova.html) assesses that this mutual benefit calculus is deepening the alignment between Pyongyang and Moscow within the broader Eurasian power contest [2].
