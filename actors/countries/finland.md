# Background

Finland shares an 1,340-km border with [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) — the longest EU-Russia border — and occupies a strategic position on the Baltic Sea's northern shore. After decades of military non-alignment anchored by the Paasikivi-Kekkonen doctrine of Finnish-Soviet friendship, Finland's security calculus shifted decisively following Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022. Helsinki applied for NATO membership alongside Sweden, completing accession in April 2023. Finland is now NATO's newest member on the Baltic frontier, transforming the alliance's northern flank and bringing the combined Nordic air fleet to over 250 fourth- and fifth-generation aircraft [1].

Finland's defense posture is built on a robust conscription-based reserve system, advanced air capabilities (64 F-35A fighters on order), and naval modernization featuring four Pohjanmaa-class corvettes. The country has consistently met or exceeded NATO's 2% GDP defense spending target and is moving toward the 5% target by 2035 alongside Poland, the Baltics, and Denmark [1].

# Current Situation

## The Åland Question

When Finland negotiated NATO accession, both the alliance and Helsinki accepted the existing legal status of the [Åland Islands]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/aland-islands-convention.html). Finland's 2024 Government report on foreign and security policy stated explicitly: "Finland's NATO membership does not affect the status of the Åland Islands, which is based on international conventions." An April 2026 Government report reaffirmed compliance with international law as a cornerstone of Finnish security without recommending any revision to Åland's demilitarized status. NATO's Baltic Sentry mission operates in the Baltic while respecting Åland's special regime [1].

The debate within Finland continues. Alpo Rusi called Åland "the Achilles' heel of Finland's defence," while Pekka Toveri argued the demilitarization clause no longer holds. A 2022 poll showed 58% of mainland Finns supporting military presence on the islands. Yet the Finnish government's formal position remains one of treaty compliance — reflecting an assessment that the diplomatic costs of abrogation outweigh the modest military gains, and that Finnish forces are adequate without permanent stationing on Åland [1].

## Strategic Position

Finland's NATO membership has fundamentally altered Baltic Sea geometry. The Baltic is now effectively a NATO lake with all littoral states except Russia as members or allies. Finland's proximity to Russia's military-industrial base — much of which lies within several hundred kilometres of Finnish territory — gives Helsinki both offensive reach and forward-defense exposure. Finland hosts NATO Forward Land Forces and participates in the combined Nordic air fleet arrangement [1].

# Key Dynamics

- **Treaty compliance as strategic asset**: Finland's government positions adherence to the Åland Convention as a signal of reliability — a reputation for keeping agreements being itself a strategic asset for smaller states.
- **Baltic NATO transformation**: Finnish (and Swedish) accession has made the Baltic Sea a NATO-dominated theater, reducing the relative military significance of any single chokepoint.
- **Russian hybrid threat**: Finland shares the long EU-Russia border and faces persistent hybrid pressure — sabotage, disinformation, and migrant instrumentalization.
