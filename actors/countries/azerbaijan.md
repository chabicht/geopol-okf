# Background

Azerbaijan is a small but strategically significant state of roughly 10 million people situated at the intersection of [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) to the north, [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) to the south, and [Turkey]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/turkey.html) to the west. Its geographic position makes it a natural arena for great-power competition, while its energy resources and military capabilities give it leverage disproportionate to its size. The country won the Nagorno-Karabakh war in 2023 with Turkish military support, consolidating territorial control and deepening its strategic partnership with Ankara. Ethnic Azeris constitute approximately 95% of the population, giving the state a high degree of ethnic cohesion unusual for the region [1].

The Aliyev dynasty has governed Azerbaijan since the Soviet collapse. Heydar Aliyev, a former Soviet intelligence official, served as president from 1993 to 2003; his son Ilham Aliyev succeeded him and has ruled since, with Mehriban Aliyeva serving as Vice President since 2017. The regime is authoritarian but stable, combining dynastic succession with energy-funded patronage and a multi-vector foreign policy that keeps all major powers engaged without committing exclusively to any one [1].

Azerbaijan has maintained close ties with [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html) since independence — a relationship that includes Israeli assistance in developing Azerbaijan's military capabilities. The partnership is partly strategic and partly geopolitical: Israel provides technology and defense expertise, while Azerbaijan offers a perch on Iran's northern border and a corridor for intelligence operations. With the US and Israel at war with Iran, Azerbaijan's role as a border state with access to Iran's large Azeri minority elevates its strategic significance [1].

# Key Dynamics

- **Geographic pivot of three powers.** Azerbaijan sits at the intersection of Russia, Iran, and Turkey, forcing a multi-vector diplomacy that engages all three while committing to none. [1]
- **Karabakh victory cemented military credibility.** The 2023 Nagorno-Karabakh victory, achieved with Turkish support, demonstrated Azerbaijan's capacity for sustained combined-arms operations and consolidated its territorial sovereignty. [1]
- **The Azeri minority in Iran is a latent pressure point.** Azeris are Iran's third-largest ethnic group at roughly 25% of the population; the Iran-Azerbaijan border is consequently a sensitive zone where ethnic kinship intersects with geopolitical rivalry. [1]
- **Israel partnership is deep and long-standing.** Israeli military assistance since independence gives Azerbaijan a technological edge and provides Israel with a strategic foothold on Iran's northern border. [1]
- **The Trump Route elevates Azerbaijan's corridor value.** The US-mediated Armenia-Azerbaijan agreement connecting Azerbaijan to its Nakhchivan exclave along the Iranian border — onward to Turkey and Europe — transforms Azerbaijan from a terminal node into a transit hub. [1]
- **Energy wealth underwrites strategic autonomy.** Caspian Sea offshore oil and gas fields provide the revenue base for Azerbaijan's independent foreign policy and military modernization. [1]

# Analysis

## Multi-Vector Diplomacy

Azerbaijan's foreign policy is defined by engagement with all major powers simultaneously. The US, [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html), and the [EU]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/organizations/eu.html) all maintain active relationships with Baku, each seeking influence over energy flows and transit corridors. Russia remains a factor through proximity and historical legacy, but the [South Caucasus]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/south-caucasus.html) is, in Friedman's assessment, "in some ways the soft underbelly of Russia" — a region where Moscow's grip has weakened as the Ukraine war degrades its capacity to project power southward [1].

## Israel Ties and the Iran Border

Azerbaijan's relationship with Israel gives it a military-technological advantage and provides Israel with strategic depth on Iran's northern flank. The partnership is particularly significant in the context of the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html): with the US and Israel engaged in kinetic conflict with Iran, Azerbaijan's position along the Iranian border — and its potential influence on Iran's Azeri minority — makes it an important player whose choices could affect the war's trajectory. Friedman notes that possible influence on Iranian Azeris is a card Baku can play or withhold depending on its own strategic calculus [1].

## The Trump Route

The Trump Route for International Peace and Prosperity (TRIPP) is a US-mediated agreement between Armenia and Azerbaijan that connects Azerbaijan to its Nakhchivan exclave along the Iranian border, creating a corridor onward to Turkey and Europe. The route transforms Azerbaijan from a terminal energy node into a critical transit hub linking the [Caspian Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/caspian.html) basin to European markets via Turkey. It also runs along Iran's northern border, making it a geopolitical instrument that potentially constrains Tehran's northern reach [1].

## The Caspian Dimension

Azerbaijan's Caspian Sea offshore oil and gas fields are the foundation of its economic independence. The [Caspian Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/caspian.html) has become an increasingly militarized theater as the US-Iran and Russia-Ukraine wars spill over into the basin, placing Azerbaijan's energy infrastructure within the zone of potential conflict expansion. Baku's incentive is to keep the Caspian a commercial waterway, not a battlefield — a position shared by the other trans-Caspian states promoting the Middle Corridor [1][2].
