# Background

Libya remains divided between the UN-recognized Government of National Unity (GNU) in the west and the Libyan National Army (LNA) under Khalifa Haftar in the east. Oil revenues continue to finance both rival factions. The country's energy exports make it a potential beneficiary of higher oil/gas prices from the Hormuz crisis, but higher fertilizer and food costs plus reduced Gulf remittances are negative spillovers.

# Current Situation

The Hormuz crisis has mixed effects: higher oil revenues benefit both factions, but increased import costs for food and fertilizer, combined with reduced remittances from Libyan workers in the Gulf, create social pressure. Libya's fragmentation prevents coherent exploitation of its elevated energy strategic position. # Key Dynamics

- **Dual-faction oil dependency.** Oil revenues finance both the GNU in the west and the LNA under Haftar in the east, binding energy income to political fragmentation. [1]
- **Mixed Hormuz spillover.** Higher oil revenues benefit both factions, but increased food and fertilizer import costs plus reduced Gulf remittances create social pressure. [1]
- **Fragmentation blocks strategic exploitation.** Libya's political division prevents coherent exploitation of its elevated energy strategic position during the Hormuz crisis. [1]
- **Energy exports as factional lifeline.** Oil and gas exports make Libya a potential beneficiary of higher prices, but revenue sustains rival armed factions rather than national governance. [1]
