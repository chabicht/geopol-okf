# Background

Inflation is growing throughout the world, from Asia to the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html). The IMF forecasts global inflation will increase to 4.4% by end-2026 — nearly one percentage point higher than originally predicted. The [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) and the blockade of the Strait of Hormuz predictably shocked the energy market, resulting in higher prices for resources and transportation due to increased freight costs, insurance premiums, and extended delivery times. The [UAE]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/uae.html)'s exit from OPEC and strikes on production and infrastructure facilities compounded the disruption [1].

The timing is especially bad. In 2022-2023, before the war in Iran, inflation was already a problem — triggered by COVID-19 supply chain disruptions, demand shifts, and large-scale economic support measures. Then came the [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html) and associated sanctions on [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html), triggering another rise in energy prices. Things improved in 2024-2025 with reduced inflationary pressures and monetary easing, but inflation remained higher than expected, even in the US, as did interest rates [1].

The through line is geopolitical risk, not market forces. The sharp rise in energy prices is driven more by trade policy uncertainty, the complexity and cost of bypass shipping routes, and the unpredictability of the Iran war. The IEA reported that oil and fuel shipments through the Strait of Hormuz fell by nearly 1.8 million barrels per day in Q1 2026, warning that the global oil market will likely remain significantly undersupplied until October even if the conflict ends soon [1].

# Current Situation

## Regional Breakdown

The energy shock lands unevenly across advanced economies. The [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) entered the crisis with April CPI at 3.8% year on year, energy accounting for more than 40% of the monthly rise and the energy index up almost 18% over twelve months. The Fed held its rate at 3.5-3.75%, explicitly citing higher global energy prices and Middle East uncertainty. Although the US imports only about 10% of its oil from the Middle East, fertilizer imports from the region drove sharp rises in nitrogen fertilizer prices (urea, ammonium nitrate). The deeper concern is transmission from volatile goods to core indicators: annualized core CPI reached 2.8%, above the Fed's 2% target. The eurozone is the most exposed advanced-economy bloc — April CPI hit 3% with energy prices accelerating 10.9%, and the ECB's March baseline projected 2026 inflation at 2.6% against growth of only 0.9%. National figures ranged from 2.9% in Germany and 2.8% in Italy to 2.5% in [France]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/france.html) and 3.4% in Poland. The [United Kingdom]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-kingdom.html)'s CPI was 3.3%; the Bank of England held at 3.75% and warned of further rises, as fuel and food feed quickly into household inflation psychology. After eight consecutive rate cuts the ECB held its deposit rate at 2%, but expansion of core inflation may force a return to tighter policy — creating the clearest stagflation policy trade-off among major economies [1].

Emerging markets absorb the shock with even less cushion. Asia's giants — China, [India]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/india.html), and [Japan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/japan.html) — import 60-70% of their oil from the Middle East. India is the most vulnerable, importing nearly 85% of its fuel (the vast majority through Hormuz); April inflation rose to 3.48%, a 13-month high, with wholesale inflation at a 3.5-year high and food inflation at 4.2%. Sustained high oil prices worsen India's current account deficit, increase dollar demand, depress the rupee, and amplify imported inflation. China showed relative resilience — April CPI at 1% month on month and 1.2% year on year, transitioning from deflation to inflation — supported by an "appropriately accommodative" policy and $3.34 trillion in forex reserves. Japan imports over 90% of its oil from the Middle East (80% through Hormuz); core inflation accelerated to 1.8% and the BoJ held at 0.75% but faces pressure to raise rates, risking slower growth and making Japan more likely to experience stagflation than China. [Turkey]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/turkey.html) raised its year-end inflation target from 16% to 24% and suspended its forecast range as annual CPI hit 32.37% — the central bank funds banks at a 40% overnight rate against a 37% base rate, battling the interaction of imported energy, exchange-rate sensitivity, and the need to preserve disinflation credibility. [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) gains fiscal revenue from high oil prices, but inflation (Rosstat: 5.58% in April) and sanctions offset the benefit; the Bank of Russia cut its key rate from 15% to 14.5% while weekly international reserves reached $771 billion in May — a war economy where the state is the dominant agent and higher prices improve near-term fiscal arithmetic even as they worsen conditions for internationally exposed firms. Central Asia is less directly affected by the energy disruption: [Kazakhstan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/kazakhstan.html)'s inflation fell to 10.6% and [Uzbekistan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/uzbekistan.html)'s to a historic low of 7%, with Russia continuing as a key energy supplier, though the region still labors under elevated inflation and extremely tight monetary conditions. In Latin America, [Mexico]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/latin-america.html)'s April inflation was 4.45% (Banxico cut to 6.5% but cited the Iran war as a reason for inflation's duration), while Brazil's accelerated to 4.39% with food and beverage prices rising 1.34% monthly at a 14.5% policy rate — making Brazil more attractive than Turkey or Mexico in a commodity-led oil shock because its external cushion is better and inflation credibility stronger [1].

## Scenarios, Business Impact, and Investor Risk

GPF identifies three scenarios, all anchored to the fact that the baseline entering mid-May 2026 already embeds an oil shock relative to prewar assumptions [1]. Under the **mild** scenario, de-escalation within 2-3 months and no sustained Hormuz closure would hold Brent at $80-$95 over six months, with governments slowing rate cuts briefly and business sentiment reviving cautiously. The **baseline** (most likely) scenario envisions periodic attacks, shipping friction, and higher insurance costs at Brent $95-$110, with governments slowing rate cuts and waiting as rising costs and declining purchasing activity slow investment and lower economic growth. The **stagflation** scenario — least desirable — involves intermittent effective Hormuz closure for up to three months, broader sanctions, and Brent $115-$140, forcing central banks to tighten amid currency depreciation to 2022 levels and beyond, culminating in recession.

The shock's transmission to business is gradual: fastest in retail fuel and traded goods, slower in utilities (3-6 months), food and wages (6-12 months), supply chain effects (3-24 months), and second-round effects (1-3 years). Inflation pressures supply chain management as higher costs produce production delays and material shortages, while tight monetary policy raises borrowing costs and limits investment in capital projects, expansion, and innovation — with small and medium-sized enterprises particularly vulnerable. Supply chain strategy is shifting from "friend-shoring" to node-by-node vulnerability mapping, focusing on Gulf shipping lanes, petrochemical intermediates, fertilizers, energy-intensive components, and routes where insurance and war-risk premiums can spike faster than physical freight rates. Firms are prequalifying alternate suppliers, building supplier finance contingency lines, and considering minimum inventory buffers only where substitution times are long; contract architecture now matters through fuel surcharge clauses, forex indexation bands, reopener clauses at defined Brent thresholds, sanctions compliance escape clauses, and rerouting/insurance pass-through language for maritime trade [1].

The effects are felt asymmetrically across investors. The US remains the best large market for relative resilience; Brazil is more attractive than Turkey and Mexico in a commodity-led oil shock; Japan is the most exposed developed equity market if the oil shock coincides with a weakened yen; in Europe, exporters with pricing power and limited energy intensity can outperform, but broad industrial beta is exposed; India is structurally attractive long-term yet among the most exposed major economies to a prolonged Gulf energy shock in the near to medium term. If inflation persists, markets will prioritize resilience, pricing power, and geopolitical insulation over rapid growth — accelerating capital rotation toward commodities, defense, energy, and inflation-resistant sectors [1].

# Key Dynamics

- **Geopolitical risk, not market forces, is the primary driver**: The inflation surge is driven by trade policy uncertainty, bypass shipping costs, and Iran war unpredictability — not cyclical economic dynamics.
- **Asymmetric impact**: Countries heavily dependent on imports with weak currencies, large fiscal deficits, and limited monetary credibility are disproportionately affected. Strong reserve currencies, diversified economies, and credible institutions absorb shocks better.
- **Core inflation transmission**: Sustained energy price pressure gradually contributes to core inflation through food, transportation, agriculture, and industry — driving up the overall price of less sensitive goods.
- **Central bank caution**: The Fed, ECB, and BoE have all signaled discomfort with the inflation side of the shock even while acknowledging weaker growth. The IMF has advised central banks to prepare to raise rates if the ceasefire breaks down.
- **Resilience over efficiency**: Prolonged inflation and geopolitical instability accelerate capital rotation toward commodities, defense, energy, and inflation-resistant sectors, fundamentally reshaping investment priorities.

# Analysis

## Inflation as a Cascade Vector

The inflationary shock is a secondary cascade of the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html), operating on its own timeline even as the primary energy shock is reversed. The [Tindale-Tchakarova Cascade]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-rupture.html) model — with 6-12 month lags for food and wage effects, and 1-3 year horizons for second-round effects — explains why inflation will persist well after the trigger event is addressed. The World Bank's projection of fertilizer prices up 31% and urea up 60% in 2026 confirms that the agricultural dimension is intensifying even as the energy dimension recedes [1].

## Connection to Global System Rupture

The inflationary spiral is a primary mechanism through which the [Global System Rupture]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-rupture.html) propagates. Geopolitical risk drives inflation, which forces central banks to tighten or pause easing, which slows growth, which increases political risk — a vicious cycle. The asymmetry of impact across regions means that the rupture's intensity varies by theater even as its direction is consistent: persistent cost inflation and volatility everywhere, but stagflation risk concentrated in the most exposed economies [1].

## Hormuz Blockade: Fertilizer-to-Food Price Cascade

Colibasanu traces the inflationary transmission from the Hormuz blockade through fertilizer to food prices. Approximately one-third of globally traded urea and nearly half of seaborne sulfur transit the strait, with Middle East exports representing around 40% of global urea supply (22 million metric tons annually). At the New Orleans hub, fertilizer prices rose from $516 to $683 per metric ton in the conflict's first week. The cascade operates through reduced fertilizer application rates — farmers facing doubled input costs apply less — leading to lower agricultural output, which feeds into food inflation on the 6-12 month lag documented in the cascade model above [2].

The fiscal dimension compounds the inflationary mechanism. Governments in import-dependent economies face a choice between absorbing higher fertilizer subsidy costs to protect farmers' margins or allowing the cost to pass through to food prices. Colibasanu emphasizes that the effects are most severe in economies with limited fiscal capacity, where the government cannot subsidize the gap and farmers cannot absorb it — meaning the fertilizer-to-food cascade hits hardest where resilience is already weakest. This reinforces the asymmetric impact pattern identified in the regional breakdown: countries with weak currencies, large fiscal deficits, and limited monetary credibility bear disproportionate costs [2].

## Central Bank Stress: ECB and Fed Repricing

[Velina Tchakarova]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/velina-tchakarova.html)'s Day 144 and Day 150 diagnostics trace a synchronized central-bank stress pattern as the Financial System Stress domain (S6) holds at CRITICAL. On the ECB side, the policy collision is explicit: the energy price surge pushes inflation upward while the growth shock from energy costs and corridor closures pushes toward easing — a classic stagflation trap. The [ECB]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/organizations/eu.html) Governing Council kept all three key rates unchanged on 23 July, describing the energy-price outlook as "well above the levels recorded prior to the conflict" and flagging that the full inflationary impact has yet to play out. Market pricing now carries roughly 70 percent odds of a hike at the 10 September meeting, and the ECB's ceiling on further support has been named explicitly — a departure from the ambiguity that characterised the June assessment [3][4].

The Fed faces the same dilemma without its traditional cushion. The Fed's hiking repricing holds — markets price a higher-for-longer trajectory despite weakening growth — yet there is no safe-haven Treasury bid. The absence of the traditional flight-to-quality dynamic during a systemic crisis suggests that the US fiscal position itself is being questioned by markets, removing the last monetary buffer from the global financial system. Without automatic stabilization through Treasury demand, the Fed must choose between allowing inflation to run hot or tightening into a growth shock, amplifying rather than absorbing the energy-driven shock [3]. The stress-testing anchor has meanwhile shifted upward: Brent settled at USD 100.69 — a single-session gain of approximately 7 percent and roughly 30 percent above the USD 71.57 level recorded on 1 July. RBC's Helima Croft has stated that extreme pressure could send Brent above the 2022 war-driven high of USD 128 and, in a full regional war scenario, toward the 2008 peak of USD 146. This range should now anchor stress-testing rather than the USD 90 to 95 range carried through prior assessments. The combination of the ECB's explicit inflation warning and the Brent threshold crossing means the monetary authority cannot look through an energy shock it has itself flagged as incomplete, and the market is pricing accordingly [4].
