# Background

Joseph Tainter's "Collapse of Complex Societies" frames collapse not as a sudden catastrophe but as an involuntary loss of institutional, legal, and technological complexity — a long, non-linear process with ups, downs, and periods of stasis. Societies are problem-solving organizations that respond to challenges by increasing structural and organizational complexity, each increment requiring a continuous and escalating input of resources and energy. Because the easiest problems with the largest benefits are solved first, each additional investment in complexity yields smaller marginal returns. When maintenance costs outstrip returns, the system becomes brittle and vulnerable to collapse [1].

Ronald Wright's "progress traps" extend this logic to technology. Innovations that yield short-term benefits create compounding unforeseen problems that cannot be easily solved — partly because the solution would threaten the existing status quo. From cliff-driving hunters who wiped out their food source to Sumerian irrigation that salinized the soil, progress promotes ecological overshoot and resource dependence. Technology, like social complexity, hits diminishing marginal returns: a higher effort is required at each incremental step just to maintain the system [1].

Lotka's maximum power principle (1925) adds the thermodynamic dimension. Systems that survive in competition are those that develop more power inflow and use it best to meet survival needs. "Industrialize or be colonized" is the logic: nations cannot voluntarily reduce energy use without risking malnutrition, discontent, and exploitation by others. This creates a double bind — the path that maximizes power is also the path that accelerates resource depletion and ecological destruction [1].

[The Honest Sorcerer]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/the-honest-sorcerer.html) integrates these three frameworks to argue that industrial modernity is the mother of all progress traps: already snapped shut in the West, triggering a slow-motion collapse of the energy-technology Jenga tower built over two centuries. The question is not whether a dead end will be reached, but how many more must be hit before a way through is found.

# Key Dynamics

- **Complexity hits diminishing returns.** Each additional investment in sociopolitical complexity yields smaller relative benefits; when maintenance costs outstrip returns, society becomes brittle and vulnerable to collapse. [1]
- **Technology is a progress trap.** Short-term beneficial innovations (irrigation, agriculture, industrial energy) create compounding unforeseen problems; technology, like social complexity, hits diminishing marginal returns. [1]
- **Energy sources stack like a Jenga tower.** Each new energy technology builds on continued extraction of the previous; renewable infrastructure cannot be built by intermittent renewable energy at scale — high heat and carbon atoms remain essential. [1]
- **War accelerates decline.** Sabotaging pipelines, sanctioning competitors, using war to damage energy infrastructure worsens deteriorating EROI by forcing longer routes, more fuel, smaller vessels; war is one of the greatest accelerators of decline. [1]
- **The maximum power principle prevents voluntary restraint.** Lotka's principle means humanity will keep squeezing maximum power from the planet; nations cannot voluntarily reduce energy use without risking malnutrition, discontent, and exploitation. [1]
- **The Western world order is on the periphery of collapse.** Pre-collapse indicators — breakdown of authority, revolts, declining revenues, military ineffectiveness, disaffected populace — are "clearly discernible and already visible on the peripheries." [1]
- **AI and space colonization accelerate rather than solve.** Automating and electrifying everything would take more energy than could be harvested from a dwindling resource base; these techno-savior proposals lock civilization more firmly into the progress trap. [1]
- **Contested — are the empirical foundations of Wright's progress-trap framework sound?** A Cicero review contests the megafauna, Easter Island, and methodology claims underpinning Wright's framework as ideology critique with patchy empirical support. See `## Contested:` in Analysis. [1][2]

# Analysis

## Tainter's Complexity Theory

Tainter's central insight is that societies are problem-solving organizations. As civilizations emerge and grow, they face constant challenges — resource distribution, territorial defense, administrative coordination — to which they respond by adding layers of complexity: new bureaucracies, specialized roles, hierarchical structures, information systems. The invention of writing, for instance, emerged as a solution to the problem of tracking debts and obligations, creating the social role of scribe. Each layer of complexity, however, requires a continuous and escalating input of resources and energy [1].

Because societies naturally solve their easiest problems with the largest potential benefit first, the marginal return on complexity declines. The last problems solved are the hardest and most expensive, yielding the smallest benefits. When the cost of maintaining sociopolitical structures exceeds the returns they provide, the system becomes brittle. Tainter identifies pre-collapse indicators: breakdown of authority and central control, revolts and provincial breakaways signaling weakening of the center, declining government revenues, increasingly successful foreign challengers, military ineffectiveness, and a disaffected populace as the hierarchy seeks to mobilize resources [1].

The [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html)-led world order, according to this framework, exhibits these symptoms. After rapid growth following WWII, the first major blow was the 1970 peak in US conventional oil production, followed by the end of the gold standard and the Vietnam War. The 1980s brought secular stagnation, briefly relieved by the Soviet collapse. Then came September 11, the war on terror, the 2008 financial crisis — a trajectory of decline that is "clearly discernible and already visible on the peripheries." The process is non-linear: there are ups and downs, periods of calm and crisis, but the trend is unmistakable [1]. This connects to the [Global System Rupture]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-rupture.html) theme.

## Wright's Progress Traps

Wright's concept extends Tainter's complexity theory to the technological domain. Progress traps have existed since the Stone Age: hunters who learned to drive whole herds over cliffs ate well for a season but wiped out their local food source. Ancient Sumerians built irrigation canals that boosted agriculture but deposited salts on the surface, eventually rendering the land barren. Forest clearing for farming exposed topsoil to erosion and floods. In each case, the initial triumph of an innovation dramatically improved survival or comfort, but also made society dependent on the new technology — without which increased population levels or social stability could no longer be maintained [1].

The modern analogue is industrial agriculture, which depletes soil nutrients while poisoning water with chemical fertilizers and pesticides. The hidden costs of environmental degradation and resource depletion accumulate in the background, requiring ever newer methods to counteract growing side effects. Technology, like social complexity, hits diminishing marginal returns: more effort is required at each step merely to maintain the system. When maintenance costs exceed returns, the technological base becomes brittle — and an involuntary loss of complexity can originate in technological failure as readily as in social disintegration [1].

Taking the concept further, individual technologies are never created in isolation but build on each other. There is no such thing as an "energy transition": humanity never stopped burning wood when it found coal, and never stopped using coal when it found oil. Coal remains essential for steel and cement production, for 24/7 power in smelting aluminum and silicon, and for countless chemical processes. The progress trap does not eliminate old technologies — it stacks new ones on top, amplifying vulnerability [1].

## Lotka's Maximum Power Principle

Lotka's principle (1925) states that systems surviving in competition are those that develop more power inflow and use it best to meet survival needs. This applies not only to biological systems but to human polities. "Industrialize or be colonized" captures the logic: nations cannot voluntarily reduce energy use without risking malnutrition, domestic discontent, and exploitation by more powerful neighbors [1].

The principle creates an inescapable double bind. On one hand, maximizing power inflow accelerates resource depletion and ecological destruction, driving civilization toward the thermodynamic ceiling. On the other hand, voluntary restraint is impossible because any nation that reduces energy consumption becomes vulnerable to those that do not. The result is that humanity as a whole will keep squeezing maximum power from the planet, reined in only by resource depletion, climate destabilization, global war — or most likely, the combination of all three [1].

This framework explains why proposals for managed degrowth or voluntary energy reduction are politically non-viable at the state level. The [Great Power Competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html) between the US, [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html), and Russia is not a policy choice but a thermodynamic imperative: each state must maximize power or lose position. See [Nate Hagens]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/nate-hagens.html) for related analysis of energy-blindness in economic theory.

## The Jenga Tower of Energy Technologies

The energy infrastructure of industrial civilization operates as a stacked Jenga tower. Wind turbines and pump-jacks are "energy extraction machines" built on mountains of coal, which is mined and transported by diesel engines made of steel. Solar panels, batteries, and wind turbines require high heat and carbon atoms for material transformations — intermittent and diffuse renewable energy cannot build the necessary equipment at scale. Instead of replacing prior energy sources, each new technology stacks on top of continued extraction of the previous ones [1].

Should coal production collapse worldwide, it would threaten not only coal-fired power plants but the entire energy technology stack: oil drilling infrastructure, wind turbine foundations and towers, and the steel and cement that underpin them all. The tower's integrity depends on the base layers remaining intact — yet the base layers are precisely the ones being depleted fastest [1].

The predicament deepens because the energy cost of energy is rising. Rich mineral deposits are being exhausted, replaced by unconventional ones requiring more energy to extract. Copper ore grades have declined 40% since 1991. The system is inexorably approaching a state where energy "in" equals energy "out" for the entire production system — not for one source that could be counteracted by another, but for the global economy as a whole. Past that point, the maintenance cost of infrastructure, cities, factories, and transportation systems continues to rise even as material and energy inputs shrink, triggering uncontrolled simplification until a new balance emerges [1]. See [Energy and Resources]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/energy-and-resources.html).

## War as Accelerator

War destroys the material-energy pipeline faster and more thoroughly than any natural disaster. By sabotaging pipelines, sanctioning competitors, or using military force to damage adversary energy infrastructure, competing nations worsen an already deteriorating EROI (energy return on investment). The mechanisms are multiple: longer shipping routes forced by chokepoint closures, more fuel consumed in transit, smaller vessels used for trans-shipments, shadow fleets and ship-to-ship transfers that waste time and energy [1].

The shift from pipeline gas to LNG illustrates the thermodynamic cost of geopolitical fragmentation: super-chilling and re-gasification add processing steps that consume additional energy at each stage. Infrastructure destruction from wars forces workarounds — less efficient diesel generators replacing highly efficient gas turbines hit by missiles, fuel imported from much further away. The net result is a massive increase in complexity consuming and destroying even more of the precious net energy societies produce [1].

The [US-Iran War 2026]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) and the [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html) exemplify this dynamic. Europe's sanctioning of its largest fossil fuel supplier, combined with the energy shock from Middle East conflict, has made the continent "an utterly import dependent region at the mercy of more powerful nations" — a case study in how war accelerates the complexity-cost spiral. Europe's energy system, built in the 1960s and 1970s, was not designed for either the physical climate (40°C+ heatwaves reducing refinery cooling efficiency) or the political climate (sanctions and supply disruptions) it now faces [1].

## The Reciprocity Alternative

The Honest Sorcerer proposes that escaping the repeating pattern of complexity collapse and progress traps requires more than a new technology or social hierarchy — it requires a paradigm built around robustness, reciprocity, and regeneration. Giving back as much as possible to the living world on which survival depends, so that it can sustain future generations. Restoring biodiversity, implementing agro-ecology, building low-tech but robust machines from the scraps of industrial civilization, and cooperating with neighbors [1].

This is not a guarantee of success but an argument that doing more of the same guarantees failure. Extracting one-time mineral reserves, destroying soil with industrial agriculture, and polluting atmosphere and rivers cannot continue indefinitely. Only reciprocity — giving back to the systems that sustain civilization — offers a path through. The proposal stands in deliberate contrast to techno-savior narratives (AI, fusion, space colonization) that increase complexity, energy, and material use, locking civilization more firmly into the progress trap [1].

## The Foothills of the Himalayas: Currie's Trajectory Marker

[Jeff Currie]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/jeff-currie.html) provides a trajectory marker for the civilizational position: "we're only in the foothills of the Himalayas at this point." The metaphor captures the relationship between current commodity-market stress and the structural challenges ahead — the crisis visible in mid-2026 is the easy part. The $800 billion annual AI buildout, with more than 50% of spending going directly into commodities, creates the biggest commodity demand shock in history while investment in commodity supply remains suppressed. The implication is that the progress-trap dynamic — technological complexity demanding more energy and materials at exactly the moment when energy surpluses are contracting — is accelerating rather than stabilizing [3].

[Nate Hagens]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/nate-hagens.html) predicts a societal bifurcation between the AI-literate and the AI-abhorrent — a cultural split that mirrors the economic K-shape already visible in wealth distribution. The bifurcation extends the progress-trap framework: the technology that accelerates complexity (and thus the trap) simultaneously fragments the society that must cope with the consequences. Those who can navigate AI tools gain adaptive capacity; those who cannot face diminishing economic and social agency. The bifurcation is itself a simplification mechanism — a loss of societal coherence that Tainter's complexity theory predicts as a pre-collapse indicator [3].

## The Economic Superorganism and Metabolic Framework

Hagens frames society as a self-organizing superorganism with a metabolism: small businesses, corporations, and nation-states self-organize to maximize profits, and those profits are ultimately based on energy and materials, not money. When the [Strait of Hormuz]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html) is disrupted, the superorganism "splinters" — the energy-flow disruption is not a financial event but a metabolic disorder. The framework integrates Tainter's complexity theory with thermodynamics: the superorganism's metabolism requires energy gradients, and as the highest-quality gradients (fossil fuels) deplete, the organism must either simplify (involuntary degrowth) or consume more energy acquiring energy (the Mordor Economy). Oil, diesel, and jet fuel are the "hemoglobin" of the system — the carriers that make all other economic activity possible. The financial sector's failure to understand this biophysical basis produces policy that amounts to "bombing without any coherent plan" — interventions that address financial symptoms while ignoring the physical-energy substrate [3]. ## Contested: Are the empirical foundations of Wright's progress-trap framework sound?

[The Honest Sorcerer]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/the-honest-sorcerer.html)'s compilation presents Wright's progress traps as empirically grounded and civilization-threatening: Stone Age hunters wiped out megafauna, Sumerian irrigation salinized the soil, Easter Island's deforestation destroyed shipbuilding — each a documented case of innovation creating dependency and overshoot. The framework is presented as archaeological narrative with direct modern analogues. [1] A Cicero review of Wright's *A Short History of Progress* contests all three exemplar cases. The "first progress trap" — Stone Age hunters versus megafauna — is empirically contestable because many large species were displaced by competition with pastoralists and farmers, not by hunter-cultures. The Easter Island example is circular: the trap had already snapped shut when humans settled an island so remote that long-distance trade was practically impossible — settlement itself was the proximate cause, not deforestation. Wright's method is ideology critique, genealogically linked to Benjamin's Angel of History and Adorno/Horkheimer's *Dialectic of Enlightenment*, empirically only patchily supported — not archaeology. His policy prescriptions — consume less, redistribute more — end in "faith" ("hilft wieder einmal allein der Glaube"), not logically or economically derivable conclusions. [2]

The split is methodological — Wright reads archaeological narrative as evidence for a civilizational pattern; the reviewer applies source-critical methodology to Wright's own claims, interrogating whether the cases support the framework they are enlisted to serve. Neither disputes the other's facts; they disagree on whether the facts constitute the pattern Wright claims.

**Tiebreaker:** No single observable can settle this — the contestation turns on cumulative interpretation of archaeological evidence (megafauna extinction causes, Easter Island settlement chronology) that does not resolve to a near-term falsifiable test. The disagreement persists as long as the underlying archaeology remains interpretively open.
**Status:** open fork.
