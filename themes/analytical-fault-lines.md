# Background

The OKF draws on analysts who apply different, often incompatible, methodologies to the
same events. When two named sources reach opposite conclusions from the same facts, the
disagreement is usually not sloppiness on either side — it traces back to one of a small
number of recurring splits in how they model the world. Naming the split is more useful
than re-litigating each instance of it, because the same fault line predicts where the
next disagreement will surface before it does.

Do not simply delete one side of a conflict to make prose cleaner. The disagreement is
signal: it tells a reader which parts of the corpus are settled and which are live bets.

# Current Situation

Four fault lines account for most of the standing `## Contested:` blocks (see rules.md
§8.4) across the bundle. Each entry below names the split, the analysts on each side, and
where in the bundle it currently produces a live disagreement.

# Key Dynamics

- **Agency vs structure.** O'Brien reads great-power trajectories as leader choices; GPF
  (Friedman) and Beckley read them as structural patterns that outlast any one leader. [1][2][3]
- **Unitary vs factional actor modeling.** Pape and Friedman model Iran as a coherent
  strategic actor; Fowler models it as factionally incoherent, with no confirmed Supreme
  Leader in effective control. [4][5][6]
- **Alliance-as-real vs alliance-as-branding.** Tchakarova's DragonBear/CRINK framing
  treats China-Russia-Iran-North Korea coordination as a deepening strategic partnership;
  Ueki's bilateral-only reading treats it as four separate relationships with no joint
  military structure. [7][8]
- **Single-publication-as-single-voice.** Outlets with multiple bylines (The Cipher
  Brief, GPF) get cited as if they hold one institutional position; two Cipher Brief
  pieces on Russian regime durability reach opposite conclusions under different bylines. [9][10]
- **Contested — is US decline structural or cyclical?** O'Brien reads compounding
  decline; Beckley reads structural durability. See `## Contested:` in
  [Great Power Competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html). [1][3]
- **Contested — are the Houthis Iran's proxy or an independent actor?** Pape reads Houthi
  strikes as Iran's deliberate leverage; Bokhari/GPF reads Houthi restraint as evidence of
  a fraying, non-compelled proxy network. See `## Contested:` in
  [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html). [11][12]

# Analysis

## Agency vs structure

O'Brien's weekly updates treat wars as choices made by specific leaders at specific
moments — Putin's decision to invade Ukraine, Trump's decision to strike Iran — that no
structural model predicted. [1] GPF's Friedman and Beckley invert the emphasis: Friedman's
"Thinking About Iran's Strategy" reads US Iran-war strategy as following a structural
war-termination pattern comparable to Vietnam, Iraq, and Afghanistan, patient rather than
personality-driven; [2] Beckley's "asymmetric spheres of influence" framework argues
declinist readings of specific US setbacks (the Iran war) miss the structural durability of
US hegemony rooted in geography and hemispheric dominance. [3]

`actors/authors/geopolitical-futures.md` already names this split in prose — "This creates
a complementary tension: GPF explains why structural pressures exist, while O'Brien
explains why the timing and direction of action depend on the specific leader in power" —
but that characterization is asserted, not tested against a case where the two frameworks
predict different near-term US behavior. The `## Contested:` block in
[Great Power Competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html) is the
first attempt at that test, and its `**Status:**` line should be read as the current
verdict on whether "complementary" holds.

## Unitary vs factional actor modeling

Pape's three-phase Survival→Ambition→Revenge model and Friedman's structural
war-termination reading both treat Iran as a coherent strategic actor executing a
deliberate plan. [4][2] Fowler's Cipher Brief piece argues the opposite: Tehran's
obstinacy may reflect internal factional dysfunction rather than confidence, including an
unconfirmed "phantom" Supreme Leader and competing factions maneuvering for position. [6]
This is close to a first-order question about whether Iran is a rational unitary actor for
modeling purposes at all — a regime can be simultaneously factionalized and still land on
coherent-looking policy through internal bargaining, which is why the corresponding
`## Contested:` block in [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html) states the
distinction explicitly rather than picking a side.

## Alliance-as-real vs alliance-as-branding

Tchakarova's DragonBear axis treats China-Russia coordination as a deepening, mutually
reinforcing strategic partnership — China as economic engine, Russia as military
disruptor, generating simultaneity across theaters by design. [7] Ueki's rebuttal, cited in
[China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html), argues CRINK cooperation is
strictly bilateral: no CRINK joint military exercise has occurred, China rejected a
proposed China-Russia-North Korea trilateral exercise, and China's economic ties to the US
vastly outweigh its ties to Russia, Iran, or North Korea combined. [8] This is the
best-evidenced fault line in the bundle — both analysts make falsifiable claims about an
observable phenomenon (joint exercises, coordinated votes, sanctions-evasion logistics),
which is why it is the one most likely to move from "open fork" to "resolved" as new
evidence arrives. Track it at the `## Contested:` block in
[Great Power Competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html).

## Single-publication-as-single-voice

The Cipher Brief published "The West Needs to Prepare for a Russian Defeat in Ukraine" —
treating Russian regime collapse as a scenario that has moved "from fringe to serious,"
urging Nunn-Lugar-style contingency planning — [9] and separately "The Continued Myth of
Russia's Imminent Collapse," arguing that predictions of Putin's imminent fall have
consistently failed and the security state remains resilient. [10] Read as one editorial
voice, this is a self-contradiction; read as two individual analysts publishing under one
outlet, it is an ordinary disagreement that happens to share a byline page. Per rules.md
§8.5, `actors/authors/the-cipher-brief.md` should attribute each piece to its individual
author where the byline is known rather than let "The Cipher Brief" stand in for a single
analytical position — the pattern likely recurs at other multi-author outlets (GPF, ISW,
RUSI, CSIS) and is worth auditing wherever an outlet file currently flattens contributors
into one voice.
