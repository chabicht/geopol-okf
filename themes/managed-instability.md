# Background

Managed instability describes a conflict condition in which fighting and bargaining persist together because key actors find continued leverage preferable to an available settlement. It builds on coercion, protracted-conflict, and gray-zone frameworks, but emphasizes the political use of a military stalemate rather than stalemate alone. [1]

# Current Situation

Alex Kolbin argues that the [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html) and the [US-Iran War of 2026]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) have each moved away from expectations of a quick political result. In both, ceasefires and narrow arrangements coexist with pressure through transit, shipping, sanctions, energy infrastructure, outside support, and deep strikes. [1]

# Key Dynamics

- **Stalemate becomes politically usable.** Military deadlock can be turned into leverage through coercive tools outside the battlefield rather than producing a freeze or settlement. [1]
- **Partial bargains can sustain conflict.** Agreements on transit, prisoners, shipping, or infrastructure can lower immediate costs without addressing the issues that prevent a wider settlement. [1]
- **Outside support links theaters.** Russian assistance to Iran, Ukrainian anti-drone expertise shared with Gulf states, and competing demand for Western interceptors create a two-war proxy context without merging the wars. [1]
- **Interdependence limits decisive outcomes.** Fragmented politics alongside continuing economic links creates additional tools for pressure while nuclear escalation limits constrain outright victory. [1]
- **Settlement requires changing incentives.** External supporters would need to condition resources on reciprocal, monitored de-escalation steps so that instability costs more than a viable agreement. [1]

# Analysis

Kolbin's framework is an assessment rather than a prediction that either war cannot end. Its diagnostic indicators are stalled battlefield gains, ceasefires that do not begin a peace process, fragmented negotiations, and geographic expansion through outside support and alternative supply chains.
