# Background

The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) process and the annual Conference of the Parties (COP) system constitute the universal framework for international climate cooperation. Growing geopolitical tensions and the shift toward a multipolar order are challenging this framework from multiple directions — ideologization of climate policy, rising adaptation and transformation costs, and great-power competition that weakens willingness for joint climate efforts.

The institutional context is under severe strain. Drastic payment cuts to the UN — especially by the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) — must be absorbed by sweeping budget savings, limiting the institutional capacity that underpins climate negotiations. Political differences over Middle East and [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html) conflicts burden cooperation among member states. The search for a successor to UN Secretary-General António Guterres adds an additional layer of institutional uncertainty.

The foresight methodology employed by [SWP]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/swp.html) researchers — conducted at the ACUNS conference "Multilateralism under Challenge and the Future of the Pact" — explores conceivable developments without claiming prediction. The approach emphasizes exploration and anticipation: identifying key drivers that could push in different directions, and assessing which measures might prevent undesirable trajectories or promote desirable ones.

# Key Dynamics

- **Multipolar pressure is fragmenting climate cooperation.** Ideologization, rising adaptation costs, and great-power competition weaken the willingness for joint climate-policy efforts, threatening the established multilateral framework. [1]
- **Fragmentation can also mean decentralization.** Climate policy conceived at local and regional levels may be more connectable to diverse contexts, enabling broader actor inclusion and higher legitimacy than top-down approaches. [1]
- **A universal framework remains necessary.** The UN General Assembly was identified as the platform where plurilateral coalitions can discuss progress with all states; the UNFCCC process remains the appropriate universal framework for climate cooperation. [1]
- **US financial retrenchment is the binding constraint.** Drastic payment cuts — especially by the United States — must be absorbed by sweeping UN budget savings, limiting institutional capacity at the exact moment climate cooperation is most urgent. [1]
- **Inequality deepens under fragmentation.** Less capable or less willing states bear disproportionate burdens from climate policy fragmentation, deepening global inequalities and undermining the legitimacy of the entire system. [1]

# Analysis

## The Foresight Exercise

The SWP analysis (Beisheim, Böttcher, and Brozus) emerged from a foresight exercise conducted at the Academic Council on the United Nations System (ACUNS) conference on multilateralism. The methodology explicitly rejects prediction in favor of exploration: identifying drivers that could shape different future trajectories, and evaluating policy measures that could steer toward desirable outcomes [1].

Climate policy was selected as the case study because it uniquely connects local, national, and international action levels with social, technological, ecological, economic, legal, and political dimensions. The interdisciplinary and international setting of the ACUNS conference provided a suitable framework for examining how an increasingly multipolar world order affects climate cooperation [1].

Three key challenges were derived from the exercise. First, ideologization and politicization combined with rising adaptation and transformation costs could progressively weaken willingness for joint climate efforts. Second, this could lead to greater fragmentation of international climate policy. Third, fragmentation would disproportionately burden less capable or less willing states, deepening global inequalities — a pattern that echoes broader [Global System Rupture]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-rupture.html) dynamics [1].

## Fragmentation as Opportunity

The exercise's most provocative finding reframes fragmentation not solely as threat but as potential decentralization. Climate policy conceived at local and regional levels may be more "connectable" — adaptable to diverse contexts, enabling inclusion of additional actors, and thereby increasing legitimacy and acceptance of political measures. This interpretation challenges the assumption that universal top-down frameworks are always optimal [1].

However, participants emphasized that decentralization does not eliminate the need for a universal framework. The UN remains the appropriate venue, with the UN General Assembly identified as a multilateral platform where plurilateral coalitions and initiatives can converge to discuss progress and obstacles with all member states. The model is not fragmentation versus universalism but fragmentation within universalism — coalitions of the willing operating within a UN-backed framework that maintains inclusivity [1].

This framing connects to the [Global System Bifurcation]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-bifurcation.html) theme: the question is whether institutional fragmentation under multipolar pressure becomes a productive form of decentralization or a destructive dissolution of collective capacity. The climate domain may be a test case, as it is one of the few areas where the costs of fragmentation are universally shared.

## US Retrenchment and Institutional Capacity

The financial dimension is the most immediate constraint. The United States has drastically cut its payments to the UN, forcing sweeping budget savings across the institution. This retrenchment limits the Secretariat's capacity to support climate negotiations, maintain technical infrastructure, and facilitate the COP process — at the precise moment when rising adaptation costs and geopolitical polarization demand more institutional capacity, not less [1].

The combination of financial squeeze and political polarization creates a vicious circle: reduced institutional capacity makes cooperation harder, which breeds frustration, which further reduces political support for funding. The Guterres succession adds uncertainty: the next Secretary-General's priorities and competencies will shape whether the UN can maintain its role as the central platform for climate diplomacy or cedes ground to plurilateral and regional forums [1].

This dynamic is a specific instance of the broader [Great Power Competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html) pattern: the US withdrawal from multilateral funding creates space for other powers — particularly [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) — to expand their influence within UN institutions. The climate domain, which requires both universal participation and substantial financial commitment, is acutely vulnerable to this shift.
