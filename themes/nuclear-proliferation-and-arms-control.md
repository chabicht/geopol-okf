# Background

The post-Cold War nuclear arms control architecture rests on two pillars: the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT) and the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (CTBT). The CTBT, opened for signature in 1996, has been signed by 187 states and ratified by 178, but has not formally entered into force — ratification by 44 designated nuclear-capable states is required, and only 35 have done so. The [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) signed but saw its Senate reject ratification in 1999; [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html), [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html), Israel, and Egypt have likewise signed without ratifying. Despite this, a de facto testing moratorium has held since 1996 under [Vienna Convention Article 18](https://theloop.ecpr.eu/us-accusations-of-chinese-nuclear-testing-reshape-arms-control/), which obliges signatories not to undermine a treaty's object and purpose.

That moratorium is now eroding from multiple directions. [Russia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/russia.html) revoked its CTBT ratification in 2023 in the context of the [Russia-Ukraine War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/russia-ukraine-war.html). The US has accused China of conducting a secret underground nuclear test at Lop Nur in June 2020. North Korea has conducted multiple tests outside the framework entirely. US funding for the CTBTO's International Monitoring System remains pending for 2026, undermining verification capacity at the precise moment it is most needed.

In parallel, the civilian nuclear cooperation "gold standard" established by the [UAE]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/uae.html) agreement of 2009 — which required a renunciation of domestic enrichment and adherence to the Additional Protocol — is being superseded. The new US-Saudi nuclear agreement permits [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html) to enrich uranium domestically with weaker inspections than the UAE received, lowering the non-proliferation bar and creating signal effects across the Middle East and beyond.

# Key Dynamics

- **The testing norm is eroding from multiple directions.** China's alleged 2020 test, Russia's CTBT de-ratification, and US funding gaps each weaken the de facto moratorium; the combination creates cascade risk as each violation lowers the threshold for the next. [1][2]
- **China's arsenal expansion is the structural driver.** Growth from 300 to 600 warheads, projected 1,500 by 2035, with alleged testing for tactical warhead designs relevant to a Taiwan contingency — the build-out is the most significant nuclear development since the Cold War's end. [1]
- **The US-Saudi nuclear agreement lowers the non-proliferation bar.** Domestic enrichment with weaker inspections than the UAE 2009 "gold standard" sets a precedent that the UAE, [Turkey]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/turkey.html), and Iran will cite in future negotiations. [2]
- **A Middle East proliferation cascade is now conceivable.** [Guido Steinberg]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/guido-steinberg.html) projects 3–5 nuclear powers in the region as Iran continues its weapons program (pursued since 1987), Saudi Arabia gains enrichment capability, and Turkey considers its own program. [2]
- **US test resumption would benefit adversaries most.** [Philipp Rombach]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/philipp-rombach.html) argues China and Russia gain more from a resumed testing regime than the US, which has superior science-based stockpile stewardship (DARHT, NIF, $10–16B/yr) and the most extensive historical testing record. [1]
- **Latent proliferators are watching.** Poland, South Korea, and Saudi Arabia face new incentives as norms erode; the cascade could extend beyond the Middle East to states that previously accepted the NPT bargain without question. [1]

# Analysis

## The CTBT and the Testing Moratorium

The CTBT emerged from a incremental process of nuclear testing restrictions: the 1963 Limited Test Ban Treaty (atmosphere, space, underwater), the 1974 Threshold Test Ban Treaty (150 kt ceiling for underground tests), and finally the 1996 comprehensive ban. The US adopted a unilateral testing moratorium in 1992 that became permanent in 1996 under President Clinton, who waived the 15 additional tests Congress had authorized as a goodwill gesture. The moratorium contains a clause permitting resumption if another state tests — a provision now invoked by advocates of renewed testing [1].

The treaty's verification regime relies on the International Monitoring System (IMS), a global network of seismic, hydroacoustic, infrasound, and radionuclide stations. The IMS is designed to unambiguously detect underground tests exceeding 1 kiloton of TNT, though its true sensitivity reaches approximately 500 tons. Verification is further complicated by seismic decoupling: an explosion in an underground cavern can reduce the measurable seismic signal by a factor of 20 to 40, meaning a 200–400 ton actual yield might appear as only 10 tons — precisely the pattern observed in the alleged Chinese test [1].

The CTBT's legal force is circumvented by the fact that it has never entered into formal force. Signatories observe it under Vienna Convention Article 18, but the absence of ratification by key states — the US, China, Iran, Israel, Egypt — means there is no mechanism for on-site inspections or legally binding enforcement. Russia's 2023 de-ratification further weakened the regime, placing Moscow on the same legal footing as Washington: signed but not ratified [1].

## China's Alleged Test and Arsenal Trajectory

In February 2026, the US presented new evidence at the United Nations regarding an underground nuclear weapons test allegedly conducted by China in June 2020 at the Lop Nur test site in Xinjiang. A CTBTO seismic monitoring station in Makanchi, [Kazakhstan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/kazakhstan.html), recorded a 2.75-magnitude seismic event on 22 June 2020 — two distinct explosions 12 seconds apart, a signature consistent with a decoupled nuclear test followed by cavern collapse. The US State Department estimates a coupled yield of 10 tons of TNT; accounting for seismic decoupling, the actual yield may have been 200–400 tons — far exceeding what conventional or subcritical tests would produce [1].

Beijing has categorically rejected the allegations but has offered no formal counter-explanations or transparency measures. Satellite imagery analysis between 2020 and 2024 documents extensive construction at Lop Nur: road expansion, electrical infrastructure, high-explosive storage facilities, tunnel systems, vertical boreholes, and diagnostic cabling — all consistent with an active and ongoing testing program rather than an isolated incident [1].

China's motivation is structural. Having conducted only 45 nuclear tests (compared to 1,054 by the US and 715 by the Soviet Union), China lacks confidence in its arsenal's safety, security, and reliability. The June 2020 event was likely a weapons physics and warhead design test aimed at developing sub-kiloton tactical warheads — providing additional nuclear response options in a [Taiwan Standoff]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/taiwan-standoff.html) scenario. This aligns with China's arsenal expansion from 300 to 600 warheads, projected to reach 1,500 by 2035, and its broader objective of establishing itself as a third nuclear superpower [1]. See also the [JL-3 SLBM Test]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/jl-3-slbm-test-2026-07-06.html) and [China's Alleged Nuclear Test]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/china-alleged-june-2020-underground-nuclear-test.html).

## The US-Saudi Agreement and the Gold-Standard Erosion

The US-Saudi civilian nuclear agreement, concluded during the [US-Iran War 2026]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html), permits Saudi Arabia to enrich uranium domestically — a right the UAE explicitly renounced in its 2009 agreement with the United States. The 2009 UAE deal was considered the "gold standard" for civilian nuclear cooperation: no domestic enrichment plus adherence to the NPT Additional Protocol, which mandates intrusive inspections. The Saudi agreement weakens both conditions [2].

[Guido Steinberg]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/guido-steinberg.html) identifies two critical problems. First, Saudi Arabia's Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman stated in 2018 that his country would obtain a nuclear bomb if Iran developed one — a position that likely still reflects Saudi thinking despite MBS's evolution as a politician. Domestic enrichment capability brings Saudi Arabia closer to a weapons option, even if the current program is civilian [2]. See the [US-Saudi Nuclear Agreement]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/saudi-arabia-us-civilian-nuclear-agreement-2026-07-24.html).

Second, the signal effect is destabilizing. The UAE will likely seek to retroactively renegotiate its terms to match Saudi Arabia's. Turkey, Riyadh's rival for regional leadership, may be tempted to launch its own nuclear program. Iran will cite the Saudi conditions in future negotiations, insisting on its own enrichment rights with even greater insistence. The agreement thus erodes the non-proliferation regime not only through its direct terms but through the cascade of demands it triggers [2].

## Proliferation Cascade Dynamics

The combination of eroding testing norms and weakened civilian nuclear conditions creates a proliferation cascade that extends beyond any single state. Steinberg projects a Middle East with three, four, or five nuclear powers — a scenario made substantially more probable by the US-Saudi agreement and Iran's continued pursuit of a weapons capability, which Tehran has maintained since 1987. Iran's new, heavily military leadership has every incentive to develop nuclear weapons: the [Hormuz Toll System]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/hormuz-toll-system.html) and Hormuz Strait leverage provide conventional deterrent tools, but nuclear weapons would offer the ultimate guarantee against the US and [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html) [2].

Beyond the Middle East, [Philipp Rombach]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/philipp-rombach.html) identifies Poland, South Korea, and Saudi Arabia as states increasingly discussed as potential nuclear proliferators. The erosion of the testing norm provides latent proliferators with new incentives: if the established nuclear powers resume testing, the technical and political barriers to proliferation diminish. Even [Germany]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/germany.html) has been mentioned in international discourse, though proliferation there faces significant technical, legal, and political obstacles [1].

The cascade dynamic is self-reinforcing. Each new nuclear state increases the incentive for its regional rivals to follow suit. Each erosion of the testing norm reduces the stigma and technical barriers. Each weakening of civilian nuclear conditions expands the pathway from civilian to military capability. The [SWP]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/swp.html) analysis suggests that Germany and Europe, as non-nuclear-weapon states, have limited room for manoeuvre — but can promote transparency, fund the CTBTO and IAEA, and support Washington's calls for new strategic arms control agreements [1].

This theme intersects with [Great Power Competition]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/great-power-competition.html) and [Global System Bifurcation]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-bifurcation.html): the nuclear domain is one of the last areas where great-power coordination remains formally possible, but also one where the consequences of fragmentation are most catastrophic.
