# Background

Democracy versus authoritarianism serves as a framing of global competition, but the framing conceals internal tensions within democratic societies. Communism's historical failures — the Soviet Union, Cuba, Mao's China — provide the empirical record against which authoritarian ideologies are measured. But the deeper question is not comparative: it is whether democratic institutions have remained faithful to the values that make them legitimate.

Conservative voters' relationship with democratic institutions is central to this question. The "Save democracy" messaging that dominates liberal discourse does not resonate with conservative [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) voters — not because they oppose democracy but because they perceive the institutional order as having drifted from the founding values (faith, family, freedom, place) that confer legitimacy.

# Current Situation

The ideological landscape is defined by a legitimacy crisis, oligarchic capture of governance, and the global erosion of democratic norms. Conservative voters reject the liberal "save democracy" framing not because they oppose democracy but because they perceive the institutional order as having drifted from the founding values — faith, family, freedom, place — that confer legitimacy. The core question is not whether America should be a democracy but whether American democracy has remained faithful to what makes it legitimate [1]. [Anne Applebaum]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/anne-applebaum.html) identifies the cutting edge of this crisis as a clash between two conceptions of nationhood: the civic project of founding abstractions versus "blood and soil" nationalism, crystallized in Vance's 2024 RNC speech dismissing founding abstractions in favor of tombstones, homeland, and ancestral belonging [12].

The empirical record of communism's failure — authoritarian governance, economic collapse, mass death — provides the comparative backdrop against which all ideological claims are measured [2][7]. [Latin America]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/latin-america.html)'s rightward wave is reshaping the hemisphere, driven by attitudes on crime and migration that moved rightward independently of US political dynamics [4]. Meanwhile, [Phillips O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) argues, using Gallup polling data, that America has balkanized into two partisan camps where loyalty to party now determines views on war and America's global role, eroding the shared national identity that once transcended partisanship [14].

# Key Dynamics

The central dynamic is the gap between institutional authority and the values that originally legitimized it — a structural drift, not an incidental one, that makes the "save democracy" appeal unresponsive to the conservative critique [1]. This legitimacy erosion is compounded by oligarchic capture: the political power of the hyper-wealthy tilts policy in their favor, and this tilt reinforces their wealth, creating a self-reinforcing spiral in which corporate taxes fall despite overwhelming public opposition [17][25]. [Latin America]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/latin-america.html)'s rightward realignment carries indigenous roots but also instability — disputed elections, institutional weakness, and leftist strongholds in Mexico and Brazil — meaning the conversion of ideological shift into security bloc architecture depends on whether right-leaning governments can consolidate institutional control [4].

# Analysis

## The Democratic Legitimacy Crisis

Osborn and Warren argue that conservative voters do not see "saving democracy" as relevant to their political priorities because they perceive institutions as having drifted from the founding values that make democracy legitimate. The framing of democratic defense as institutional preservation misses the substance of the conservative critique — which is not anti-democratic but anti-institutional in the specific sense that the institutions no longer embody the values they claim to represent [1]. The central question, as they frame it, is not "Should America be a democracy?" but "Has American democracy remained faithful to what makes it legitimate?" The founding values — faith, family, freedom, place — are the basis on which conservative voters assess institutional legitimacy. When institutions are perceived as having forsaken these values, the appeal to "save" them is heard as a defense of the very drift that delegitimized them [1].

[Anne Applebaum]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/anne-applebaum.html) frames the core ideological division in contemporary America as a clash between two conceptions of nationhood that gives the legitimacy crisis its cutting edge. [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) and Vance represent "blood and soil" nationalism — Vance's 2024 RNC speech explicitly dismissed American founding abstractions in favor of tombstones, homeland, and ancestral belonging. Applebaum counters that the American nation is the community created by those abstractions — uniting people with radically different origins in a shared civic project. The destruction of the 250th Fourth of July celebration — usurped by a privately funded Trump event featuring his own political speech — symbolizes the displacement of the founding ideology by the clan project [12]. The legitimacy crisis is thus not a disagreement about policy but a disagreement about what the nation *is*: whether it is a civic project held together by abstractions or a blood-and-soil community held together by ancestry. When the institutions designed to embody the civic project are captured by adherents of the clan project, the conservative voter's perception that institutions have drifted from founding values is not paranoia but accurate description.

[The Honest Sorcerer]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/the-honest-sorcerer.html) poses whether we have passed "peak rationality" — the limits of human rationality, the mechanistic worldview, and diminishing returns of scientific research. Drawing on Jean Gebser's theory of consciousness structures, the framework asks what awaits at the end of the Age of Reason. This is not anti-rational but meta-rational: the recognition that the rationalist project has internal limits that cannot be overcome by more rationality [10]. The legitimacy crisis has an epistemic dimension: a political culture that has lost confidence in its own founding abstractions and in the rationalist framework that once justified them cannot generate the shared premises needed for democratic deliberation. [Phillips O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) traces the consequence using Gallup polling data: America has balkanized into two partisan camps where loyalty to party now determines views on war and America's global role. Unlike during Vietnam or Kosovo, when partisanship was secondary to national identity, shared attachment to being American has eroded [14].

## Kleptocracy, Oligarchic Capture, and the Powermaxxing Spiral

[Anne Applebaum]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/anne-applebaum.html)'s Kleptocracy Tracker documents the systematic monetization of presidential power as the operational logic of [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html)'s governance, not a side-effect: the $400 million Qatari jet kept as Air Force One and afterward; the Kazakhstan tungsten mine deal with $1.6 billion in federal financing benefiting the Trump and Lutnick sons; over 3,600 stock trades by the president; the repeal of shell company transparency; the Live Nation antitrust settlement after a personal call to the CEO; a pardon lobbying industry run by former Trump officials; Trump Jr.'s Kalshi equity growing from $300K to millions [12]. The tracker's June-July 2026 update documents accelerating monetization: Trump diverted funds from 900+ national projects — including safety upgrades at national parks — to finance White House beautification, including $700,000 for a walkway. He purchased $1M-$5M in Axon stock two weeks before ICE solicited a $220M taser contract (Axon makes ~90% of US tasers). He made ~$1.2 billion from crypto companies in one year, including $500M from World Liberty Financial and $600M from his meme coin. 250 pardons were planned for America's 250th birthday, with white-collar defense attorneys reporting they can win pardons for clients for ~$2M. A $500M no-bid contract for a White House ballroom was awarded to Clark Construction, with the president directly involved in negotiations. Melania Trump earned $10M+ from an Amazon MGM Studios documentary. Trump made hundreds of stock purchases the day before pausing tariffs, sending the S&P 500 soaring ~10% — one of the largest single-day gains ever. Gulf entities paid $300M+ to Trump's businesses, including $263M from the sale of half his stake in World Liberty Financial to a UAE royal-backed firm. Trump made 21,000 securities trades in his first year, growing his investment account to $858M across ~1,600 companies (vs. Biden's 13 total trades during his entire presidency). Nearly 1 million meme coin buyers lost $3.8 billion while Trump walked away with $636 million. Palm Beach International Airport was renamed Trump International Airport — requiring Florida to pay royalties to the president's family for licensing his name [16].

[Paul Krugman]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/paul-krugman.html) provides the judicial infrastructure for this analysis: the [Slaughter SCOTUS ruling]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/slaughter-scotus-ruling.html) represents a judicial milestone in democratic backsliding — the Supreme Court overturning 90 years of precedent to grant Trump dictatorial power over regulatory agencies. The ruling is not about the presidency in general but about *this* president specifically: the Court is "utterly partisan," and Krugman expects it to strip discretionary power from any future Democratic president the moment one takes office. The core of Krugman's analysis is that the ruling is fundamentally about enabling corruption. The Federal Reserve carve-out exposes the Court's class politics — monetary policy independence is preserved not on constitutional grounds but because the Justices feared market reaction, leaving "the little people" exposed to the destruction of regulatory protections while large portfolios are shielded. The ruling eliminates government by professional civil servants in favor of "government by henchmen and lackeys" [5].

The oligarchy spiral is self-reinforcing. Krugman documents that corporate taxes fell from 35% in the 1960s to around 12% today, despite overwhelming and stable public opposition — Gallup polling over 20+ years shows a huge majority consistently saying corporations pay too little, while hardly anyone says they pay too much. Yet corporate taxes keep going down. The mechanisms are threefold: campaign finance increasingly dominated by billionaire money, accelerated by Citizens United; corruption shifting from implicit revolving-door incentives to open cash, crypto, and sweetheart contracts under Trump 2.0; and the shaping of the information environment — right-wing think tanks, subsidized "research," and captured media relentlessly push billionaire interests, shifting the Overton Window so that even centrists view billionaire-friendly policies as "sensible" while high corporate taxes are viewed as "radical." The 2017 Trump corporate tax cuts produced no surge in US investment, confirming that there is very little trickle-down from corporate tax cuts — the decline was effectively a massive indirect tax cut for wealthy stockholders, fueling the oligarchy spiral [17].

The wealth concentration driving this spiral is staggering. Krugman argues that the political backlash against billionaires — what Tyler Cowen dismisses as "billionaire derangement syndrome" — is belated rather than excessive, and that the real puzzle is why it did not happen sooner. The wealth of the Forbes 400 grew from $92 billion in 1982 to $6.6 trillion in 2025, dwarfing gains in income and wealth for the average American even after adjusting for inflation. The hyper-wealthy are not merely "landmaxxing" with giant estates — they are "powermaxxing," seriously undermining American democracy as well as lowering the living standards of ordinary Americans. A New York Fed report documents that the share of national income going to workers is at an 80-year low. As recently as the 2000s, the hyper-wealthy played little direct role in campaign finance, but the combination of soaring billionaire wealth and the Roberts Supreme Court's Citizens United decision opened the floodgates — billionaires accounted for almost 20% of campaign spending in 2024, and that figure surely understates their influence [25]. Krugman catalogs the specific mechanisms: Larry Ellison's family has taken control of CBS, rapidly corrupting it into a right-wing mouthpiece, and is trying to take over CNN too. Elon Musk appears personally committed to white supremacy and right-wing extremism. Peter Thiel, who bought JD Vance his Ohio Senate seat, has called for a return to monarchy and now rants about the antichrist. As [Henry Farrell](https://www.programmablemutter.com/p/the-political-economy-of-billionaire) argues, the right framing is not "billionaire derangement syndrome" but "deranged billionaire syndrome." Not all billionaires are deranged — some are public-spirited — but the Citizens United decision and the Trump administration's raw corruption opened the door for the numerous predatory billionaires to acquire more political power in order to further rig a system already tilted in their favor. Above all, billionaires want low taxes for themselves: because income from wealth is taxed at much lower rates than income from wages, the wealthiest 400 Americans pay an average tax rate of 24%, compared with 30% for the population at large and 45% for high-income Americans who derive income from earnings rather than asset ownership. Low taxes on the hyper-wealthy feed a downward spiral of oligarchy in which low taxes make it easier for huge fortunes to grow even larger, and the power of vast wealth keeps increasing, leading to even more favorable policies for the few. "Democracy is on the cliff-edge. Can we pull out of the oligarchic spiral soon enough to save it?" [25].

The powermaxxing extends to the systematic commandeering of media and political life. [Paul Krugman]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/paul-krugman.html) argues that a handful of billionaires are systematically commandeering US media and political life, not through hidden conspiracy but through open seizure. At the level of media acquisition, the Ellison family acquired CBS (hiring Bari Weiss to reshape it) and is attempting a Warner/CNN takeover; [Musk]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) transformed X into a right-wing-aligned platform by tilting its algorithm; Zuckerberg is tilting Facebook; and Bezos shifted the *Washington Post*'s editorial policy hard right while eviscerating its news division. The objective is influence, not profit. At the level of campaign finance, a *New York Times* analysis found that 300 billionaires and their families supplied roughly 20% of all 2024 contributions — and their giving is more targeted and strategic than that of average donors. At the level of direct corruption, the purchase of politicians is now open rather than discreet, with billions in cash and crypto directed at the president and his family. The information space is further shaped through the promotion of zombie ideas (the claim that tax cuts pay for themselves) and climate disinformation — Krugman notes that 100% of skeptical studies have fossil fuel backing, while critics face sponsored attacks. Krugman's conclusion: the only durable solution is reducing wealth concentration at the top, because "if there are men big enough to own the government, they're going to own the government." Oligarchy is not the root of all evil, but it is the root of a lot of it [32].

The powermaxxing extends to AI policy and antitrust. Krugman's interview with Lina Khan — who chaired the FTC under Biden and played an important role on the Mamdani transition team — frames the AI supply chain as a stack in which each layer (chips, hyperscalers, cloud infrastructure, models, apps and services) has economic properties that can easily tip toward monopoly through network effects. Allowing monopolization at one layer without rules such as common carriage or equal access can result in other layers becoming monopolized or distorted, depriving the public of the full promise and innovation of the technologies. The FTC had been investigating bottlenecks emerging at the chip layer (Nvidia as gatekeeper) and the cloud layer (hyperscalers privileging their own AI products). The "interlocking directorates" pattern — Microsoft and OpenAI, Google and Amazon — raises questions about whether existing monopolists are gaining outsized control over competitively strategic decisions through opaque financial relationships [27]. Khan's February 2025 *Times* op-ed "Stop Worshiping the American Tech Giants" framed the choice starkly: paradigm-breaking innovations historically come from competitive markets, not from markets where gatekeepers decide who enters. The catalyst was DeepSeek — a Chinese AI firm whose model rivalled American sophistication with much greater efficiency, shocking Silicon Valley and Wall Street. The conflict of interest is structural: Microsoft, Amazon, and Google have their own cloud computing businesses, so they have incentives to favor AI models that are inefficient (consuming more compute) rather than efficient. Allowing American giants to control these markets risks innovating on trajectories the incumbents determine rather than letting competition drive efficiency. The historical cautionary tale is Boeing: the late-1990s merger approval allowing Boeing to buy up its last US rival, McDonnell Douglas, led down a path where Boeing's planes started falling apart and people died — a lack of competition that probably played a role, compounded by a culture shift from engineering to McKinsey-type consulting [27]. The Trump administration's AI policy embodies the national-champions risk Khan warned about. The CEOs of the major technology companies have made currying favor with the White House a top business priority — sitting on the stage at the inauguration, making regular pilgrimages to state dinners. There is a very serious risk of regulatory capture: rules and regulations favoring the biggest, most powerful incumbents at the expense of startups and newer firms that may have better ideas. The empirical evidence on the Schumpeter-Arrow debate shows that monopolies can be good at incremental improvements on existing technologies, but the breakthrough, paradigm-shifting innovations have come from outsiders and competitive markets. "Openness and competition have been such a key driver of American innovation and market growth" [27]. ## The Collapse of Institutional Restraint

[Adam Tooze]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/adam-tooze.html) introduces "hyper-agency" as the concept explaining why destructive fantasies are now being acted upon by men in power — Netanyahu's project of total regional revision, Musk going to Mars, Trump living out his fantasies in real time, Hegseth with his crusader tattoos. The question is not whether such fantasies existed before (every prior Pentagon holder from Western civilization may have had "wet dreams" about fighting a crusader war) but why the restraints that previously prevented their enactment have collapsed. Tooze traces this to a chain of contingent events that is overdetermined but not necessary — the situation was not inevitable, but the lines of determination can be traced [18]. The hyper-agency concept connects to the broader pattern: institutional restraints — the courts, the civil service, the alliance system, the presumption that certain things "are not done" — have been dismantled one by one. The Slaughter ruling stripped regulatory independence; the Kleptocracy Tracker documents open monetization of power; the [Hormuz ceasefire collapse]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/hormuz-ceasefire-collapse-2026.html) demonstrates that even foreign policy failure does not generate restraint. The cumulative effect is a system in which the remaining constraint on executive action is the personal psychology of the leader — the most dangerous possible constraint, as O'Brien's "leader-as-decision-maker" thesis demonstrates [18].

The collapse operates through theatrical violence as governance. [David Frum]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) documents how ICE has become less a law-enforcement agency than a content creator — producing videos of aggressive arrests set to pumping music for MAGA social media. ICE lowered training standards and ditched background vetting to meet deportation targets. The agency has killed four people since the crackdown began in June 2025, with 30+ incidents of agents opening fire or holding people at gunpoint. JD Vance's vociferous defense of the Minneapolis ICE shooting was planned, not spontaneous — reflecting the MAGA logic of redistributing respect away from overeducated coastal elites toward white Americans who feel left behind. The ICE agent who killed Renee Nicole Good is Trump's second-term Kyle Rittenhouse: enforcement of the MAGA vision of who must submit to whom [11]. MAGA does not reliably care about laws or the people who enforce them — Trump pardoned 1,500 January 6 rioters including many convicted of violent offenses against police, denigrated the FBI, and transformed it into a tool of retribution. The only law-enforcement agency the administration consistently champions is the one with the most violent record. The point is to prove that the fearsome power of the American state is being wielded by righteous MAGA hands against despised MAGA targets [11].

The collapse operates through collaboration. Applebaum traces the career of [Lindsey Graham]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/lindsey-graham.html) as the quintessential case study of political collaboration under Trump. Graham — a JAG Corps lawyer, reserve officer, and lifelong patriotic internationalist who once called Trump a "race-baiting, xenophobic, religious bigot" who should "go to hell" — made a 180-degree transformation, becoming one of the president's most important defenders. Applebaum frames this through the lens of collaboration under political occupation: like Markus Wolf, who stuck with the East German regime, versus Wolfgang Leonhard, who escaped. Graham had a deep need to be relevant, to be in the game, at any cost — and that need, rather than any particular achievement, is what he will be remembered for [16]. The comparison to Mitt Romney — who voted to impeach Trump despite seeming less deeply attached to patriotic ideals — underscores that the clichés about who will resist and who will comply are often wrong. The broader pattern illustrates the institutional corrosion of democratic governance: figures who once defended founding ideals choose collaboration over resistance, not through dramatic conversion but through incremental accommodation that eventually becomes indistinguishable from allegiance [16].

The collapse is symbolized in the appropriation of state institutions. [Heather Cox Richardson]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/heather-cox-richardson.html) tracks the consolidation of authoritarian symbolism through mid-July 2026. The Treasury announced a commemorative $1 coin with [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html)'s likeness — a practice associated with living political leaders who try to make themselves indistinguishable from the government (Bashar al-Assad in Syria, Idi Amin in Uganda, Saddam Hussein in Iraq), made possible only by exploiting a loophole in the 1866 law barring living people from U.S. financial instruments. Trump is building a helipad on the South Lawn of the White House without permission from Congress or any review panel, with Lockheed Martin — a major defense contractor that makes the helicopters Trump uses — donating the construction. The Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool renovation he ordered without review is failing at the seams; experts attributed the failures to installation flaws, but the White House denied missteps and had the Department of Justice arrest a 67-year-old cyclist for "vandalism" after he touched the detached lining. Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche — Trump's former criminal defense attorney — slipped during his confirmation hearing, answering "I'm his lawyer" before correcting himself; a federal judge questioned whether Blanche is fit to practice law. In Seattle, federal judges unanimously appointed a former judge as U.S. attorney to replace a Trump appointee whose 120-day interim position had expired; within an hour of taking the oath, he was fired by email [23]. A Washington Post/Ipsos poll showed Trump's approval at 37%, with only 15% of Americans "strongly" approving and 66% saying groceries are unaffordable — the lowest strong-approval figure on record [23].

The collapse extends to the international system, where the belief in a consequence-free world meets its limits. [Phillips O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) documents Undersecretary of State Elbridge Colby's July 2026 tweet thread as the moment the Trump administration's own policies produced a backlash it could no longer dismiss. Colby — who for years has been lambasting Europeans for not doing more to defend themselves and leading the call for withdrawal of forces from Europe — insisted that a "collective middle powers strategy" is a distraction and that "access to the American DIB is a privilege, not a right." The thread is read as panic: the administration is realizing that its own Europe-withdrawal policies have produced the strategic autonomy European states are now beginning to pursue. The thread insults Europeans as too weak to act independently while simultaneously threatening and pleading with them not to do so — destroying its own case in real time. The episode illustrates a general pattern: a great power that believes it lives in a consequence-free world, where it can do what it wants and others will simply bow to its magnificence, eventually discovers that actions have consequences. ## Ideological Weaponization: From Anticommunism to Dehumanization

The empirical record of communism's failure is clear. Gliedman argues that communist governments are authoritarian by structural necessity — the seizure of private property required by communist doctrine cannot be achieved through voluntary compliance and therefore requires coercive state power. This authoritarian character is not a deviation from communist theory but an implementation of it. The historical record — Soviet Union, Mao's China, Cuba — confirms that communist governance leads to mass death, not as an accident but as a consequence of the concentration of power required to enforce collectivization [2]. The economic failure is equally structural: communist governments cannot run an economy because central planning cannot replicate the information-processing function of price signals in a market. The only nominally functioning communist states are those heavily subsidized by external patrons — Cuba by the Soviet Union and later Venezuela — which demonstrates that the system cannot sustain itself [2]. Polovynka extends the critique to theory itself: communism is bad even in theory. The Communist Manifesto contains fundamental logical flaws — the assumption that humans are angels who will work according to ability and receive according to need without incentive structures is not an empirical error but a category error about human nature [3]. [Marian Tupy]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/marian-tupy.html) documents communism's 100-year human cost: 100 million+ deaths, drawn from "The Black Book of Communism" (94M+) and subsequent research (80M+). The death toll exceeds that of Tsarist Russia and the Inquisition combined. The deaths are structural consequences of the concentration of power required to enforce collectivization, not accidental policy outcomes. Tupy notes the irony of socialism's intellectual resurgence despite this record — a resurgence driven by dissatisfaction with crony capitalism, not evidence of socialist success [7].

[Paul Krugman]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/paul-krugman.html) draws the sharpest distinction between social democracy — a market-based system with progressive taxation, safety nets, and regulation — and actual socialism (government ownership of the means of production). He argues that very few Americans, including self-described "democratic socialists," are actually socialists. America already has extensive social-democratic policies (Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, progressive taxation, minimum wage) that are immensely popular, even among Republicans [6]. The appearance of rising socialist sentiment is largely an artifact of right-wing propagandists labeling mainstream social democratic policies as "socialist." This conflation causes some voters to reclaim the label — "Well, if that's socialism, I guess I'm OK with socialism." The real groundswell is not a lurch to the left but dismay over an economy that increasingly favors a tiny group of billionaires and a political system that works on their behalf. The important radical movement in America is not left-wing socialism but right-wing radicals who want to dismantle both social democracy and democracy itself [6]. The anticommunist weaponization and the oligarchy spiral are thus two faces of the same dynamic: a political movement that labels social democracy "communism" while actively dismantling the democratic institutions that would restrain its own capture of the state.

[Heather Cox Richardson]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/heather-cox-richardson.html) identifies Trump's July 2026 pivot to anticommunist rhetoric as a sign of panic rather than confidence. Trump's Mt. Rushmore speech framed political opponents as "communists" — "the greatest threat to our country, including World War I, World War II, Pearl Harbor, or even 9/11" — a fabricated narrative constructed after the electoral success of Democratic Socialists in primary elections and the popularity of New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani. Richardson notes the irony that it is Trump, not his opponents, who is taking government stakes in private enterprises — the actual definition of communism. She draws a careful distinction: Democratic Socialists embrace private enterprise alongside government provision of services like healthcare, education, and infrastructure — positions that echo Republican platforms from the 1860s through the Eisenhower era (91% top marginal tax rate in 1956). The conflation of Democratic Socialism with communism is a deliberate political strategy [15].

The *Unhumans* ideology operationalizes the dehumanization rhetoric that the anticommunism-as-political-weapon framing (above) had flagged as a sign of panic. The escalation from anticommunist rhetoric to outright dehumanization came on July 17, 2026, when Trump, Secretary of State Rubio, and deputy White House chief of staff Stephen Miller threw the weight of the U.S. government behind the far-right conspiracy theory articulated in Jack Posobiec and Joshua Lisec's *Unhumans: The Secret History of Communist Revolutions (and How to Crush Them)* — blurbed by Donald Trump Jr. and J.D. Vance and introduced by Stephen Bannon. The book argues that anyone from communists to "progressives" places themselves "outside the category" of humanity and must be "unhumaned" first; the authors tie together left-wing revolutions from the late Roman Republic to the French and Russian revolutions and treat Francisco Franco — who ordered illegal detentions, torture, and mass killings in the name of social cleansing — as a hero [24]. Richardson documents how Miller's State Department speech echoed the book's central framing almost verbatim, describing antifa demonstrators as people of whom "not one looks normal" and whose "outer appearance becomes a manifestation of their inner hatred." Bannon argued on his *War Room* podcast that the communist-threat narrative is "a predicate for immediate action" — a national security emergency executive order that "took all of SAVE America plus plus plus plus." Trump's prime-time speech the previous night tied American journalists to an alleged Chinese plot, claimed China acquired 220 million U.S. voter files (the White House's own documents showed such information is publicly available for purchase), and demanded Congress pass the SAVE America voter-suppression act. DHS Secretary Mullin then threatened state officials with criminal prosecution if they don't comply with Trump's demands to purge voter rolls through a federal digital tool that federal judges have blocked sixteen times for flagging recently naturalized citizens as noncitizens. The White House declared that "far-left extremism will be treated with the same seriousness and ferocity the world has long reserved for jihadist terrorism," despite a 2025 CSIS analysis showing left-wing political violence remains much lower than historical levels of right-wing and jihadist violence [24]. See [Trump Election Interference Pretext]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/trump-election-fraud-pretext-2026.html).

The shift from naming opponents "communists" to naming them "unhuman" is a categorical escalation: it removes political opponents from the category of persons to whom moral consideration is owed, and it provides the "predicate for immediate action" that Bannon explicitly names as the goal. The historical analogy is direct: the book treats Franco as a hero, and the administration's rhetoric mirrors the book's framing on the same days it demands the SAVE Act and threatens state officials with criminal prosecution for refusing to purge voter rolls.

## Comparative Democratic Decline

[Aurelien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/aurelien.html) traces the erosion of Western influence from its historical roots. The missionary and colonial era was characterized by seriousness, long-term commitment, and moral purpose — administrators spent lifetimes in-country, built schools and hospitals, and took their vocation seriously. The post-independence aid industry, by contrast, is characterized by short rotations, performative projects, mind-numbing bureaucracy, and a self-sustaining consultancy class that extracts rents from the aid pipeline. The structural problems are compounded by the contemporary West's loss of credibility: preaching governance while political systems collapse, offering drug-smuggling assistance while parts of Europe become narco-states, presuming to sort out others' crises while France's political system disintegrates and Britain faces its seventh government in ten years [13]. The deeper ideological problem is the West's "soulless and unappealing liberal-technocratic managerial ideology" competing against states whose elites project "not just competence, but a genuine civilisational ideology, and are not self-mutilated by self-hatred." The West is losing ground to states with civilisational confidence — not because of hard power deficit but because of ideological vacuity. "All we have left now is money" [13].

[Paul Krugman]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/paul-krugman.html) challenges the conventional narrative that [Europe]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/europe.html) is clearly falling behind the US economically. He argues that productivity growth comparisons are misleading, that Europeans have more time and social security, and that the US-Europe paradox (slow European growth without a growing gap) needs better explanation. The narrative of European decline, while containing elements of truth, is being weaponized to justify policy choices that would dismantle the social democratic model [8]. [The Honest Sorcerer]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/the-honest-sorcerer.html) and [Nate Hagens]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/nate-hagens.html) provide a different angle on European decline: not relative economic performance but absolute industrial decline driven by energy costs, the loss of cheap Russian gas, and the impossibility of maintaining industrial throughput with renewable energy alone. Europe's gas storage at 46% at end of June 2026, well below seasonal norms, is the physical indicator that the decline narrative has a material basis that Krugman's economic analysis does not capture [9].

[Velina Tchakarova]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/velina-tchakarova.html) argues that [Latin America]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/latin-america.html)'s rightward wave is converting ideological alignment into a hemispheric security bloc. The shift is significant because it represents a structural realignment rather than a cyclical swing — attitudes on crime and migration moved rightward independently of US political dynamics, meaning the realignment has indigenous roots. However, the wave carries instability. Disputed elections, institutional weakness, and leftist strongholds in Mexico and Brazil mean that the realignment is not uniform or settled. The conversion of ideological shift into security bloc architecture depends on whether right-leaning governments can consolidate institutional control — and the historical record suggests that this is not guaranteed [4].

Turkey's trajectory from competitive authoritarianism to full autocracy, documented by Yaşar Aydın ([SWP]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/swp.html)), illustrates how a single crisis event can consolidate autocracy. Erdoğan used the 2016 failed coup to consolidate power through comprehensive purges (~130,000 dismissed, ~332,000 arrested, 4,000 judges removed, 131 media companies shut down) under a state of emergency lasting until July 2018. The April 2017 constitutional referendum introduced a presidential system — passed by a narrow, contested majority. Freedom House downgraded Turkey to "Not Free" in 2018. The economic consequences are documented: average GDP growth fell from 6.5% to 3.5%, nominal GDP per capita fell from ~$11,000 to ~$9,700, FDI declined, inflation rose. See [Turkey]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/turkey.html) for the full trajectory [20]. Isabelle Werenfels (SWP) documents the systematic dismantling of Tunisia's democratic institutions under President Kais Saied — opposition figures sentenced to lengthy prison terms, the EU Ambassador summoned for meeting with trade unions, the 2011 democratic dividend entirely reversed. The democratic collapse is compounded by a soft power reversal: [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) has gained visibility and popularity while Europe has lost both. See [Tunisia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/tunisia.html) [21]. The comparative pattern across Turkey, Tunisia, and the West is that democratic decline is neither sudden nor uniformly coercive: it proceeds through incremental institutional capture, the weaponization of crisis, and the exhaustion of credible alternatives.

A separate political dynamic — the rapid backlash against local AI data center construction (opposition rising from 42% to 71% in under a year) — is treated in the [technology and AI diplomacy]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/tech-stack-diplomacy.html) theme, as it concerns the politics of compute infrastructure rather than democratic legitimacy per se.

## Enforcement as Governance: ICE, Xenophobia, and the Safety Net

The enforcement apparatus serves as a substitute for governance across multiple contexts. Müller ([SWP]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/swp.html)) documents the 2026 South African case as a template for how anti-migration sentiment can be organized into a substitute for governance. See [South Africa]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/south-africa.html). The structural ingredients are familiar across the post-conflict and post-colonial world: a liberation movement whose transformation promise was unfulfilled, a large unemployed youth population, service-delivery failure, and a visible migrant minority that can be scapegoated. Operation Dudula and March and March combine social-media reach, "concerned citizen" framing, and plausible-deniability distance from the political parties backing them — a template for networked populist mobilization that can scale faster than institutions can respond. The November 2025 High Court judgment affirming that human rights apply regardless of citizenship, and the 160-organization counter-coalition, illustrate the resilience of the constitutional inheritance — but the question is whether that resilience can hold as economic conditions worsen and political entrepreneurs learn to weaponize xenophobia. The pattern is not unique to South Africa: the same structural ingredients are present in many post-conflict and post-colonial states, and the networked mobilization template is exportable [28]. See [Migration Policy]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/migration-policy.html).

In the United States, the enforcement apparatus operates with lethal consequences. [Heather Cox Richardson]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/heather-cox-richardson.html) documents ICE agents shooting and killing two immigrants within one week in July 2026: Lorenzo Salgado Araujo, 52, in Houston on July 7, and Joan Sebastián Durán Guerrero, 25, in Biddeford, Maine, on July 13. Neither was the intended target of the operation in which he was killed. The two died six days apart, rekindling the unpopularity of the administration's crackdown on undocumented immigrants. In the Maine case, the shooter was identified as David Brouillette — an Army combat veteran and former security agent whose ex-wife revealed he had a domestic abuse history, with both his ex-wives holding restraining orders against him. His ex-wife told reporters she did not believe he had actually been hired as an ICE agent when he mentioned it. Thirty-nine Democratic senators, led by Senator Angus King (I-ME), sent a letter to DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin demanding "immediate, thorough, independent, and transparent investigations" of both shootings. The letter noted that former DHS Secretary [Kristi Noem]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) and White House immigration advisor Tom Homan had both committed to equipping agents with body cameras, yet the agents involved in these shootings were not wearing them — confirming that neither commitment was honored. ICE's acting director David Venturella had promised Congress that all field agents would be equipped with cameras by the end of July. The senators also demanded that agents wear clear ICE identification labels. The killings occurred amid a record enforcement surge: immigration enforcement scholar Austin Kocher noted that arrests in early July hit 1,474 per day, a Trump administration record. *The Guardian* reported that many of the thousands of new ICE agents recruited in recent months lacked requisite qualifications and were not properly vetted — with Brouillette's hiring despite his domestic abuse history serving as a case in point [30]. See [Donald Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html). The political cost of the crackdown compounds: Senator Susan Collins — chair of the Senate Appropriations Committee and a key vote for the $70 billion immigration-enforcement appropriation — called for Homeland Security Secretary Mullin to "cease all non-urgent vehicle stops"; ICE issued a memo ordering agents to prioritize other tactics; Trump then publicly insisted, incorrectly, that traffic stops must remain an ICE tool [22].

The enforcement logic extends to the systematic dismantling of domestic public services while war spending escalates. [Heather Cox Richardson]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/heather-cox-richardson.html) documents that under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, 440,000 Arizonans lost SNAP benefits — the deepest food-assistance cuts in US history — with expanded work rules and costs shifted to states, while Arizona laid off a third of caseworkers. Agriculture Secretary Rollins defended the cuts by citing fraud, but the error rates reflect classification mistakes, not fraud. [RFK Jr.]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) announced withholding over $1 billion in Medicaid funding from California and Minnesota on unproven fraud claims — California had already lost $1.3 billion since May, now facing $867 million more; Minnesota had $243 million withheld since February, with $200 million more. The National Park Service is being systematically dismantled under Interior Secretary Burgum — staff slashed, funds starved, political narrative imposed on historical interpretation. TSA began reverting to pre-9/11 private security screening at Des Moines and Tampa airports, abandoning the federal model created after 9/11 precisely because private screening had proven vulnerable. Meanwhile, the Pentagon seeks a $67 billion supplemental for the [Iran war]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html), with the Navy and Air Force exhausting operational funds by end of July. The pattern reveals an ideological preference for military spending over domestic welfare, enforced through executive action rather than legislative deliberation. Sen. Ossoff's confrontation of Defense Secretary Hegseth — pressing whether his claims that Iran's missile threat was "destroyed" and "combat ineffective" were truthful, and receiving only deflection — underscores that the war's declared successes cannot withstand scrutiny. Sen. Andy Kim's MediKids proposal — Medicaid-for-all-children from birth to age 26, addressing 4.4 million uninsured and 23 million underinsured children, endorsed by medical associations — represents the inverse ideological proposition: that government exists to protect its citizens rather than to wage wars it cannot win [31].

Congress has acquiesced in its own marginalization. [Phillips O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) documents that 144 days into the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html), Congress remains passive — the War Powers Act has not been invoked, no war authorization has been debated, and the Pentagon is suppressing press briefings (CNN reported on the suppression). The constitutional crisis is not only that [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) launched a war of choice without congressional authorization; it is that Congress has acquiesced in its own marginalization. The absence of legislative pushback compounds the institutional hollowing documented across the judiciary (Slaughter), the civil service (oligarchic capture), and the regulatory state. An anonymous Pentagon official confirmed to O'Brien that the US does not have enough munitions to safely sustain operations — and "I don't think the White House is aware of that" — yet Congress has not held hearings on munitions readiness or war costs [33]. See [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html) and [US-Iran War of 2026]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html).

## The Collapse of Global Respect and the Election Interference Endgame

[Paul Krugman]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/paul-krugman.html) documents the spectacular gap between [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html)'s boasts and the empirical record on global respect for America. Pew's [Global Attitudes Survey](https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2026/07/15/people-in-many-countries-now-view-china-more-positively-than-the-u-s/) shows that people in many countries now view [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) more positively than the US — and formal survey data is unnecessary to confirm what overseas travel makes obvious. Governments around the world, including nations that used to be America's most loyal allies, are scrambling to reduce their dependence, economic and military, on an America that can't be trusted and has proved itself far weaker than anyone imagined [26]. The "America is respected" lie serves two political functions. First, it obviously matters to Trump personally: claims that America was a laughingstock under Biden but is now admired are near the top of many of his speeches, and Trump lives for external validation — "clearly, the delusion that the world is in awe of his prowess is key to his fragile sense of self-worth." Second, and more important, the boasts about international reputation are part of the case Trump is building for disrupting and rejecting the results of the midterm elections. Trump insists that since he is doing an incredible job — "presidenting like nobody has ever presidented before" — nobody should get in his way or place any limits on his power. At this point, none of what Trump says is really about persuasion; it is all about laying the groundwork for his attempt to destroy democracy. See [Trump Election Interference Pretext]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/trump-election-fraud-pretext-2026.html) [26].

The election interference endgame connects foreign policy failure to domestic repression. Krugman argues that Trump's pivot from "the economy is great, we won" to bombing Iran with no strategy signals an enormous domestic risk. With the Iran war unable to deliver a victory narrative for the midterms, the administration has shifted to preparing the groundwork for massive interference in the November elections — a primetime speech about 2020 election fraud as pretext for blocking fair elections or blocking them entirely. The proposition that "there are things even Trump would not do" has been "the best way to be wrong about everything, every step of the Trump administration." See [Trump Election Interference Pretext]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/trump-election-fraud-pretext-2026.html) [19]. This is not causation — the authoritarian impulse was always present — but the removal of the last constraint of electoral pragmatism. The Slaughter ruling provided the judicial infrastructure; the oligarchy spiral provided the financial infrastructure; the Iran war failure removed the electoral incentive to restrain [19].

## The Authoritarian Coalition and the Normalization Mechanism

[Phillips O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) frames the transatlantic field of pro-Trump, anti-EU, anti-civil-society figures as a unified ideological project, not a coalition of tactical allies. [Laura Loomer]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/laura-loomer.html), Tommy Robinson, [Donald Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html), Viktor Orban, Robert Fico, Nigel Farage, and Tucker Carlson share a single objective: "destroying civil society in the USA and Europe and replacing it with some authoritarian, anti-liberal, discriminatory state, which uses state power to harass and imprison its enemies and denies citizens their rights" [34]. Tactical disagreements among them (e.g., on Ukraine) mask fundamental ideological alignment. Similarities across the coalition outweigh any issue-specific differences; the practical consequence of praising one coalition figure's "good" position is the broader normalization of the rest of their program.

The mechanism operates as [Political Normalization]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/political-normalization.html): one acceptable position from an authoritarian figure gains mainstream praise, the figure reaches new audiences, and the broader project is amplified through that reach. [Laura Loomer]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/laura-loomer.html)'s late-July 2026 [Ukraine visit]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/laura-loomer-ukraine-visit-2026.html) is the canonical case: she traveled to [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html), was interviewed by [Zelensky]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/zelensky.html), and was praised by some pro-Ukraine voices for her supposed conversion — yet throughout the trip she tweeted that "I haven't seen a single Muslim here... It's so refreshing," publicly called for banning Muslims from holding office in America, demanded the arrest of [Mark Zuckerberg]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html), accused [Microsoft]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) of working with the Chinese to steal the 2020 election, posed with Tommy Robinson in a "DEFEND EUROPE" shirt, and called on Trump to further gut US intelligence agencies. After Trump's primetime speech she posted a thread demanding arrests — "It's time for people to go to jail!" — and claiming that China had "illegally acquired over 220 MILLION American voter files," that "COVID-19 scamdemic was a Chinese bioweapon," and that "China helped the Democrats steal the 2020 Presidential election" [34]. One pro-Ukraine visit would not rehabilitate a racist conspiracist in any normal accounting — but the praise helps her broadcast her broader message to audiences that would otherwise reject her, and provides cover for the next step of the underlying project.

O'Brien documents the same tactical-pivot logic at policy scale in [Donald Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html)'s Ukraine posture. Ten pro-Ukraine/anti-Russia statements between January and October 2025 — sanctions threats, Patriot/Tomahawk talk, "paper tiger" rhetoric, "bullshit from Putin" — produced zero policy change. After 18 months of Trump 2.0: all US aid to [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html) stopped, Ukraine's Patriots depleted, Russia selling oil freely, Ukraine has no effective defense from Russian ballistic missiles. The Graham-Blumenthal sanctions bill, even if passed, will not necessarily be implemented; Trump's public suggestion that Iran be added functioned to "muddy the waters" and [Laura Loomer]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/laura-loomer.html) misread it as substantive pro-Ukraine movement [34]. Trump's rhetorical shifts track adjustments that serve his interest — recognition that [Ukraine]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ukraine.html) has cards and Russia is struggling on the battlefield — not humanitarian awakenings. Europe's gains in the war have come not from flattering Trump but from disregarding the United States and helping Ukraine fight — flattery has demonstrably failed.

The "It Is All One Fight" thesis closes the geographic gap between Ukraine, the US, and Europe: the democratic-vs-authoritarian divide is one fight with separate battles. When the United States stood for democratic civil liberties, the rule of law, curbing the coercive power of the state, and constraining the political power of massive capital, it made a difference — even if it did not always live up to those ideals. A Ukraine win alongside an American devolution into permanent Trumpist self-loathing and isolation would produce a "much darker place." Reciprocally, an American recovery that abandoned an already-damaged Ukraine would be equally disastrous. The fight in Ukraine and the United States is structural — not loose separate contests but one combined struggle across theaters [34]. See [Political Normalization]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/political-normalization.html), [Laura Loomer]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/laura-loomer.html), and [Donald Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html).

## The European Establishment Crisis: CDU Collapse and the AfD Eastern Surge

The collapse of the European liberal-conservative governing mainstream — long seen as more stable than the American counterpart — is now visibly underway. The CDU's July 2026 cabinet reshuffle cascade under Chancellor Merz illustrates the same legitimacy crisis in different form: the governing party's crisis is not ideology but competence. CDU staff circulated Loriot's "Das schiefe Bild" sketch under the caption "Der Kanzler am Werk" — a comic scene in which a well-meaning agent, attempting to fix one crooked picture, cascades through total room destruction. The cultural artifact captures the dynamic precisely: the attempt at refresh destroys more than it restores. The CDU has polled behind the AfD for months; the reshuffle deepened rather than reversed the decline. The governing formation defined by "regieren können" — by the capacity to govern competently — has had that self-understanding publicly demolished by its own members, including Johannes Winkel (Junge Union), who stated he could no longer defend the party's competence in his Wahlkreis. East-west divergence sharpens the crisis: the eastern Landesverbände (Saxony-Anhalt, Saxony, and via the Schnieder affair Rhineland-Palatinate) were alienated consecutively by a reshuffle designed to refresh Central-European momentum. The internal collapse of the establishment party and the structural decline in its polls are two phases of the same phenomenon. See [CDU Internal Leadership Crisis]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/cdu-internal-leadership-crisis-2026.html) [35].

The AfD's eastern regional strength, classified "gesichert rechtsextrem" (confirmed right-extremist) by the Verfassungsschutz, directly connects the domestic political crisis to the NATO legitimacy question. The classification is not merely descriptive — it is the threshold for constitutional boundary-testing on a state-government scale. AfD Spitzenkandidat Ulrich Siegmund, leading INSA polls in Saxony-Anhalt at 41%, has stated explicitly: "We will of course put the topic of ideological energy policy on the agenda — and in such a way that we advocate strongly against sanctions, for cheap energy, no matter which country." When asked if "from Russia" is included: "Yes, of course." Kiesewetter (CDU, Auswärtiger Ausschuss) frames the consequence: the Kremlin is "exploiting divergent Russian-policy attitudes within Germany" to make Germany unreliable as NATO's logistics hub. A western defense source frames Russia's broader strategy: "the fastest way to break the Alliance is to find and deepen political fault lines." The AfD thus operates as the vehicle by which foreign-policy positions aligned with Moscow enter the institutional architecture of a NATO state — through electoral success, then through constitutionally-procedural federalism, then through obstruction of routine military operations the alliance depends on. The September 20 Saxony-Anhalt election is a concrete test of what such an AfD-led Landesregierung would do with control of the state Interior Ministry, Landespolizei, and Landesverfassungsschutz. See [Saxony-Anhalt State Election]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/sachsen-anhalt-state-election-2026.html) [36].
