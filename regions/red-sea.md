# Background

The Red Sea is a strategic maritime basin connecting the Mediterranean, via the Suez Canal, to the Indian Ocean, via the Bab el-Mandeb Strait. It is one of the world's most critical trade corridors. The basin borders [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html), [Egypt]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/egypt.html), [Sudan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/sudan.html), Eritrea, [Ethiopia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ethiopia.html) (landlocked but pursuing access), [Somalia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/somalia.html)/Somaliland, Djibouti, and [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html) (via the Gulf of Aqaba).

The basin's strategic landscape has historically been dominated by the Houthi threat to commercial shipping, but is increasingly shaped by broader interstate rivalries.

# Current Situation

The Red Sea is evolving from a theater dominated by non-state maritime threats — chiefly the Houthi rebels — into one defined by overlapping interstate rivalries with potentially broader implications for regional stability [1]. The Houthis themselves avoided direct involvement in the Iran war and remain focused primarily on consolidating control over northwestern Yemen, including the capital, Sanaa, and much of the country's Red Sea coastline, while contending with the Saudi-backed internationally recognized government based in Aden and the UAE-backed Southern Transitional Council [1]. The more consequential changes are taking place around Yemen, where states are pursuing competing military, economic, and political objectives [1].

[Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html) is weighing a multibillion-dollar expansion of its East-West oil pipeline to the Red Sea, a project that could add up to 2 million barrels per day in capacity, as it seeks to reduce reliance on the Strait of Hormuz for energy exports [1]. Concurrently, container shipping giants Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd announced they would reroute a key Asia-Mediterranean service back through the Red Sea and Suez Canal rather than around Africa's Cape of Good Hope [1].

Along the Horn of Africa littoral, [Ethiopia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/ethiopia.html)'s military leadership has continued to signal its determination to secure maritime access, with Navy Commander Kindu Gezu calling it "inevitable" that Ethiopia would regain its "unfairly lost" access to the Red Sea, while Army Chief Birhanu Jula told senior officers that "elaborate preparations" were needed [1]. Ethiopia has sought arrangements with coastal actors, including Somaliland and Puntland, to diversify its maritime options [1]. [Israel]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/israel.html)'s [recognition of Somaliland]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/israel-somaliland-recognition.html) in 2025 reflects its effort to extend its security perimeter into the southern Red Sea, near the Bab el-Mandeb, monitoring and countering Houthi activity while safeguarding maritime routes [1]. [Turkey]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/turkey.html) has expanded its influence through its military partnership with [Somalia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/somalia.html), its support for the Sudanese Armed Forces, and its presence in Libya, seeing engagement across the Red Sea and toward the Indian Ocean as essential to securing its regional position [1]. The evolving Egypt-Turkey relationship adds another dimension concerning for Israel, as Ankara builds political, economic, and security partnerships in the Horn of Africa [1].

[Egypt]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/egypt.html)'s July 3 inauguration of the Octagon, a sprawling military headquarters designed to centralize command and control, improve crisis management, and integrate intelligence capabilities, reflected Cairo's ambitions to enhance its regional military posture [1]. Egypt's approach includes efforts to influence Sudan's civil war, continued pressure on Ethiopia over the GERD dispute, and closer alignment with Saudi Arabia, including the 2017 transfer of sovereignty over the Red Sea islands of Tiran and Sanafir to Riyadh [1]. [Sudan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/sudan.html)'s civil war between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the Rapid Support Forces is a major source of instability, particularly because Sudan possesses a strategically significant coastline along the Red Sea [1]. The conflict has become an arena for regional competition as outside actors back rival factions in pursuit of influence over Sudan's political future and access to maritime infrastructure [1].

# Key Dynamics

- **Chokepoint geography** — The Bab el-Mandeb Strait and the Suez Canal together make the Red Sea a global trade artery. Disruptions cascade through energy markets, shipping costs, and supply chains worldwide.
- **Interstate rivalries replacing non-state threats** — The strategic landscape is shifting from Houthi maritime disruption to competing state-level ambitions for coastline, ports, and maritime infrastructure.
- **Hormuz bypass dynamic** — Saudi Arabia's pipeline expansion reflects a broader effort to reduce dependence on the Strait of Hormuz, making the Red Sea an alternative energy export corridor.
- **Horn of Africa-Middle East linkage** — The Red Sea basin connects [African]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/africa.html) and [Middle Eastern]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html) security dynamics, with Turkey, Israel, Egypt, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE all projecting influence across the waterway.
- **Port infrastructure as geopolitical currency** — Port concessions (Berbera, Djibouti, Mogadishu, Tadjourah) translate commercial investment into military and diplomatic leverage.
- **Contested — Are the Houthis Iran's proxy or an autonomous actor?** — The proxy framing and the autonomy framing diverge on whether Tehran directs Houthi actions or the movement exploits opportunities independently. See the full analysis below.

# Analysis

## The Houthi Blockade and Horizontal Escalation

In mid-July 2026, as [Trump]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/trump.html) announced plans to attack [Iran]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/iran.html)'s bridges and power plants, [Heather Cox Richardson]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/heather-cox-richardson.html) reports, Iranian leaders asked the Houthis they back in Yemen to close the Bab el-Mandeb strait — the passage between the Red Sea and the Indian Ocean that, alongside the Strait of Hormuz, is one of the two main routes for Middle East oil exports [2]. Closure of the second strait would exacerbate energy shortages even as the U.S. [SPR]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/spr-depletion-crisis.html) dropped to its lowest level since 1983 [2]. The move operationalizes Iran's capacity to widen the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) geographically by activating its Red Sea proxy, imposing costs on the global economy without requiring Iran itself to close Hormuz [2]. Within 48 hours of July 16, [Robert Pape]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/robert-pape.html) documents, the Houthis launched a coordinated barrage of ballistic missiles and drones targeting [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html)'s Abha International Airport in direct retaliation for airstrikes on Sanaa — an assault that risks *horizontal escalation*, opening a second maritime front at the Bab el-Mandeb gateway [3]. If realized, Pape argues, this second front would trap critical Saudi exports and inflict far more damage on world energy markets at a time they are already highly vulnerable, with oil inventories running down [3]. Until now the Iran war had centered on the Strait of Hormuz; opening a second front at Bab el-Mandeb transforms the crisis from a regional confrontation into a broader contest over the security of global energy flows, increasing pressure on Washington, Riyadh, and their allies to respond militarily rather than diplomatically, while Iran gains additional leverage without directly committing its own forces [3].

On July 20, the Houthis announced a blockade of Saudi Arabia's Red Sea ports, a move that could potentially halt Saudi oil exports to Asia — approximately 7% of global oil supply [4]. Whether the Houthis can enforce a total blockade is, Colibasanu argues, irrelevant: the demonstrated will and capability to disrupt traffic is sufficient to deter shipping, as precedent from 2018 and 2024 confirms [4]. The announcement compounds the US-Iran War's Hormuz disruption by opening a second maritime front — effectively threatening both major Middle East oil export corridors simultaneously [4]. Richardson similarly reports that the Houthi naval blockade threatens passage through the Bab el-Mandeb; if closed, the global oil supply would take another 7% hit on top of existing Hormuz disruptions — a scenario Colibasanu had already flagged as escalating the cost cascade across insurance, freight, and energy markets [5]. [Phillips O'Brien]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/phillips-obrien.html) reports that the Houthis then announced targeting tankers carrying Saudi Arabia's oil — a direct threat to Saudi export revenue that extends the Red Sea maritime threat from general commercial shipping to Saudi-specific vessels, creating a layered maritime coercion campaign against Saudi energy exports [6].

[Velina Tchakarova]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/velina-tchakarova.html)'s Day 144 diagnostic identifies a generative cross-domain pairing between S1 (US-Iran War and Hormuz) and S5 (Global Supply Chain Fracture): strikes on Iran's power infrastructure produced the Red Sea blockade through a third-party actor [7]. Unlike transmissive pairings — where stress in one domain worsens conditions in another — a generative pairing creates entirely new disruption vectors [7]. The US strikes on Iranian infrastructure did not merely worsen existing Red Sea tensions; they produced the Houthi blockade of [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html) as a qualitatively new interdiction vector — one of three simultaneous maritime interdictions that define the S5 RUPTURE status — upgrading S5 from CRITICAL to RUPTURE [7]. The Red Sea thus functions not merely as a secondary theater but as a generative output of the S1 domain — the US-Iran War's kinetic dynamics produce Red Sea disruptions through proxy activation, making the [Red Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/red-sea.html) a downstream consequence of Hormuz rather than an independent crisis vector [7]. Tchakarova's Day 150 diagnostic records the transition from declaration to confirmed enforcement: the Houthis claimed missile and drone strikes on two Saudi oil tankers in the Red Sea, setting one ablaze — the first confirmed kinetic strikes on tankers rather than diversions alone [10]. In the framework's indicator architecture, indicator #3 (Yanbu loadings above 2.2 mbpd) has been upgraded from ACTIVE-NEG to **CONFIRMED-SUPPRESSED**: the behavioral compliance that preceded enforcement is now superseded by kinetic confirmation [10]. The S5 domain escalates further as the confirmed tanker strikes combine with the [Kazakhstan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/kazakhstan.html) CPC suspension to extend the supply chain fracture beyond the named chokepoints [10]. See [Global System Rupture]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/global-system-rupture.html).

## Energy Market and Supply Chain Impact

The kinetic escalation cascaded directly into energy markets. Richardson reports that the price of oil surged by 7% to more than $100 a barrel for Brent crude after Iran-backed Houthi rebels claimed to have attacked two Saudi Arabian oil tankers in the Red Sea, raising concerns about the potential closure of the Bab el-Mandeb strait, through which about 12% to 15% of the world's maritime trade passes every year [8]. The average price of gasoline in the United States rose to $4.09 a gallon [8]. These strikes represent a new development in the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html): after twelve days of escalating strikes between the U.S. and Iran centered on the Strait of Hormuz, the Red Sea front adds a second chokepoint to the crisis [8]. When economists at Moody's added higher gas prices to the cost of the war, they estimated it has cost U.S. households about $1,100 each [8]. The energy price shock underscores how disruption at the Bab el-Mandeb cascades directly into consumer costs, translating maritime insecurity into household-level economic pain [8].

## Pipeline Bypass Architecture and "Bypass the Bypass"

[Javier Blas]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/javier-blas.html) frames the Red Sea problem as one of pipeline-capacity arithmetic. The Bab el-Mandeb strait — between war-torn Yemen on the Arabian Peninsula and Djibouti/Eritrea on the Horn of Africa — is approximately 14 nautical miles wide, narrower than the Strait of Hormuz's 21 nautical miles; its name translates roughly to "Gate of Tears" in recognition of its difficult navigational challenges. Saudi Arabia has been offloading most of its oil through the East-West pipeline at the [Red Sea]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/red-sea.html) port of Yanbu for more than 100 days — successfully bypassing [Hormuz]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html) until the Houthi threat emerged. With that workaround now itself under pressure, Saudi Arabia needs a bypass for the bypass — pushing barrels north through the Suez Canal and onward to the Mediterranean rather than south toward the chokepoint [12].

The constraint is Suez draft. Very Large Crude Carriers — the workhorse of long-haul crude transport, each carrying up to two million barrels — cannot transit the Suez Canal fully laden because the waterway is too shallow. The required workaround is lightering: supertankers offload part of their cargo into the Sumed pipeline — a 50-year-old, 320-kilometer pair of pipes connecting Ain Sukhna (an Egyptian town on the north end of the Red Sea) to Sidi Kerir (a port near Alexandria on the Mediterranean). Sumed has capacity of ~2.5 mbpd, roughly half of current Yanbu flow. The Eilat-to-Ashkelon pipeline, originally built in the 1960s as an Israeli-Iranian joint venture that would become one of the strangest infrastructural artifacts of the Middle East, adds another ~1.2 mbpd of transport capacity, narrowing but not closing the ~1.3 mbpd gap. Marc Rich used the pipeline as a cash machine during the 1973–1974 oil crisis; the Iranians were expelled from the joint venture after the 1979 revolution. The pipeline's operations remain classified to this day. Riyadh does not recognize the Jewish state and "desperate times may call for desperate measures" — but neither the Saudis nor the Israelis would publicly admit to any rerouting through it [12].

The logistics are strained even when the hydrocarbons do flow. Saudi Arabia has opened talks with Asian refiners — [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html), South Korea, [India]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/india.html), and Japan buy the bulk of Saudi crude exported via the Red Sea — about shifting liftings from Yanbu to Sidi Kerir on the Mediterranean side. Suezmax-class tankers carry roughly one million barrels and can transit fully laden, but most Saudi crude is currently in VLCCs. The combination of Sumed + Eilat-Ashkelon + Suezmax + VLCC lightering can keep oil flowing, but tankers leaving the Mediterranean for Asia are routed through the wrong end of the map: the trip from Saudi Arabia to [Japan]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/japan.html) gains approximately 25 days on the rerouting, and the return trip gains the same. The longer voyage ties up a much larger share of available tanker capacity at any given moment, intensifying the charter-demand surge already underway at Hormuz and pushing freight rates higher across multiple chokepoint routes [12].

[Robert Pape]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/robert-pape.html) makes the bypass vulnerability the centerpiece of his August buffer-collapse forecast. Over 40% of Saudi oil bypassing [Hormuz]({{ site.baseurl }}/regions/middle-east.html) via the Red Sea is not reaching the global market after Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia's Red Sea terminals. The Yanbu bypass — the largest of three buffers that absorbed the Hormuz shock — is the one now failing fastest. The structural implication is that physiological shortage, not just logistical rerouting, becomes the binding constraint by end of August: "shortages, not just disruption, are about to begin in earnest." Operation Rough Rider (March 15 – May 6, 2025) sets the planning benchmark — a seven-week, 1,000+ strike campaign that tactically dominated but strategically produced a tie, with Red Sea shipping still down 60% a year later. If reopening a single maritime corridor required months and still failed to restore traffic, reopening both Hormuz and the Red Sea together will take at least as long, with no carrier reserve to absorb the simultaneity [11].

The Houthi threat reached the level of operational completion the week of July 21. The Houthis announced a naval blockade of [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html), including foreign ships calling at the kingdom's Red Sea oil ports — days after Saudi Arabia bombed the airport in Sanaa. When the Houthis executed that threat, striking two Saudi-flagged tankers in the southern Red Sea, oil prices climbed back to $100 a barrel. The blockade declaration was at least partly retaliation for the Sanaa strike, and the dependency runs through the Red Sea's geography: any ship using the Bab el-Mandeb is in Yemeni waters, and any ship using Yanbu is on Saudi Arabia's Red Sea coast — also in range. Larger consequences emerge from the simultaneity rather than from any single attack: Indian, South Korean, Chinese, and Japanese refiners are the most exposed because their Saudi crude has historically lifted at Yanbu. Tank costs for Asia-bound VLCCs have spiked, charter demand at multiple chokepoints is competing for the same constrained tonnage, and the war-risk premium regimes discussed in [Insurance and Trade Warfare]({{ site.baseurl }}/themes/insurance-and-trade-warfare.html) are now repricing three simultaneous maritime corridors rather than one [12]. See [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) and [Saudi Arabia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/saudi-arabia.html).

## Mediterranean Extension: The Damietta Strike and Beyond

On July 29, 2026, the disruption pattern that had defined the Red Sea basin broke its geographic limits. Two ships caught fire after an explosion at Damietta Port on Egypt's Mediterranean coast, north of the Suez Canal — the US-owned Energos Winter (an LNG storage tanker owned by Energos Infrastructure (Stamford, CT) via Apollo Global Management funds and leased to Egyptian Natural Gas Holding Company, EGAS) and the Greek-operated GasLog Salem. Maritime tracking firms Kpler and Ambrey corroborated the event; Ambrey's Joshua Hutchinson stated the explosion was believed to have been caused by a drone, hitting at least one of the ships. Egypt's petroleum ministry confirmed no injuries or deaths but did not officially confirm drone causation. Two anonymous Iranian sources framed the strike as an escalation signal regarding global shipping and energy disruption, without identifying the launching actor. President Trump implied Iran was responsible and pledged retaliation [13].

The strike means the Red Sea's relevance to the [US-Iran War]({{ site.baseurl }}/conflicts/us-iran-war-2026.html) is no longer confined to the basin itself. Vessel traffic through Bab al-Mandeb was already down by one-fifth since July 20 (Windward data), and traffic through the Strait of Hormuz was at less than one-tenth of prewar levels before the Damietta event. The Mediterranean strike indicates that the threat is no longer contained in those two chokepoints but extends onward through the Suez Canal into the Mediterranean approach — and the logistic infrastructure that was being lined up to handle the rerouted traffic (the Sumed pipeline's 2.5 mbpd capacity between Ain Sukhna and Sidi Kerir, the Suez Canal, the VLCC lightering workaround) is now exposed on its Mediterranean terminus too. See [Damietta Port Drone Strike]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/damietta-port-drone-strike-2026-07-29.html) and [Egypt]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/egypt.html).

## Contested: Are the Houthis Iran's proxy or an autonomous actor?

Richardson, Pape, and Tchakarova all frame the Houthi blockade as an Iranian-directed or Iranian-generated escalation. Richardson reports that "Iranian leaders asked the Houthis they back in Yemen to close the Bab el-Mandeb strait" [2]. Pape argues that Iran "gains additional leverage without directly committing its own forces" through the Houthi second front [3]. Tchakarova's diagnostic describes the Red Sea blockade as produced by "proxy activation" — a downstream output of US-Iran War kinetic dynamics [7].

Kamran Bokhari of [GPF]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/authors/geopolitical-futures.html) directly inverts this framing: the Houthis are not fighting Iran's war but pursuing their own interests with considerable strategic autonomy [9]. Despite being widely described as the most active member of Iran's regional network, the group had avoided escalation throughout the worst crisis the Iranian regime has faced since its inception, prioritizing the protection of their own gains in Yemen [9]. Iran supplies resources that bolster Houthi capabilities, Bokhari argues, but does not exercise the command and control associated with a traditional proxy relationship; the Houthis retain considerable freedom to operate as they see fit [9]. The movement's central objective is to consolidate power as Yemen's dominant political and military force, and every move it makes is in service to that goal [9]. The Houthis struck Saudi Arabia's Abha airport with missiles and drones on July 13–14, after accusing the Saudis of attacking Sanaa International Airport while a Houthi delegation returning from Tehran attempted to land; that attack followed clashes in Hodeidah between Houthi and Yemeni government forces and was followed, in turn, by a July 21 announcement of a maritime blockade on Saudi Arabia, which Riyadh condemned as a threat to oil exports [9]. These actions, Bokhari argues, are best read as instruments of political coercion aimed at reminding Saudi Arabia that the Houthis can still impose real economic and political pain, rather than demonstrations of ideological commitment to Iran's war with the United States [9]. The movement is also mindful of Operation Rough Rider, the 52-day U.S. campaign in 2025 that consisted of more than 1,000 strikes against them, and does not want to relive it — a memory that constrains their willingness to sustain escalation in the face of overwhelming force [9]. Opportunism, not ideological obligation, remains the defining feature of Houthi behavior: they will exploit opportunities created by wider geopolitical confrontation so long as the benefits exceed the costs, and can be expected to pull back once sustained escalation threatens their domestic standing or invites unacceptable retaliation, regardless of Tehran's preferences [9]. As Iran's position deteriorates, Bokhari concludes, the cohesion of its regional network will depend increasingly on the independent calculations of its members rather than direction from Tehran, a dynamic that limits Iran's ability to offset its conventional military losses through its partners [9].

The split is interpretive, not evidential — both sides observe the same Houthi actions but diverge on the question of agency. Richardson, Pape, and Tchakarova read the timing of Houthi escalation alongside the US-Iran War as evidence of Iranian direction; Bokhari reads the Houthis' prior restraint during Iran's worst crisis and their Yemen-centric objectives as evidence of autonomy. Neither side disputes the other's facts.

**Tiebreaker:** Whether the Houthis sustain or withdraw their blockade in the face of direct US or Saudi military retaliation, independent of any Iranian ceasefire or de-escalation.
