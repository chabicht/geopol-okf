# Background

The Pacific Islands region comprises the independent states and territories scattered across the South Pacific, including the 18-member Pacific Islands Forum (PIF). During the Cold War, the region was a site of superpower nuclear testing: the United States conducted approximately 100 nuclear tests in the Pacific, and France almost twice that many. The 1985 Treaty of Rarotonga established the South Pacific Nuclear Free Zone in response. This testing legacy shapes contemporary Pacific Island attitudes toward great-power military activity in the region [1].

The region is emerging as a contested strategic space where [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html), the [United States]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/united-states.html), and [Australia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/australia.html) compete for influence through aid, infrastructure, security partnerships, and diplomatic engagement. A de facto division of labor has emerged: the US handles the Compacts of Free Association with Palau, the Marshall Islands, and Micronesia (the Northern Pacific), while Australia serves as the day-to-day security partner across the South Pacific [1].

# Key Dynamics

- **China's JL-3 SLBM test triggered regional backlash.** The July 6, 2026 missile test into the South Pacific Nuclear Free Zone revived memories of Cold War superpower testing, drawing condemnations from Tuvalu, Vanuatu, Palau, and Solomon Islands leaders. [1]
- **The Pacific Islands Forum split revealed China's diplomatic clout.** The 18-member PIF failed to agree on a collective statement condemning the test — a sign of Beijing's ability to divide Pacific Island consensus. [1]
- **Australia is building a minilateral alliance architecture.** The Ocean of Peace Alliance with Fiji (July 2026), the Pukpuk Mutual Defense Treaty with PNG (October 2025), and the Nakamal Agreement with Vanuatu (June 2026) form a layered security framework. [1]
- **Australia dominates Pacific development finance.** Australia disbursed $1.5 billion in official development finance in 2024 — 37% of the regional total — and has displaced China as the top bilateral lender with $2.4 billion in new Pacific loan agreements since 2021. [1]
- **China's lending has collapsed but SOE persistence remains.** Chinese annual lending declined to approximately $91 million since 2020 from its 2010s peak, and Beijing has shifted to smaller grants — yet roughly 40% of Pacific infrastructure projects since 2008 have involved Chinese state-owned enterprises. [1]
- **A US-Australia division of labor structures the architecture.** The US manages the Compacts of Free Association in the Northern Pacific while Australia serves as the day-to-day security partner across the South Pacific. [1]

# Analysis

## The JL-3 Test and the Pacific Backlash

On July 6, 2026, [China]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/china.html) launched a ballistic missile — likely a JL-3 submarine-launched ballistic missile with approximately 10,000km range — from a nuclear submarine in the South China Sea. The missile traveled approximately 7,300km, landing in the South Pacific Nuclear Free Zone established by the 1985 Treaty of Rarotonga. It was China's first publicly acknowledged SLBM test into open international waters, demonstrating a survivable second-strike nuclear deterrent capability. See [China JL-3 SLBM Test]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/jl-3-slbm-test-2026-07-06.html) [1].

The test triggered immediate backlash from Pacific Island states. Leaders from Tuvalu, Vanuatu, and Palau stated that the Pacific should not be a testing ground; the Solomon Islands PM said it was "not the behavior expected from a friend." Yet the 18-member Pacific Islands Forum failed to agree on a collective statement — a sign that China's diplomatic clout has divided Pacific Island consensus. The same day as the test, Australia and Fiji signed the Ocean of Peace Alliance, suggesting that Chinese military signaling may be catalyzing the very containment architecture Beijing seeks to avoid [1].

## Australia's Emerging Alliance Architecture

[Australia]({{ site.baseurl }}/actors/countries/australia.html) is constructing a layered minilateral security framework across the South Pacific:

- **Ocean of Peace Alliance (July 6, 2026)**: A mutual defense treaty between Australia and Fiji — Fiji's first formal alliance and Australia's fourth. Signed the same day as China's JL-3 test, the alliance is open to other Pacific countries. See [Ocean of Peace Alliance]({{ site.baseurl }}/events/ocean-of-peace-alliance-2026-07-06.html).
- **Pukpuk Mutual Defense Treaty (October 2025)**: Australia and Papua New Guinea — Australia's first new alliance in over 70 years.
- **Nakamal Agreement (June 29, 2026)**: Australia and Vanuatu — prohibits foreign military bases and requires Australian consultation on third-party critical infrastructure projects [1].

This architecture represents a structural shift: Australia is no longer merely a US deputy in the South Pacific but an independent security provider building its own alliance network. The Nakamal Agreement's prohibition on foreign military bases directly targets Chinese ambitions, while the requirement for Australian consultation on critical infrastructure projects creates a veto-like mechanism over Chinese SOE engagement.

## The Aid and Lending Competition

Australia has displaced China as the dominant external financial actor in the Pacific. Australia disbursed $1.5 billion in official development finance in 2024 — 37% of the regional total — and has committed $2.4 billion in new Pacific loan agreements since 2021. Chinese annual lending declined to approximately $91 million since 2020, a sharp fall from its 2010s peak, and Beijing has shifted to smaller grants. Yet China retains structural presence: roughly 40% of Pacific infrastructure projects since 2008 have involved Chinese state-owned enterprises. Chinese warships have circumnavigated Australia and conducted live-fire exercises in the Tasman Strait, signaling that reduced lending does not mean reduced strategic ambition [1].
